Whatever state of being one remembers when he quits his body, O son of Kuntī, that state he will attain without fail.
Purport

The process of changing one’s nature at the critical moment of death is here explained. A person who at the end of his life quits his body thinking of Kṛṣṇa attains the transcendental nature of the Supreme Lord, but it is not true that a person who thinks of something other than Kṛṣṇa attains the same transcendental state. This is a point we should note very carefully. How can one die in the proper state of mind? Mahārāja Bharata, although a great personality, thought of a deer at the end of his life, and so in his next life he was transferred into the body of a deer. Although as a deer he remembered his past activities, he had to accept that animal body. Of course, one’s thoughts during the course of one’s life accumulate to influence one’s thoughts at the moment of death, so this life creates one’s next life. If in one’s present life one lives in the mode of goodness and always thinks of Kṛṣṇa, it is possible for one to remember Kṛṣṇa at the end of one’s life. That will help one be transferred to the transcendental nature of Kṛṣṇa. If one is transcendentally absorbed in Kṛṣṇa’s service, then his next body will be transcendental (spiritual), not material. Therefore the chanting of Hare Kṛṣṇa, Hare Kṛṣṇa, Kṛṣṇa Kṛṣṇa, Hare Hare/ Hare Rāma, Hare Rāma, Rāma Rāma, Hare Hare is the best process for successfully changing one’s state of being at the end of one’s life.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
6. Whatever one thinks of when leaving the body, one attains a state similar to that object of constant thought, O son of Kuntī.
Commentary

This verse explains that just as, by remembering Me, one attains Me (stated in the previous verse), so by remembering something else, one attains that object. Being influenced (bhāvitaḥ) by constant thoughts (bhāva) of that object, a person becomes similar to the object (bhāvitaḥ).

Baladeva Vidyābhūṣaṇa Gauḍīya
Translation
6. Whatever one thinks of when leaving the body, one attains a state similar to that object of constant thought, O son of Kuntī.
Commentary

It is not a rule that all remember Me and attain Me. Others remember something else and attain that. Whatever object (bhāvam) a person remembers, he attains that after giving up his present body and state of mind. Bhārata, remembering a deer at the point of death, became a deer. The last thoughts at death are the subjects of previous thoughts. His mind being controlled by that previous remembrance (tad bhāva bhāvitaḥ), he attains a body according to the object he thinks of at death.

At the time of death the whole storehouse of impressions and desires is churned out and the most prominent? the strongest and cherished desire comes to the surface of the mind or the field of mental consciousness. This churned up butter or cream (cherished desire) arrests his attention for immediate gratification. He thinks of only that at the time of death. Just as the most vital mango plant shoots up prominently in the nursery? so also the strongest desire shoots up on the surface of the mind. If the desire is not gratified his mind gets saturated with it and it is gratified in his nextbirth. This desire will become very promenent in his next birth.You yourself are the author of your own destiny. You yourself are responsible for your thoughts? character? feelings? actions and experiences. You planted certain worldly desires and Samskaras in your subconscious mind? and allowed them to germinate and grow. If you had planted spiritual aspiration? the desire for liberation and spiritual Samskaras? you would reap the fruit of immortality and eternal bliss. As you sow so shall you reap.He who practises constant and profound meditation on the Self of his own tutelary deity throughout his life will be able to meet death with an unruffled mind. He alone would go to the Supreme? thinking of It at the time of his departure from this world? too. You should have exclusive devotion to God. Your whole mind must be absorbed in Him. You should not allow any outside worldly impressions? wherein there is an iota of selfish desire? to sink into your subconscious mind. Then you can think of the Lord exclusively at the time of death and enter His very Being.

Personal Guidance
Whatever state of being one remembers when leaving the body — that very state one attains.
Practical Applications
  • Recognize the seriousness of the final moment's content.
  • Cultivate the content you want to die with.
  • Don't fill the mind with content you wouldn't want to attain.
Reflection Questions
  • What content fills my mind most often?
  • Is it content I'd want to die with?
Analogy

The mind at death is like an iron filing released near whichever magnet it has spent its life lying beside. After years of proximity to one pole, it has no choice in which way it flies. Kṛṣṇa does not say the dying soul is free at the last moment; He says it is bound by which magnet it has been keeping company with.

Stories
Bharata's deer (continued)

Bharata Mahārāja, having spent his renounced years tending an orphaned fawn, found at the moment of death that his mind would not move from the deer. He had been a king, then a sage, then a deer-keeper — and the bhāva-bhāvitaḥ of his final season had been deer.

The Bhāgavatam records what 8.6 predicts: tam tam eva eti — that very state he attained. He took birth as a deer in the next life. The verse is not poetry; it is law. Bharata is its case study.

Important Words
  • yam yam — whatever
    Reduplicated indefinite — 'whichever, whichever'. The doubling intensifies the universality: no matter what state, no matter how chosen, this rule holds. Kṛṣṇa is not making exceptions for sincere mistakes.
  • smaran — remembering
    Same root as in 8.5 — smṛ. But here the remembering is not necessarily of Kṛṣṇa; it is of whatever bhāva one has cultivated. The neutral word carries either liberation or bondage depending on its object.
  • bhāvam — nature
    From bhū — to be. State of being, mood, disposition. Not a passing thought but the stable mode the consciousness has been resting in. The Gītā distinguishes flickering thoughts from the deeper bhāva that surfaces at death.
  • kalevaram — this body
    Slightly archaic word for body, often used in dying contexts. Different from deha or śarīra — kalevara carries the connotation of something to be left behind, almost a husk. The lexical choice fits the verse's setting.
  • kaunteya — O son of Kuntī
    Kṛṣṇa addresses Arjuna by his mother's name precisely at a verse about what gets transmitted across the moment of death. Maternal lineage is foregrounded as Kṛṣṇa speaks of the bhāva that carries the soul into its next form.
  • bhāvitaḥ — remembering, having been cultivated
    Past participle of the causative of bhū — 'caused to be, contemplated, cultivated'. The same root as bhāvanā, the technical Sanskrit word for sustained contemplative practice. The verse names the state one has been deliberately habituating oneself toward — disciplined inclination, not random thought.
