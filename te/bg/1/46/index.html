Important Words
  • saṅkhye — in the battlefield
    *Saṅkhya* — battle (here); but the same word, with another stress, names the philosophy of *sāṅkhya* (enumeration, analysis). Sanskrit lets the same letters mean *battle* and *analysis*. The chapter ends on a word that contains the next chapter's name: Sāṅkhya-yoga (BG 2). Vyāsa is preparing the transition phonetically.
  • upasthe — on the seat
    Locative of *upastha* — lap, seat. Arjuna sits down on the *seat* of the chariot — not on the ground, not in retreat. He stays *on* the chariot. The collapse is contained within the divine framework. He has not fled the war; he has only sat down on the platform from which the Gītā will be heard.
  • upāviśat — sat down again
    Aorist of *upa-viś* — to sit down. Same root *viś-* as in *praviśanti* and *viśvatomukha*. "He took his seat." The same verb the Vedic tradition uses for a disciple *taking his seat* before a teacher (*upaviṣat*). The military *upāviśat* is, by its very verb, also the disciplic *upaveśana* — sitting down to learn.
  • visṛjya — putting aside
    Absolutive of *vi-sṛj* — to release, to let go, to discharge. Same root as *sṛṣṭi* (creation, lit. "emission"). Arjuna *releases* his bow and arrows. The Gītā will use *visṛjya* repeatedly for the act of *renouncing*. The first *visṛjya* of the Gītā is a confused one. The last *visṛjya* (BG 18.66: *sarva-dharmān parityajya*) will be the perfected one.
  • śoka — by lamentation
    *Śoka* — grief, sorrow. The exact word Kṛṣṇa will name in His first teaching reply (BG 2.11: *aśocyān anvaśocas tvam* — "you grieve for those not worthy of grief"). The chapter ends on the word the next chapter will diagnose. *Śoka* is the disease; the Gītā is its medicine.
Sañjaya said: Arjuna, having thus spoken on the battlefield, cast aside his bow and arrows and sat down on the chariot, his mind overwhelmed with grief.
Purport

While observing the situation of his enemy, Arjuna stood up on the chariot, but he was so afflicted with lamentation that he sat down again, setting aside his bow and arrows. Such a kind and soft-hearted person, in the devotional service of the Lord, is fit to receive self-knowledge. Thus end the Bhaktivedanta Purports to the First Chapter of the Śrīmad Bhagavad-gītā in the matter of Observing the Armies on the Battlefield of Kurukṣetra.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
46. Speaking in this manner, Arjuna, giving up his bow and arrows, mind disturbed with lamentation, sat down on his chariot amidst the warriors assembled for battle.
Commentary

Sitting on his chariot (rathopaṣṭhe) amidst the assembly of warriors (saṅkhye), Arjuna gave up his weapons.

Thus the commentaries on the first chapter of the Gītā, Sārārtha Varṣiṇī, has been completed for giving joy to the hearts of devotees, following after the tradition of our ācāryas.

Baladeva Vidyābhūṣaṇa Gauḍīya
Translation
46. Speaking in this manner, Arjuna, giving up his bow and arrows, his mind disturbed with lamentation, sat down on his chariot amidst the war.
Commentary

“Then what happened?” Saṣjaya then replied with this verse. Arjuna sat down on the chariot in the midst of the battle. Previously he had been standing in order to fight, and to see the opposing army. From the first chapter it is understood that inquiry about ātmā takes place in a person who is compassionate in nature and non-violent, and not in one who is cruel and violent. Chapter 2

Sanjaya said: Arjuna, having thus spoken on the battlefield, cast aside his bow and arrows and sat down on the chariot, his mind overwhelmed with grief. (cn/DS) Arjuna has put down his bow. Study Notes — Gauranga Priya Dāsa
Gauranga Priya Dāsa Study
Bhakti-śāstrī Notes, ISKCON Chowpatty (2009)
Theme

Arjuna had vowed to kill anyone who even suggested that he put down his bow. An incident later

tested that vow.

During the battle, Karëa defeated and severely injured Yudhiñöhira. Karëa then taunted Yudhiñöhira,

saying, “You are not even a kñatriya! You should learn how to fight. See how easily I have defeated

you! I’m not even going to bother to kill you.”

When Arjuna heard of Yudhiñöhira’s injury, he immediately left the battlefield and went to Yudhi-

ñöhira’s tent. Upon hearing that Arjuna had left the battlefield, Yudhiñöhira concluded that Arjuna,

avenging his honor, had already killed Karëa and had now come to inform him.

But it wasn’t true. Arjuna had left the battlefield only out of concern for Yudhiñöhira’s injury.

As soon as Yudhiñöhira saw Arjuna, he asked, “Have you killed Karëa and avenged me?”

Arjuna replied, “No, I did not kill Karëa. I only came to see how badly you were hurt.”

Yudhiñöhira was furious. He said, “You have left the battlefield without even killing Karëa? You

coward! How could you have done this? It is better that you give up your Gäëòéva bow!”

Upon hearing his words, Arjuna became enraged and thought, “How dare Yudhiñöhira suggest that I

give up my Gäëòéva bow, which is more dear to me than my life! I have vowed to kill anyone . . .”

Arjuna drew his sword to slay his brother. Kåñëa, who was watching, immediately caught Arjuna’s

arm and said, “All right, you have to kill Yudhiñöhira. It is your vow, but kill him by insulting him. For

a kñatriya, an insult is worse than death.”

As Yudhiñöhira sat there regretting his words to Arjuna,

Arjuna angrily approached him and said, “You say I am a coward because I left the battlefield with-

out killing Karëa, but look what you have done. You looked on as our wife Draupadé was insulted. I

would have alone killed the entire Kaurava army, but you would not allow me. You are not a man.

You are not a king!”

Arjuna finished hurling his insults at Yudhiñöhira, but then quickly touched Yudhiñöhira’s feet and

begged forgiveness for his words.

Arjuna had not lightly made his “Gäëòéva vow.” Therefore it is significant that Arjuna—the great

son of Päëòu and Indra—himself cast aside his bow and sat down on the chariot, his mind over-

whelmed with grief. We can only imagine how disturbed Arjuna must have been about the disaster

awaiting him and the entire world

Personal Guidance
The bow falls from the hand precisely so that Kṛṣṇa can be approached as guru — Arjuna's collapse is the necessary doorway into the rest of the Gītā.
Practical Applications
  • Treat your moments of total collapse as Kṛṣṇa's invitation to formally take shelter.
  • When you cannot continue under your own steam, do not bandage yourself — surrender.
  • Read BG 2.7 immediately after this verse to feel the natural progression from collapse into surrender.
Reflection Questions
  • Where has the bow fallen from my hand in this season of life?
  • Am I willing to use this collapse as a doorway to formally surrender to Kṛṣṇa?
Analogy

The bow falls. The arrows are laid aside. The warrior sits down on the seat of his own chariot — which is now, with Kṛṣṇa at its reins, no longer a war-chariot but a teaching-platform. The collapse is total, and exact: the *visṛjya* (laying aside) prepares the *prapadye* (taking shelter) that will come in 2.7.

Stories
Hanumān and the falling bow

Hanumān, riding on the flag above the chariot, had agreed to remain there until the war was complete. When Arjuna's bow falls in this verse, the flag is still flying. The servant of Rāma is watching the descendant of Pāṇḍu collapse. Hanumān himself, in another age, had refused to despair even before Laṅkā with its hundred-thousand soldiers and golden walls. He had said: *I am the servant of Rāma; what is impossible for the Lord's servant?*

Arjuna's collapse is the moment Hanumān's quiet presence on the flag becomes most pointed. The lineage of perfect service is silently overhead while the warrior of imperfect service sinks. The Gītā will now begin the work of bringing Arjuna into the same posture Hanumān has always held — and the bow that has just fallen will, by chapter 18, be picked up again in that very spirit.
