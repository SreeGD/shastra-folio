Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 11.3.2

Although I am drinking the nectar of your statements about the glories of the Supreme Personality of Godhead, my thirst is not yet satiated. Such nectarean descriptions of the Lord and His devotees are the actual medicine for conditioned souls like me, who are tormented by the threefold miseries of material existence.
Purport

According to Śrīla Śrīdhara Svāmī, one may argue that since the symptoms of a pure devotee of the Lord have already been elaborately described, one can perfect his life by advancing to the platform mentioned in the previous verse, and there is no need for further questions. But hari-kathāmṛtam, topics about the Lord and His devotees, are so pleasing and beautiful that one cannot give up hearing them, even after spiritual liberation. Caitanya Mahāprabhu, in this connection, has quoted the following verse:

ātmārāmāś ca munayo nirgranthā apy urukrame kurvanty ahaitukīṁ bhaktim ittham-bhūta-guṇo hariḥ

“Those who are self-satisfied and unattracted by external, material desires are also attracted to the loving service of Śrī Kṛṣṇa, whose qualities are transcendental and whose activities are wonderful. Hari, the Personality of Godhead, is called Kṛṣṇa because He has such transcendentally attractive features.” ( Bhāg. 1.7.10 ) Material medicine is not desirable once a disease has been cured, but on the absolute platform the means and the end are not different. So chanting and hearing the glories of Lord Kṛṣṇa are both the means and the end of transcendental bliss.

King Nimi said to the sages, “You are all great saintly persons absorbed in love of Godhead. Therefore although you will speak about māyā, or illusion, the conclusion will undoubtedly be Kṛṣṇa consciousness. Please don’t think that you have already explained everything to me. The intoxicating nectar of your instructions has left me more eager than ever to hear about the Supreme Personality of Godhead.”

King Nimi was also a great devotee of the Lord, otherwise there would have been no question of his personally conversing with such exalted living entities as the nine Yogendras. But as a humble Vaiṣṇava he considered himself an ordinary conditioned soul covered by material designations. Thus he showed his eagerness to understand the actual nature of māyā in order to be safe from her future attempts to place him in the burning fire of material existence.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Although I am suffering in material existence and am drinking the nectar of your statements about the Lord’s glories which is the remedy for those suffering material existence, my thirst is not yet satiated. The cause of asking this question is the irrepressible greed to drink the nectar of your words, since I am eager for your association.

Personal Guidance
Hari-kathā is both medicine and nectar — the only remedy for saṁsāra's threefold fire, and a thirst that never tires of being quenched.
Practical Applications
  • Before opening the day's distractions, drink ten minutes of hari-kathā as actual medicine for the fever of conditioned life.
  • Notice when you say 'I've already heard this' about a Bhāgavata verse, and re-read it as though you were Nimi pleading the sages for more.
  • When the threefold miseries scorch you today — body, mind, or circumstance — reach first for kīrtana or a Bhāgavata page, not for distraction.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my hearing of hari-kathā do I treat it as information already digested, instead of nectar I have barely tasted?
  • Which of the threefold tāpas is burning me most right now, and how have I been medicating it with something other than topics of the Lord?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • When the exam-and-comparison fire flares up, take fifteen minutes of Bhāgavata reading as actual treatment, not as a 'spiritual hobby' alongside the real fix.
  • Audit one week of your feed: count the hours of scrolling versus minutes of hari-kathā, and ask honestly which one is leaving you more burnt.
  • In your friend group, refuse the pose of 'I already know all this Kṛṣṇa stuff' — speak about a verse you re-heard this week as if it were new.
householder
  • Build hari-kathā into the family's medicine cabinet: a fixed time when the Bhāgavata is read aloud, especially on days the threefold miseries have hit the household hardest.
  • When a work or money crisis sends you reaching for news, podcasts, or venting, redirect the first ten minutes to a verse and purport — then return to the problem.
  • Speak to your spouse and children about hari-kathā as bheṣaja, not as duty — model Nimi's eagerness so the next generation sees hearing as nectar, not obligation.
senior
  • As the body's tāpas intensify with age, deepen — don't reduce — your daily intake of hari-kathā; this is the medicine that does not become unnecessary after the cure.
  • Mentor a younger devotee by reading one Bhāgavata verse with them weekly, letting your own thirst, not your seniority, be the teaching.
  • Refuse the temptation to feel 'I have heard enough in this lifetime' — sit again with familiar verses as Nimi did, asking the Lord to make them taste like first hearing.
Common Pitfalls
  • Treating hari-kathā as a finite curriculum to be completed, rather than the eternal occupation of even the liberated.
  • Hearing for novelty or scholarship while the heart's thirst — the actual symptom of saṁsāra — goes unaddressed.
  • Approaching kṛṣṇa-kathā as escapism from suffering rather than as the bheṣaja that uproots its cause.
Discussion Prompts
  • Nimi calls himself martya and tāpa-nistapta even while conversing with the Yogendras — what does his humility teach us about the posture of a real hearer, and where does our own self-image as 'advanced' get in the way?
  • Prabhupāda says material medicine is discarded once disease is cured, but hari-kathā continues even after liberation — discuss with your partner what this reveals about the difference between material and spiritual ends.
  • Share a time when hari-kathā actually functioned as medicine for a specific misery in your life — and a time when you tried to use it that way and felt nothing. What was different?
Role Play

One student plays King Nimi after the Yogendras have already spoken at length about the pure devotee; the other plays a fellow seeker who says, 'They've answered your question — why are you still asking?' Nimi must respond from this verse's mood, explaining why the nectar has only increased his thirst rather than ended it.

Objections & Responses
  • Isn't this just religious addiction? Replacing one craving with another, sanctified one?
    Material cravings increase the disease they promise to cure — eating salt to quench thirst. Hari-kathā is the one 'thirst' whose object is the soul's actual food; on the absolute platform the means and the end are not different, so the craving and its fulfillment are the same substance. That is not addiction; that is the soul finally tasting what it was made for.
  • If hearing the same verses over and over is the remedy, why do most devotees still seem anxious, irritable, and burnt by saṁsāra?
    Nimi calls the words amṛtam because he is drinking them, not merely sitting near them. The medicine is potent, but it must be juṣan — actually consumed, with the humility of a martya who knows he is being burnt. When hearing becomes ritual performance without that thirst, the bottle is on the shelf and the fever remains.
