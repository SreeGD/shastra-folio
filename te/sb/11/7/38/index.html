Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 11.7.38

శశ్వత్పరార్థసర్వేహ: పరార్థైకాన్తసమ్భవ: । సాధు: శిక్షేత భూభృత్తో నగశిష్య: పరాత్మతామ్ ॥ ౩౮ ॥

A saintly person should learn from the mountain to devote all his efforts to the service of others and to make the welfare of others the sole reason for his existence. Similarly, as the disciple of the tree, he should learn to dedicate himself to others.
Purport

Great mountains bear unlimited quantities of earth, which in turn give sustenance to innumerable forms of life such as trees, grass, birds, animals, and so on. Mountains also pour forth unlimited quantities of crystalline water in the form of waterfalls and rivers, and this water gives life to all. By studying the example of mountains, one should learn the art of providing for the happiness of all living entities. Similarly, one may take excellent lessons from the pious trees, who offer innumerable benefits, such as fruits, flowers, cooling shade and medicinal extracts. Even when a tree is suddenly cut down and dragged away, the tree does not protest but continues to give service to others in the form of firewood. Thus, one should become the disciple of such magnanimous trees and learn from them the qualities of saintly conduct.

According to Śrīla Madhvācārya, the word parārthaikānta-sambhavaḥ indicates that one should dedicate all of one’s wealth and other assets to the welfare of others. By one’s acquired opulence, one should especially try to please the spiritual master and the Supreme Personality of Godhead. Thus, the demigods, as well as all truly respectable superior personalities, will automatically be pleased. By developing saintly conduct, as described in this verse, one will become tolerant, and this will free one from the useless agitation of the material senses, which drive one to wander throughout the world searching in vain for material happiness. Lord Śrī Caitanya Mahāprabhu has also emphasized the tree’s quality of tolerance: taror iva sahiṣṇunā, kīrtanīyaḥ sadā hariḥ. One who is as tolerant as a tree can chant the holy name of Kṛṣṇa constantly, finding ever-new satisfaction.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
A saintly person should learn from the mountain to devote all his efforts to the service of others and to make the welfare of others the sole reason for his existence. Similarly, as the disciple of the tree, he should learn to dedicate himself to others. He speaks of the mountain and the tree, which also represent the earth. The mountain always devotes its efforts for others by holding up the earth, producing waterfalls and giving jewels. The devotee should learn this conduct from the mountain. As the disciple of the tree, he should learn to dedicate himself completely to others. One can take the tree from one place to another, plant it and water it. The tree accepts this, and does not protest. The yogī should be like that. This is the particular nature of the tree not seen in the mountain.
Personal Guidance
Learn from the mountain and the tree: exist for others, give without protest, and let tolerance become the soil in which constant remembrance of Kṛṣṇa grows.
Practical Applications
  • Pick one resource you guard — time, money, skill — and spend a measurable portion of it today serving someone who cannot repay you.
  • When someone cuts into you with criticism or demand, practice taror iva sahiṣṇunā: do not protest, and continue offering what service you can.
  • Before sleep, dedicate today's earnings and efforts explicitly to the pleasure of the spiritual master and Kṛṣṇa, refusing the thought that they were 'yours' to begin with.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my life am I still demanding that my service be recognized, and how would a mountain or tree act in that same situation?
  • What 'cutting down' am I currently resisting — and what would it mean to keep serving from that very wound?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • In your friend group or hostel, identify the person everyone overlooks and quietly do one concrete service for them today without telling anyone.
  • When a classmate takes credit for your work or a teacher is unfair, practice not protesting outwardly — write the reaction in your journal instead and offer the sting to Kṛṣṇa.
  • Refuse the social-media logic that says service only counts if it is seen; do one good act this week that you tell no one about, not even online.
householder
  • Review this month's spending and reassign a clear percentage to the service of devotees, the spiritual master's mission, or a person in genuine need — make parārtha a line item, not a leftover.
  • When your spouse, child, or aged parent 'cuts into' your evening with an inconvenient demand, treat it as the tree being chopped and respond with service rather than negotiation or sigh.
  • In a work conflict this week, choose the response that protects the other person's dignity even at cost to your own position, and dedicate that cost to Kṛṣṇa before bed.
senior
  • Identify the knowledge, contacts, or possessions you have been quietly hoarding 'for later' and begin transferring them now to younger devotees and family members who can use them in Kṛṣṇa's service.
  • When the body's decline 'cuts you down' — pain, dependency, being passed over — practice taror iva sahiṣṇunā and use that very tolerance as the ground for steadier chanting of the holy name.
  • Become a mountain for your grandchildren and juniors: let them draw freely from your time, stories, and patience, expecting nothing in return, and aim every such gift at their eventual remembrance of Kṛṣṇa.
Common Pitfalls
  • Mistaking doormat passivity for tree-like tolerance, so that one absorbs abuse without any inner offering to Kṛṣṇa.
  • Performing visible philanthropy while keeping the best of one's wealth and energy for one's own sense gratification.
  • Reducing the verse to material humanitarianism — feeding bodies while ignoring the soul's need for Kṛṣṇa consciousness, which is the real parārtha.
Discussion Prompts
  • Share a recent moment when you were 'cut down' — did you protest, withdraw, or keep serving? What does the tree's example expose in your response?
  • Is there a difference between selflessness taught by modern ethics and the parārthaikānta-sambhavaḥ of this verse? Where do they part ways?
  • Whose welfare do you currently treat as the 'sole reason' for your existence? Be honest — name the person or thing, and weigh it against what this verse asks.
Role Play

A householder student has worked overtime for weeks on a project; a colleague takes public credit and the manager scolds the student for a minor error. That evening the student's spouse asks for help with the children and an elderly neighbor knocks asking for a ride to the hospital. Two students enact the scene — one playing the householder, one playing spouse/colleague/neighbor in turn — and the group then discusses what mountain-like and tree-like responses would actually look like, moment by moment, without collapsing into either resentment or fake saintliness.

Objections & Responses
  • If I live only for others, I will be exploited and burn out — this is unhealthy, not spiritual.
    The verse does not ask you to serve your own false ego's image of being useful; it asks you to make parārtha — the welfare of others, ultimately their relationship with Kṛṣṇa — the axis of your life. A mountain is not depleted by giving water, because it is fed by clouds from above; a sādhu is sustained by Kṛṣṇa, not by the gratitude of those served. Burnout comes from serving for one's own subtle return, not from genuine parārthaikānta-sambhavaḥ.
  • Trees and mountains are insentient — comparing a conscious human being to them romanticizes inertia and passivity.
    Prabhupāda's purport is precise: we are to learn specific qualities — tolerance, magnanimity, sustenance of many — not to imitate insentience. Lord Caitanya himself cited taror iva sahiṣṇunā as the qualification for constant kīrtana, which is the most active, conscious engagement possible. Tree-like tolerance is the inner posture that frees the senses from agitation, so that real service and chanting become possible.
