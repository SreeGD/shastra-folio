Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.10.8

Important Words
  • buddhi-bhraṁśaḥ — fall of intelligence
    Compound: buddhi (discriminative intelligence) + bhraṁśa (falling away). Gītā 2.63 names this as the final stage before destruction. Nārada locates wealth without education at exactly this collapse-point.
  • rajaḥ-guṇaḥ — the mode of passion
    Compound: rajas + guṇa. In Gauḍīya tattva, rajo-guṇa generates work, ambition, and the hankering that drives one toward stree-dyūta-āsava. Wealth is rajas' fuel — without sattva to direct it, it always burns toward viṣaya.
  • śrī-madāt — from intoxication of wealth
    Ablative of śrī-mada. Among the six madas Vaiṣṇava ācāryas catalogue (wealth, beauty, family, knowledge, position, strength), Nārada names wealth as the most bewildering. The hierarchy of intoxications has its own grammar.
  • strī — women
    Nominative. In context, the word names not women per se but illicit liaison — para-strī. Gauḍīya gṛhastha-dharma distinguishes sva-strī (one's wife, helper in bhakti) from para-strī (the road to ruin). Wealth without education collapses the distinction.
  • dyūtam — gambling
    Neuter noun. Listed by Manu and Bhāgavata as one of the four pillars of adharma. Dyūta corrodes truthfulness — exactly the dharma-leg most needed to handle wealth — and so the rajasika rich are pulled toward it first.
Nārada Muni said: Among all the attractions of material enjoyment, the attraction of riches bewilders one’s intelligence more than having beautiful bodily features, taking birth in an aristocratic family, and being learned. When one is uneducated but falsely puffed up by wealth, the result is that one engages his wealth in enjoying wine, women and gambling.
Purport

Among the three modes of material nature — goodness, passion and ignorance — people are certainly conducted by the lower qualities, namely passion and ignorance, and especially by passion. Conducted by the mode of passion, one becomes more and more involved in material existence. Therefore human life is meant for subduing the modes of passion and ignorance and advancing in the mode of goodness. tadā rajas-tamo-bhāvāḥ kāma-lobhādayaś ca ye ceta etair anāviddhaṁ sthitaṁ sattve prasīdati ( Bhāg. 1.2.19 ) This is culture: one must subdue the modes of passion and ignorance. In the mode of passion, when one is falsely proud of wealth, one engages his wealth only for three things, namely wine, women and gambling. We can actually see, especially in this age, that those who have unnecessary riches simply try to enjoy these three things. In Western civilization, these three things are very prominent because of an unnecessary increase of wealth. Nārada Muni considered all this in the case of Maṇigrīva and Nalakūvara because he found in them so much pride in the wealth of their father, Kuvera.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Nārada Muni said: Among all the attractions of material enjoyment, the attraction of riches bewilders one's intelligence more than having beautiful bodily features, taking birth in an aristocratic family, and being learned. When one is uneducated but falsely puffed up by wealth, the result is that one engages his wealth in enjoying wine, women and gambling.
Commentary

For the man absorbed in serving (jusatah) objects of enjoyment (jyosyan), pride in a high family and knowledge do not destroy intelligence as much as intoxication with wealth. In other words intoxication with wealth certainly destroys intelligence completely. Abhijati means being born in a high family. From that one develops pride. Adi indicates knowledge. That also gives rise to pride. As pride in wealth arises from the mode of passion raja guna is mentioned in this verse. With pride in wealth arise many more sins than arise through pride in family or knowledge. The various sins are mentioned here ( women, gambling and intoxication), and in the following four verse.

Śrīdhara Svāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

On seeing they were blinded by pride in wealth, desiring to give a curse to destroy their wealth, Nārada criticizes wealth in five verses. One becomes proud from being born in a good family (ābhijātyādiḥ). The word ādi indicates knowledge and other items. vidyā-mado dhana-madas tathā cābhijano madaḥ madā etair aliptānām ta eve hi satāṁ damāḥ On can develop pride in knowledge, wealth or birth. The devotees who are uncontaminated by these have control of their mind. Mahābhārata If one has pride in wealth, because the other items follow, one becomes most bewildered, since these things become a great cause of lust and anger. These two are criticized in the Gītā: kāma eṣa krodha eṣa rajo-guṇa-samudbhavaḥ | mahāśano mahā-pāpmā viddhy enam iha vairiṇam || The Lord said: It is lust and then anger, arising from the mode of rajas. Know this as the great devourer, the great sinful one, the enemy. BG 3.37 Where there is pride in wealth, there will be anger. Or when there is pride in wealth with attachment to various objects, anger etc. arise. Women are mentioned first since they are the root of all faults. Or starting with women, the successively greater causes of anarthas is listed. Gambling is superior to women as a cause because of the suffering caused by quarreling and the loss of money. Intoxication is superior to that because one loses all interest in protecting the body.

Personal Guidance
Wealth without education breeds rajo-guṇa pride that funnels life into wine, women, and gambling — guard your intelligence before riches bewilder it.
Practical Applications
  • Audit today where your money quietly flows toward viṣaya — intoxication, sense-pleasure, or speculative thrills — and redirect one such expense toward Kṛṣṇa's service.
  • Refuse to take pride in any wealth, lineage, beauty, or learning you possess; before each meal, remind yourself these are Kuvera's loans, not your achievements.
  • Notice when an unearned advantage (family money, inherited status, good looks) is dulling your discrimination, and respond by performing one act of humble, sober sādhana — chanting, study, or service — to re-anchor in sattva.
Analogy

A man whose pockets are full but whose head is empty walks into the bazaar at night — every shopkeeper of wine, women, and dice claims him. Riches without education are the loose purse that buys the soul's ruin. Wealth in the hands of a fool is not opulence; it is rope.

Stories
Duryodhana's wealth

Duryodhana possessed the entire wealth of Hastināpura, with vast revenues, a hundred brothers, allies, weapons, and the loyalty of armies. What he lacked was education — not in śāstra, but in the use of wealth in dharma's service.

The result Nārada predicts here is exactly what played out at Yudhiṣṭhira's rājasūya: insulted by his own envy, Duryodhana ran toward dyūta (gambling), draupadī-vastrāharaṇa (insulting women), and surā-feasts. SB 10.10.8 names the disease before the Mahābhārata illustrates it on a kingdom-wide scale.
