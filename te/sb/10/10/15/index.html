Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.10.15

Important Words
  • nir-aham-stambhaḥ — freed from the pillar of false ego
    Compound: nir- (without) + aham (I) + stambha (pillar, post). Ahaṁkāra is pictured as a stiff pillar that wealth feeds; poverty saws through it. Gauḍīya frame: nirahaṁkāra is the soil in which prapatti finally takes root.
  • muktaḥ — liberated
    Past participle from √muc, 'to release.' Used here in a preliminary, ego-level sense — not mokṣa proper, but freedom from one specific bondage (false prestige). Gauḍīya distinction: this is the entry-level mukti from which bhakti-mukti begins.
  • yadṛcchayā — by chance, from providence
    Same word as in SB 10.10.5 — there for the descent of the sādhu, here for the supply of food. Gauḍīya frame: the same divine spontaneity that sends Nārada also sends the pauper's daily bowl. Providence is Kṛṣṇa's calligraphy.
  • tapaḥ — austerity
    From √tap, 'to heat, burn.' Austerity is a controlled burn of the lower nature. The verse equates forced privation with voluntary tapas only in effect, not in mood — a tapas one chooses for Kṛṣṇa retains its bhakti-flavor; one merely endured at least burns ahaṅkāra.
A poverty-stricken man must automatically undergo austerities and penances because he does not have the wealth to possess anything. Thus his false prestige is vanquished. Always in need of food, shelter and clothing, he must be satisfied with what is obtained by the mercy of providence. Undergoing such compulsory austerities is good for him because this purifies him and completely frees him from false ego.
Purport

A saintly person voluntarily accepts a state of poverty just to become free from material false prestige. Many great kings left their princely standard of living and went to the forest to practice austerity according to Vedic culture, just to become purified. But if one who cannot voluntarily accept such austerity is put into a situation of poverty, he automatically must practice austerity. Austerity is good for everyone because it frees one from material conditions. Therefore, if one is very much proud of his material position, putting him into poverty is the best way to rectify his foolishness. Dāridrya-doṣo guṇa-rāśi-nāśi: when a person is poverty-stricken, naturally his false pride in aristocracy, wealth, education and beauty is smashed. Thus corrected, he is in the right position for liberation.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
A poverty-stricken man must automatically undergo austerities and penances because he does not have the wealth to possess anything. Thus his false prestige is vanquished. Always in need of food, shelter and clothing, he must be satisfied with what is obtained by the mercy of providence. Undergoing such compulsory austerities is good for him because this purifies him and completely frees him from false ego.
Commentary

When one is poor, automatically one practices detachment suitable for liberation. This is expressed in three verses. The poor person is free from lack of humility stemming from pride in wealth (nirahamstabha) and free from pride in family and education.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

The poor man is devoid of arrogance arising from pride in wealth (nirahaṁ-stambhaḥ). He is freed from agitation of the heart arising from all things related to wealth (sarva-madaiḥ muktaḥ). He experiences suffering such as lack of food by the nature of poverty (yadṛcchayā). That suffering is the best austerity because it destroys his pride. Like the best type of austerity, poverty cannot be the cause of pride.

Śrīdhara Svāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

The poor man becomes humble (stambhaḥ), free of pride in wealth (niraham). Or the poor man becomes devoid of the causes (stambhaḥ) of pride (wealth and power). By this he becomes the opposite of the wealthy man who does not consider his aging body etc. By the nature of poverty, he experiences suffering like hunger. Poverty is the best remedy because it destroys pride.

Personal Guidance
Forced poverty does the work of voluntary tapas — it strips the false ego built on wealth, birth, and beauty, leaving you fit for surrender to Kṛṣṇa.
Practical Applications
  • Identify one prop of false prestige today — title, salary, a possession — and refuse to mention or lean on it in a single conversation where you normally would.
  • Voluntarily accept one small inconvenience (a plainer meal, a longer walk, a cheaper option) and receive it as Kṛṣṇa's mercy rather than a hardship.
  • When something is taken from you today — money, comfort, recognition — name it aloud as tapas and thank Kṛṣṇa for the chance it gives you to drop ahaṅkāra.
Analogy

An ascetic vows to eat once a day, sleep on the ground, and own one cloth — tapas chosen for bhakti. A pauper is forced into the same regimen by circumstance. The bodies undergo the same austerities; whether ahaṅkāra is burned out or merely fermented depends on which mood the man brings to the privation.

Stories
Raghunātha dāsa Gosvāmī's renunciation

Born to immense Bengali wealth and married to a beautiful wife, Raghunātha dāsa repeatedly fled his palace to join Mahāprabhu. After his final escape, in Vraja, he reduced his food to nearly nothing, slept under a tree, and spent his hours weeping and chanting.

What the world saw as poverty was, in Caitanya-caritāmṛta, the highest tapas — voluntary stripping of every prop of false ego. SB 10.10.15 describes the forced version that does the same work without the choice; Raghunātha shows what the freely chosen version looks like in mādhurya-rasa.
