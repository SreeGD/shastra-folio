    Important Words
    • parvaṇi — on the new-moon day
      Locative of parvan, the joint of the lunar month. Demonic activity classically rises at amāvāsyā; the sages had told Balarāma that Balvala's pollution always came at the parva. The verse begins by marking sacred time — the moment Bhagavān's protection is most needed.
    • pūya-gandhaḥ — the smell of pus
      Compound: pūya (pus) + gandha (smell). The brāhmaṇas' yajña should carry the fragrance of ghee and flowers toward the devas; demonic mood inverts it into stench. Gauḍīyas read the contrast as the standing law of the worlds — wherever Hari's service rises, the asuric wind tries to foul the air.
    • pracaṇḍaḥ — fierce
      From pra + caṇḍa, 'extremely furious.' The wind itself is personified as violent — not weather, but a weapon of the demon. The verse is preparing us to see Balvala as the agent behind every sense-impression that strikes the sacrificial arena.
    • pāṁśu-varṣaṇaḥ — raining dust
      Compound: pāṁśu (dust) + varṣaṇa (rain). The first of a series of obscene 'rains' (dust, then pus, then trident-borne demon) that invert the auspicious abhiṣeka. Each downpour is a parody of bathing; only at verse 7 will Balarāma receive the true abhiṣeka, from the sages who survive the pollution.
    Śukadeva Gosvāmī said: Then, on the new-moon day, O King, a fierce and frightening wind arose, scattering dust all about and spreading the smell of pus everywhere.
    Gauḍīya Ācāryas
    Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
    Translation
    Śukadeva Gosvāmī said: Then, on the new-moon day, O King, a fierce and frightening wind arose, scattering dust all about and spreading the smell of pus everywhere.
    Commentary

    In the seventy ninth chapter, Balarama kills Balvala, goes on pilgrimage and returns to see Bhima and Duryodhana fighting. He then returns to Dvaraka. When the dark moon arrived (upavrtte), there was a strong wind filled with dust and the odor of pus.

    Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
    Commentary

    At that moment, a sharp (pracaṇḍaḥ), frightful (bhīmaḥ) wind smelling of pus arose. Śukadeva calls to Parīkṣit in amazement.

    The new-moon day is when the sages' yajña-fire should rise cleanest into the sky, and instead Balvala's stink-wind sweeps across it like a bucket of pus poured over an altar. The pure offering brāhmaṇas raise toward Kṛṣṇa is precisely what the demonic mood wants to foul; Balarāma comes to that arena because the protection of yajña is His own work.

    Stories
    Why Balarāma is at Naimiṣa at all

    In the previous chapter the sages of Naimiṣāraṇya had told Lord Balarāma that on every parva day for a long time the demon Balvala had been polluting their twelve-year sacrifice with showers of pus, blood and wine. They begged Him to free the yajña-bhūmi.

    Balarāma, halāyudha, did not refuse. The pilgrimage that fills this chapter begins from that promise — He waits for the new moon at Naimiṣa first, slays the polluter of the sacrifice, and only then turns south. The whole tīrtha-yātrā is bracketed by yajña-protection.

    Important Words
    • amedhya-mayam — full of abominable things
      Compound: a (not) + medhya (fit for sacrifice) + mayam (consisting of). The exact opposite of medhya — what cannot enter a yajña. Balvala's signature is to coat the sacred with the technically forbidden, the way Kali's signature is to introduce the inadmissible into every clean act.
    • yajña-śālāyām — upon the arena of the sacrifice
      Compound: yajña (sacrifice) + śālā (hall, arena), locative. The exact spatial target. Demonic obstruction is not random vandalism; it targets the yajña-bhūmi, because every successful yajña delivers offerings to Viṣṇu. Gauḍīyas extend the principle to the modern temple, the kīrtana-hall, the bhakta's japa-seat.
    • śūla-dhṛk — carrying a trident
      Compound: śūla (trident) + dhṛk (bearer). The trident is Śiva's weapon; Balvala wields a perverted copy. The verse subtly tells us that the demonic always parodies the divine — he imitates the symbols of authority while opposing the Person they serve.
    • balvalena — by Balvala
      Instrumental of Balvala. The name resembles bala (strength) but means the bloated, the obstinate. He is brute force without surrender — a study, by contrast, in what Bala-rāma is: bala that is also rāma, strength that gives delight.

    Important Words
    • sasmāra — remembered
      Perfect of √smṛ. Balarāma does not call out, does not stretch out His hand — He simply remembers, and the weapons appear. Gauḍīyas note that smaraṇa is one of the nine limbs of bhakti (SB 7.5.23); here we see that the Lord's own smaraṇa is itself creative — what He remembers, manifests.
    • mūṣalam — His club
      The musala (or mūṣala) is Balarāma's signature personal weapon alongside the hala. With the club He kills demons in close combat; the verse names the weapon by its specific function — para-sainya-vidāraṇam, that which tears asunder opposing armies.
    • halam — His plow
      The hala marks Balarāma as the protector of agriculture and brāhmaṇical order. He alone among the Yādavas carries the plow as weapon — Gauḍīyas read it as the emblem of cultivation, of breaking the hard ground of the demonic heart open so that yajña can be re-planted there.
    • daitya-damanam — which subdues the demons
      Compound: daitya (descendant of Diti, demon) + damana (subduer). The plow's adjectival epithet — the same instrument that prepares the earth for grain is the one that disciplines the daityas. Cultivation and chastisement are one act in the hand of Bhagavān.
    • upatasthatuḥ — presented themselves
      Dual perfect of upa + √sthā, 'stood near.' The verb is reverential — the weapons do not arrive like tools; they stand attendant like servants. In Vaiṣṇava grammar the Lord's paraphernalia is conscious, and serves Him as His own retinue.

Upon seeing him, Lord Balarāma thought of His club, which tears to pieces His enemies’ armies, and His plow weapon, which punishes the demons. Thus summoned, His two weapons appeared before Him at once.

    Commentary

    The various adjectives describe him with increasingly ferocious nature, from having a big body to having fangs and frowning eyebrows. Balarāma would give joy (rāmaḥ) to the sages by killing him. He thought of his club and plough since they could kill the enemy very easily.

    Analogy

    Balarāma's hala and musala do not need to be fetched from an armory — He only remembers them and they stand at His side. The plow that breaks earth open and the club that breaks demons open are extensions of His will, the way Kṛṣṇa's flute is an extension of His breath. The weapons of Bhagavān are not instruments He picks up; they are limbs of His līlā that arrive the instant He turns His attention.

    Stories
    Sudarśana arrives unsummoned

    When Ambarīṣa Mahārāja was threatened by Durvāsā's kṛtyā (Bhāgavatam 9.4), Lord Viṣṇu's Sudarśana cakra was already in position before the king could speak — the cakra serves the bhakta whether or not the Lord visibly commands it.

    Balarāma's plow and club obey the same law as Viṣṇu's disc: weapons of Bhagavān move at the speed of His sasmāra. They are not separate machines; they are His śakti taking the shape the moment requires.

    Important Words
    • brahma-druham — the harasser of brāhmaṇas
      Compound: brahma (brāhmaṇas, also the Veda) + druh (one who injures). The same word-field that condemns Hiraṇyakaśipu and Rāvaṇa. Bhāgavata-dharma turns lethal precisely at this point — Balarāma's anger is not personal but a defense of the dvija class who carries the Veda.
    • hala-agreṇa — with the front end of His plow
      Compound: hala (plow) + agra (tip), instrumental. The same plow that opens furrows for grain serves here as a hook to lift a demon down from the sky. The hala has two operations and one purpose: to keep the earth fit for yajña.
    • ākṛṣya — pulling toward Him
      Absolutive of ā + √kṛṣ, 'to drag.' The same root that gives the name Kṛṣṇa (the all-attractive). Balarāma's hala is the elder brother's ākarṣaṇa — what He draws toward Himself, no sky can keep away.
    • kruddhaḥ — angry
      From √krudh. Bhagavān's krodha is not vice; it is the form His protective will takes when His devotees are attacked. Gauḍīyas distinguish it from rajasic anger: it arises, accomplishes its work, and subsides without leaving residue.

    Important Words
    • sannihitaḥ hariḥ — Hari is directly manifested
      Compound: sam + ni + √dhā, 'placed near, permanently established' + Hari. The verse uses this phrase precisely for Śrī-raṅgam — among all tīrthas listed, this is the one where Hari Himself is recognized as ever-present. For Gauḍīyas the line authorizes Śrī-raṅgam as a Hari-kṣetra of the highest order.
    • veṅkaṭam — Veṅkaṭa Hill
      The abode of Bālajī (Veṅkaṭeśvara) at Tirumala. Vaiṣṇava tradition reads veṅkaṭam as 'that which burns away sin' (veṁ kaṭati). Balarāma touches Veṅkaṭa in the same line that names Śrī-raṅgam — the verse strings together the two greatest South Indian Viṣṇu-kṣetras.
    • gayām — to Gayā
      The tīrtha where Viṣṇu's footprint is worshipped and where śrāddha-rites for one's ancestors yield maximum benefit. Gauḍīyas know Gayā as the very place where the young Viśvambhara (Mahāprabhu) met Īśvara Purī and received the gopāla-mantra, beginning His revealed mission.
    • pitṝn iṣṭvā — worshiping His forefathers
      Compound clause: pitṝn (forefathers) + iṣṭvā (having worshiped). Balarāma performs pitṛ-yajña at Gayā — the Lord who is Himself the destination of all worship enacts pitṛ-pūjā, modeling that even an Avatāra honors the lineage He has appeared into.
    • draviḍeṣu — in the southern provinces
      Locative plural of Draviḍa. The verse explicitly names southern India as the field of pilgrimage. Gauḍīyas note Mahāprabhu's later south-India yātrā as a deliberate retracing of Balarāma's footprints — Saṅkarṣaṇa first, then Caitanya.
    • mahā-pātaka-nāśanam — which destroys the greatest sins
      Compound: mahā (great) + pātaka (sin) + nāśana (destroyer). Epithet of Setubandha. The Bhāgavatam classifies Setu as a tīrtha that destroys even mahā-pātaka — the five great sins; only Rāma-kṣetras carry that exact power, because they hold Rāma's footprint.
    Lord Balarāma bathed in the Gomatī, Gaṇḍakī and Vipāśā rivers, and also immersed Himself in the Śoṇa. He went to Gayā, where He worshiped His forefathers, and to the mouth of the Ganges, where He performed purifying ablutions. At Mount Mahendra He saw Lord Paraśurāma and offered Him prayers, and then He bathed in the seven branches of the Godāvarī River, and also in the rivers Veṇā, Pampā and Bhīmarathī. Then Lord Balarāma met Lord Skanda and visited Śrī-śaila, the abode of Lord Giriśa. In the southern provinces known as Draviḍa-deśa the Supreme Lord saw the sacred Veṅkaṭa Hill, as well as the cities of Kāmakoṣṇī and Kāñcī, the exalted Kāverī River and the most holy Śrī-raṅga, where Lord Kṛṣṇa has manifested Himself. From there He went to Ṛṣabha Mountain, where Lord Kṛṣṇa also lives, and to the southern Mathurā. Then He came to Setubandha, where the most grievous sins are destroyed.
    Purport

    Usually one goes to Gayā to worship deceased forefathers. But as Śrīla Viśvanātha Cakravartī explains, although Lord Balarāma’s father and grandfather were still alive, it was on His father’s order that He carefully worshiped His forefathers at Gayā. Drawing insight from the Vaiṣṇava-toṣaṇī, the ācārya further explains that although Lord Balarāma was in the immediate proximity of Jagannātha Purī, He did not go there, since He wanted to avoid the embarrassment of having to worship Himself among the forms of Śrī Kṛṣṇa, Balabhadra and Subhadrā.

    Commentary

    Going to each of the rivers such as the Gomati, Gandaki and Vipasa, he bathed in each one. At Gaya he worshipped the forefathers; that is, as his grandfather and father were still living, he worshipped those who came before that, on the order of Vasudeva. Bathing (upasprsya) at Ganga Sagara, after that he should have gone to Puri, but did not go, since it would be embarrassing for him to have to worship Krsna Subhadra and himself there. This is the opinion of Vaisnava Tosani. At Mahendra he offered prayers to Parasurama (rama).

    Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
    Commentary

    Since his father and grandfather were still living, he offered sacrifice to his ancestors at Gāyā on the order of Vasudeva. He bathed (upaspṛśya) at Gaṅgā-sāgara. Though it was easy to go from there to Puri, he did not go since it was not necessary to worship Jagannātha since Jagannātha was non-different from Kṛṣṇa who had now appeared in complete form with all the avatāras. Or there was no particular goal in going there, since he was directly present and the deity there was non-different from him. He would also be embarrassed to worship himself. He then saw Paraśurāma. He had bhakti for Paraśurāma. After that he went to Sapta-godavārī and other places and bathed there. Another version has sapta-godavaram. Venā and Bhīmarathī are rivers and Pampā is a lake. He saw the mountain of Veṅkaṭa most pure (mahā-puṇyam) because it was the abode of Trimalla-nātha (Balaji) and an important place of the Śrī-vaiṣṇavas. He went to Kāma-koṣṭi and other places (verb should be supplied), since he was capable of all things (prabhuḥ). In all those places he performed the pilgrimage duties completely. He went to Śrī-raṅgam where the Lord who destroys all suffering and attracts all (hariḥ) resides eternally (sannihitaḥ). Therefore the place was most pure (mahā-puṇyam). The word ca means “then.” He went to Samudra-setu which destroys great sins just by going there. One can atone for killing a brāhmaṇa there.

    Analogy

    This block reads like a recited mālā — Gomatī, Gaṇḍakī, Vipāśā, Śoṇa, Gayā, Gaṅgā-sāgara, Mahendra, the seven Godāvarīs, Veṇā, Pampā, Bhīmarathī, Śrī-śaila, Veṅkaṭa, Kāñcī, Kāverī, Śrī-raṅga, Ṛṣabhādri, Madurai, Setubandha — bead after bead of holy place strung on Balarāma's walking feet. The pilgrimage is itself a kīrtana of geography, every tīrtha sanctified again by the Lord retracing it.

    Stories
    Balarāma meets Paraśurāma at Mahendra

    Mahendra Mountain is the residence to which Paraśurāma retired after his twenty-one campaigns against the corrupted kṣatriya order (Bhāgavatam 9.15–16). Balarāma's visit there is one āveśa-līlā meeting another: the brāhmaṇa-protecting elder son of Yadu, holding plow and club, before the kṣatriya-chastising son of Jamadagni, holding axe.

    The two share an ancestral mood — both arrived to clear the earth of adharmic rulers — yet Paraśurāma is famously the one who, in Caitanya-caritāmṛta and the older Purāṇas, will later become a witness and devotee of the Yadu line. The encounter is a kind of intra-Viṣṇu courtesy.

    Śrī-raṅgam, where Hari is permanently present

    Śrī-raṅga, on an island in the Kāverī, is the place where Lord Raṅganātha reclines on Ananta — sannihito hariḥ, as the verse says, 'Hari is manifested here.' The Gauḍīya tradition honors Śrī-raṅgam through Mahāprabhu's four-month stay with Veṅkaṭa Bhaṭṭa during His South Indian tour, where He converted the entire Śrī-Vaiṣṇava family to gopī-bhāva.

    By the time the Bhāgavatam is recited at Naimiṣa, Hari is already 'sannihita' at Śrī-raṅgam. Balarāma's pilgrimage is therefore not just to a tīrtha but to His own younger brother's reclining form — Saṅkarṣaṇa visiting Viṣṇu.

    Stories
    Setubandha — the bridge of Rāmacandra

    Setubandha, also called Rāmeśvaram, is the stone-bridge built by Hanumān and the vānara army to cross to Laṅkā in the Rāmāyaṇa. When Lord Rāmacandra returned from Laṅkā, He installed a Śiva-liṅga there in gratitude — and the place became one of the seven mokṣa-puris.

    Agastya, who drank the ocean

    Agastya Ṛṣi is the southern sage par excellence — born from a pot, husband of Lopāmudrā, the seer who once drank the entire ocean to expose the asuras hiding within (Mahābhārata Vana-parva). Rāmacandra received the Brahmāstra from him on His way to Laṅkā.

    Balarāma bowing to Agastya in the Malayas is again Lord meeting His own previous līlā's teacher. Gauḍīyas read this whole chapter as Saṅkarṣaṇa retracing Rāma-līlā geography while Kṛṣṇa-līlā continues elsewhere.

      Phrase: ātmānam (Himself, accusative) + viśva-gam (universe-pervading). The reciprocal of the previous phrase — universe in Him, He in universe. Acintya-bhedābheda in the precise grammatical shape: simultaneous inside-and-outside, with the Person as the constant.

      From vi + √bhū, 'pervading, all-mighty.' A standing epithet of Bhagavān. Used here pointedly: the vibhu, the all-pervader, is also the One the sages now see pervading. Subject and object collapse into Person.
