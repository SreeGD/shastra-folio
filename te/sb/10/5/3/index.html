Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.5.3

śātakaumbhāmbarāvṛtān
Nanda Mahārāja gave two million cows, completely decorated with cloth and jewels, in charity to the brāhmaṇas. He also gave them seven hills of grain, covered with jewels and with cloth decorated with golden embroidery.
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Nanda Mahārāja gave two million cows, completely decorated with cloth and jewels, in charity to the brāhmaṇas. He also gave them seven hills of grain, covered with jewels and with cloth decorated with golden embroidery.
Commentary

Niyuta is twenty times one hundred thousand or two million. Tiladri or mountain of sesame is described in the Bhavisyaottara as follows: first class tiladri is ten drones, second class tiladri is five drones and third class tiladri is three dronas. 256 palas make one drona and 4 tolas make one pala. Thus in one drona there are 1024 tolas.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Niyute means two million (since niyuta is one million). This will be explained in the twelfth chapter. Kṣīrasvāmī confirms this in his commentary. The two million cows were decorated with silver hooves and gold horns. The amount is sesame is described. Bhaviṣyottara says: uttamo daśabhir drauṇair madhyamaṇ paṣcabhir mataḥ tribhiḥ kaniṣṭho rājendra tilaśailaḥ prakīrtitaḥ pūrvavac cāparaṁ sarvaṁ viṣkambhaṁ parvatādikam The best mountain of sesame is ten droṇas, then five droṇas and then three droṇas. They were all similar to mountain ranges. Concerning grains it says: itthaṁ niveśyāmara-śailam agryam atas tu viṣkambha-girīn krameṇa turīyabhagena caturdiśaṁ ca saṁsthāpayet puṣpa-vilepanāḍyān Putting the divine mountain in front, one should establish mountain ranges in four parts in the four directions, covered with flowers and sandalwood. The four mountains are Mandara Mountain and others. merur mahān vrīhi-mayas tu madhye suvarṇa-vṛkṣa-traya-saṁyutaḥ syāt pūrveṇa muktāphala-vajra yukto yāmyena gomedaka-puṣparāgaiḥ paścāc ca gārutmata-nīla-ratnaiḥ saumhye ca vaidūrya sarojarāgaiḥ brahmātha viṣṇur bhagavān purārir divākaro yatra hiraṇmayaḥ syāt Meru Mountain of rice in the middle should be decorated with three gold trees. In the east should be pearls and diamonds. In the south should be gomedhas (garnet) and yellow sapphires. In the west should be emeralds and sapphires. In the south should be cat’s eye gems and rubies. There also should a deity of Viṣṇu, like the sun, made of gold. śuklambarāṇy ambudharāvalī syāt pūrveṇa kṛṣṇāni ca dakṣiṇena vāsāṁsi paścād atha karburāṇi raktāni caivottarato ghanāni There should be white cloth in the east, black cloth in the south, multicolor cloth in the west and deep red cloth in the north. A droṇa is described. khārī-droṇāḍhaka prasthāḥ kuḍavaṁ ca palaṁ picuḥ sāṇako māṣakaś caiva yathā pūrvam catur guṇāḥ The droṇa, āḍhaka, praṣthā, kuḍava, pala, picu and māṣaka are from last to first, quadruple in quantity. A droṇa is therefore 256 pālas. The piles were coved with gold cloth resembling Sumeru and were placed in the eight directions and covered with gems. He gave all this excellently (pra), by first washing their feet, and having his men place the items in the houses of the brāhmaṇas.

Concepts
Jiva
Personal Guidance
Nanda Mahārāja's lavish dāna at Kṛṣṇa's birth shows that real wealth is what we joyfully release in yajña — give in proportion to your gratitude.
Practical Applications
  • Identify one thing you've been quietly hoarding — money, time, knowledge, or contacts — and give a portion of it away today, decorated with care, not leftovers.
  • Before your next purchase, pause and ask whether the same rupees could serve a brāhmaṇa, a devotee, or a hungry person, and redirect at least a token amount.
  • Refuse the modern reflex of giving stripped-down or 'efficient' charity — offer your dāna with full attention and beauty, as if presenting it directly to Kṛṣṇa.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my life am I giving from a place of calculation and reciprocity rather than from the overflow of gratitude that Nanda Mahārāja showed at Kṛṣṇa's appearance?
  • What would it look like for me to give the 'best of what I have' — not the leftovers — to those who serve Kṛṣṇa, and what attachment is that act exposing in me right now?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • When something good happens — you ace an exam, get into the program, the crush texts back — your default move is probably to post it. Try Nanda's move instead: give something away within 24 hours. Buy lunch for the friend who is struggling, Venmo a small amount to a temple or cause, hand off the notes you no longer need. Mark the auspicious moment with dāna, not with a story.
  • Audit your phone the way Nanda audited his wealth. What are you accumulating 'just in case' — saved reels, screenshotted outfits, follower counts, group chats you never leave? Pick one and renounce it this week as an experiment in aparigraha. Notice what comes up when you do.
  • In your friend group there is almost always one person quietly running short — on money, on time, on confidence. Identify them. Without making it a performance, give them something real this week (cover their coffee, send the notes first, introduce them to the senior they've been afraid to ask). Practice karuṇā as concrete action, not as a vibe.
householder
  • The next time household income spikes — bonus, tax refund, raise, a deal closing — decide the dāna percentage BEFORE the money lands, and route it the same day it arrives. Nanda did not deliberate; the auspicious moment and the giving were one motion. Pre-commitment is how you make this possible inside a family budget without it becoming a fight.
  • Sit with your spouse and name one item of family parigraha you have been justifying as 'for the kids' or 'for retirement' that is, honestly, hoarding dressed up as prudence. Decide together what a Nanda-scale gesture would look like at your scale — not symbolic, not painless. Then do it, jointly, this quarter.
  • Teach your children dāna by doing it visibly, not by lecturing. Let them see you write the check, hand the envelope, serve the meal to the brāhmaṇa or sādhu or guest. Nanda's giving was public — that is how the next generation learned what a householder's wealth is for. Your children are watching where the money actually goes; make sure some of it goes where it should.
senior
  • You are at the stage of life where parigraha quietly hardens into identity — the house, the collection, the title, the 'estate.' Begin a deliberate program of giving things away while you are still alive to see them received. Not via the will. Now. Each major item handed off in your own hand is a small rehearsal for the final letting-go.
  • Identify one or two younger practitioners — a grandchild, a junior colleague, a student at the temple — and become a Nanda for them: lavish, specific, structural support for their spiritual and practical formation. Pay for the course. Fund the trip to the dhāma. Stand the cost of the books. Mentoring at this stage is a form of dāna that no one else in their life is positioned to give.
  • Examine where you are still keeping a 'rainy day' reserve that is really a fear-of-death reserve. Speak with your spouse or a trusted devotee and move a meaningful portion of it into yajña — temple service, brāhmaṇa support, deity worship, scripture distribution — while you are still able to direct it personally. Aparigraha at sixty-plus is no longer theoretical; it is the actual work.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading the verse as ancient royal extravagance and quietly excusing oneself, instead of asking what proportional, decorated, whole-hearted dāna looks like in my own means today.
  • Treating dāna as a transactional purification of wealth — 'I gave, now I'm clean' — rather than as yajña, an offering whose sweetness lies in the spirit of giving to Kṛṣṇa's devotees.
  • Confusing aparigraha with stinginess or austerity-posturing, and so refusing to give beautifully and abundantly when the occasion (like Kṛṣṇa's appearance in one's life) actually calls for lavish, decorated offering.
Discussion Prompts
  • Nanda Mahārāja gave two million cows and seven hills of grain — a scale that feels almost absurd. Share with your partner: when you give, what is the largest 'gift' you have ever made relative to what you owned? Did the size of it feel like dāna or like loss? Where do you think the cut-off is between generosity and recklessness, and is that cut-off actually scriptural or just self-protective?
  • The verse has Nanda giving to brāhmaṇas at the moment of Kṛṣṇa's birth — generosity as an expression of yajña, not as charity to the needy. Discuss: is it more spiritually weighty to give to those who already have enough (a qualified brāhmaṇa, a temple, a guru) than to give to the visibly poor? Where do you and your partner disagree, and what does that disagreement reveal about your understanding of karuṇā vs. material compassion?
  • Nanda did not keep a 'rainy day' reserve before giving — the verse describes lavish, immediate, public dāna at an auspicious moment. In your own life, name one thing you are accumulating 'just in case' that is functioning as parigraha rather than as prudence. Be specific. Then let your partner press you: are you actually saving it, or hoarding it?
Role Play

Two students play a married couple. They have just received an unexpected windfall — a year-end bonus equal to six months of expenses. Student A wants to follow Nanda Mahārāja's example and give a substantial portion (say, 30%) to their family guru / temple / a qualified brāhmaṇa immediately, as yajña, while the auspicious feeling is fresh. Student B wants to put it all into the children's college fund and a home down-payment, arguing that providing for dependents IS their dharma and that 'Nanda was a king with two million cows — we are not.' A third student plays the family elder who must hear both sides and offer guidance rooted in the verse — without flattening either position. Run the scene for 8 minutes. Then swap roles so each student has to argue the side they did not pick first.

Objections & Responses
  • Two million cows and seven hills of grain — this is mythological exaggeration. Treating it as a model for real life is naïve. Modern people can't take these numbers seriously, and pretending we can just produces guilt or performative giving.
    The numbers are not the instruction — the bhāva is. Nanda Mahārāja's act tells us what a heart looks like when Kṛṣṇa has just entered the household: it cannot contain itself, it overflows toward the brāhmaṇas, toward yajña, toward everyone present. The question for us is not 'can I match two million cows' — of course not — but 'when something genuinely auspicious happens in my life, is my first instinct to hoard the good fortune or to distribute it?' The scriptural exaggeration, if you want to call it that, is precisely there to break our small-hearted calculus. If we read the verse and our first response is to argue down the number, that itself is the diagnosis.
  • Giving cows and grain to already-wealthy brāhmaṇas while poor people exist is bad ethics. Real compassion gives to those in need, not to a priestly class. This verse glorifies a feudal arrangement we should not be importing into modern life.
    This is the exact place where the Gītā distinguishes material compassion from real compassion. Material compassion targets bodily suffering — feed the hungry, clothe the cold — and that work is genuinely good and should continue. But dāna to a qualified brāhmaṇa, or to devotional service, is aimed at the soul: it sustains the people and institutions that keep transcendental knowledge alive in the world, which is the only thing that finally relieves anyone's suffering. Nanda was not choosing brāhmaṇas over the poor — kings of his stature gave broadly. He was modeling that the highest dāna goes where it nourishes the eternal, not only the temporary. A Gauḍīya teacher would not ask you to stop feeding the hungry; he would ask you to also fund the people and places that are keeping Kṛṣṇa-kathā alive, and to notice that secular ethics has no category for that second kind of giving.
