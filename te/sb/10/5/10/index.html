Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.5.10

Their lotuslike faces extraordinarily beautiful, being decorated with saffron and newly grown kuṅkuma, the wives of the cowherd men hurried to the house of mother Yaśodā with presentations in their hands. Because of natural beauty, the wives had full hips and full breasts, which moved as they hurried along.
Purport

The cowherd men and women in the villages lived a very natural life, and the women developed a natural feminine beauty, with full hips and breasts. Because women in modern civilization do not live naturally, their hips and breasts do not develop this natural fullness. Because of artificial living, women have lost their natural beauty, although they claim to be independent and advanced in material civilization. This description of the village women gives a clear example of the contrast between natural life and the artificial life of a condemned society, such as that of the Western countries, where topless, bottomless beauty may be easily purchased in clubs and shops and for public advertisements. The word balibhiḥ indicates that the women were carrying gold coins, jeweled necklaces, nice cloths, newly grown grass, sandalwood pulp, flower garlands and similar offerings on plates made of gold. Such offerings are called bali. The words tvaritaṁ jagmuḥ indicate how happy the village women were to understand that mother Yaśodā had given birth to a wonderful child known as Kṛṣṇa.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Their lotuslike faces extraordinarily beautiful, being decorated with saffron and newly grown kuṅkuma, the wives of the cowherd men hurried to the house of mother Yaśodā with presentations in their hands. Because of natural beauty, the wives had full hips and full breasts, which moved as they hurried along.
Commentary

The lotus faces of the gopis were splendid with new kumkuma powder. Balibhih (with gifts) indicates putting gold coins, jewel necklaces, expensive cloth, fruits such as coconut, rice, durba sandalwood and garlands on a gold tray, covering with that a gold tinged cloth, and holding it in the left hand. Though they had heavy hips, they moved quickly because of joy and enthusiasm.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Their faces were beautiful because of the fresh kuṁkuma made of saffron. Their faces actually became beautiful because of their great anurāga. They went quickly holding gem-studded trays of white rice even though they had wide hips. Their breasts moved to express their extreme longing to see Yaśodā and her child.

Śrīdhara Svāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Their faces were beautiful because of the fresh kuṁkuma made of saffron. Their faces actually became beautiful because of their great anurāga. They went quickly holding gem-studded trays of white rice even though they had wide hips. Or strong assistants (balibhiḥ) helped them so they could move fast. Or balibhiḥ indicates three folds of skin on their navels. Though their hips were broad, their breasts moved because of their quick gait. Or though they were incapable of moving quickly because of their heavy breasts, they still moved quickly.

Personal Guidance
The gopīs ran to Yaśodā with offerings — when Kṛṣṇa appears, real bhakti shows up bodily, eagerly, with something in hand to give.
Practical Applications
  • Bring something tangible — flowers, food, a coin — when you visit the deity or a Vaiṣṇava today; never arrive empty-handed before Kṛṣṇa.
  • Notice when you delay going to maṅgala-ārati or kīrtana out of comfort, and instead move tvaritam — quickly, like the gopīs hurrying to Yaśodā.
  • Refuse the artificial life that severs you from natural duty; choose one simple, embodied act of seva today over an abstract spiritual feeling.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my own life am I mistaking 'artificial' polish — curated images, manufactured presentation — for the natural fullness Prabhupāda points to, and what would living more naturally actually require me to give up?
  • When I bring an offering (bali) to Kṛṣṇa or to His devotees, am I hurrying with the unguarded eagerness of these gopīs, or do I withhold, calculate, and arrive empty-handed?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Audit your social media feed for one week: how much of it is the modern equivalent of 'topless, bottomless beauty for public advertisement' that Prabhupāda names? Mute or unfollow three accounts that train you to read bodies as products rather than as souls.
  • Pick one friend or classmate whose news you usually meet with envy or competition (a good grade, an internship, a relationship). The next time something good happens to them, do what the Vraja women did — go to them quickly, hands not empty, with a small genuine offering (a note, a treat, real time). Notice what shifts in you.
  • Before your next exam or presentation, prepare a small bali in your room — a flower, a coin, a sweet — and offer it to Kṛṣṇa with the specific mood: 'I am running toward this not to win, but to give You my effort.' Then go and perform.
householder
  • When a child is born to a friend, a colleague is promoted, or a neighbor moves in, do not send a digital message — go in person, hands not empty, in the spirit of the Vraja women carrying bali. Make this a household practice your children watch you keep.
  • Examine your family budget this month for one line item that represents 'artificial living' purchased at the cost of 'natural living' — convenience food replacing a shared meal, screens replacing presence, status purchases replacing simplicity — and reallocate that spend toward something sattvic and offerable.
  • With your spouse, identify one occasion this year where you will host neighbors or extended family the way Yaśodā was honored — not as performance, but as a household opening itself in joy around Kṛṣṇa at the center. Plan it concretely; put it on the calendar.
senior
  • Take inventory of the 'gold coins, jeweled necklaces, nice cloths' of your life — the accumulated possessions of decades — and identify three meaningful items to give away this month, in person, to younger family or devotees, as bali rather than as bequest. Let vairāgya look like generosity now, not paperwork later.
  • Identify one young householder or student in your circle who is, in their own way, 'a Yaśodā with a new Kṛṣṇa' — beginning sādhana, raising a child in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, starting a service. Go to them this week with a tangible offering and your blessing; do not wait to be asked.
  • Each evening, in your contemplation, rehearse the tvaritaṁ jagmuḥ mood — the hurrying toward Kṛṣṇa with hands full — as preparation for the final journey. Ask: what am I still carrying that is not bali, and can be set down before I go?
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading the purport's critique of modern civilization as license for crude commentary on women's bodies, instead of as Prabhupāda's pointed contrast between natural and artificial life that applies to one's own lifestyle choices.
  • Spiritualizing the verse into a vague 'be natural' platitude while ignoring the concrete instruction it models — drop what you are doing, gather a real offering, and run toward Kṛṣṇa with it.
  • Treating bali as merely symbolic and skipping the literal practice of bringing tangible offerings (flowers, food, cloth, service) to the Deity, guru, and Vaiṣṇavas, as if intention without substance were sufficient.
Discussion Prompts
  • The purport sharply contrasts the 'natural life' of Vraja's cowherd women with the 'artificial life' of modern Western society, including a pointed line about commercialized beauty. Where do you agree, and where do you find this comparison too sweeping or uncomfortable to repeat as-is in mixed company? What is the spiritual point underneath the social commentary?
  • The women run to Yaśodā's house carrying bali — gold, jewels, cloth, sandalwood, garlands — not as transaction but as overflow of joy at Kṛṣṇa's appearance. In your own life, where does giving feel like overflow, and where does it feel like obligation or display? Be specific; disagree with each other.
  • Prabhupāda reads physical detail (full hips, full breasts, lotus faces) as evidence of sattvic, unartificial living, not as objectification. Some in the group will find this reading liberating; others will find it jarring. Sit with the disagreement: what does it mean to read the body through the lens of sattva rather than through modern eyes?
Role Play

Two students play cowherd women of Vraja preparing to walk to Yaśodā's house with bali in hand. A third plays a skeptical neighbor (a city-dwelling cousin visiting Vraja) who asks, 'Why are you running? Why these gold coins and garlands for someone else's baby? What do you actually expect to receive in return?' The two must answer not with doctrine but from the bhāva of the moment — joy at Kṛṣṇa's appearance, dāna as overflow, yajña as the natural shape of a heart turned toward Him. Then swap roles. The teacher debriefs: where did the answers slip into transaction, performance, or sentimentality, and where did they ring true?

Objections & Responses
  • The purport's comments on women's bodies and 'topless, bottomless' Western clubs feel dated, sexist, and culturally chauvinistic. How can a serious modern student take this verse seriously?
    Read what Prabhupāda is actually pointing at: a contrast between life lived in harmony with prakṛti and sattva, and life lived under heavy rajas and tamas where the body is commodified for sale and advertisement. The Vraja-vāsīs are not idealized as objects; they are presented as souls whose outer life still matches their inner orientation toward Kṛṣṇa. The discomfort with the language is fair — but do not let it carry away the substance. The substance is: artificial living distorts even the body, and a society that sells the body publicly has lost something the cowherd women still had. A Gauḍīya teacher does not soften that diagnosis; he or she invites the student to feel it without flinching.
  • Why would Kṛṣṇa, the Supreme, need village women to bring Him gold coins and sandalwood paste? Surely the Absolute does not require gifts.
    He does not require — He receives, and in receiving He gives the giver the very thing the giver most needs: the chance to love. This is the heart of yajña in the personalist line. Kṛṣṇa is not an impersonal Absolute who is indifferent to offerings; He is a Person who reciprocates with the bhakta in the precise mood the bhakta brings. The gopīs run with bali not because Kṛṣṇa lacks anything, but because their hearts cannot hold the joy of His appearance without spilling it outward as dāna. To object that 'God doesn't need gifts' is to miss that bhakti is not a supply chain — it is a relationship, and relationships move through offering.
