Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.1.12

Important Words
  • kṛṣṇa-viceṣṭitam — the activities of Lord Kṛṣṇa
    Viceṣṭita, from vi-ceṣṭ, 'to move about, act distinctively.' Not generic 'activities' but Kṛṣṇa's specific, charactered movements. The compound itself frames līlā as recognizable behavior — Kṛṣṇa acts in a way that only Kṛṣṇa would act.
  • sarva-jña — because you know everything
    Sarva + jña — omniscient. Parīkṣit ascribes sarva-jñatva to Śukadeva not as flattery but as the operative qualification: the hearer cannot generate kṛṣṇa-kathā; he must receive it from one who knows. The speaker's omniscience is the condition of the hearer's progress.
  • śraddadhānāya — because I am not envious but have all faith in Him
    Dative of śraddadhāna, 'one having śraddhā.' Śraddhā in Gauḍīya is the first stage of bhakti — not blind belief but a confident trust that bhakti will yield Bhagavān. Without it, kathā lands on rocky soil; with it, even half-heard verses germinate.
  • vistṛtam — in full detail
    Same family as vistarāt (verse 3). Parīkṣit repeats his demand: do not summarize. The Bhāgavata's method is loving particularity — Yaśodā binding Kṛṣṇa with rope (10.9) takes nine verses, because the rope itself is the lesson.
O great sage, who know everything about Kṛṣṇa, please describe in detail all the activities of which I have inquired and also those of which I have not, for I have full faith and am very eager to hear of them.
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
O great sage, who know everything about Kṛṣṇa, please describe in detail all the activities of which I have inquired and also those of which I have not, for I have full faith and am very eager to hear of them.
Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

An ignorant person like me has asked many questions. You should explain all of this--whatever I have asked and also what I have not asked. You should explain the astonishing (vi) activities (ceṣṭitam) of Kṛṣṇa, Svayam Bhagavān in human form. You can do this because you are very conversant (muni) with these pastimes. “But since the pastimes are infinite, how can I be aware of them all?” You are omniscient (sarvajṣa). You know everything about them.

Personal Guidance
Like Parīkṣit, cultivate śraddhā so eager that you beg to hear every detail of Kṛṣṇa's activities — both what you asked and what you didn't.
Practical Applications
  • Read or hear one passage of Kṛṣṇa's pastimes today with the explicit posture of a śraddadhāna — eager, faithful, ready to receive what you didn't think to ask for.
  • Approach a sādhu, guru, or trusted devotee and ask an open-ended question about Kṛṣṇa, then refuse to interrupt — let them describe vistṛtam, in detail.
  • Notice when you treat hari-kathā as background noise, and instead sit, fold your hands inwardly, and listen as one whose liberation depends on it.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my hearing of Kṛṣṇa's līlā am I quietly filtering — accepting the parts that flatter my reason and skipping the ones that strain my śraddhā?
  • If I truly believed, like Parīkṣit, that I had only seven days left, which kṛṣṇa-viceṣṭita would I beg to hear today, and what is stopping me from hearing it right now?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Pick one teacher, parent, or mentor whose judgment you actually trust, and this week ask them: 'What should I be learning that I am not even thinking to ask about?' Then sit with the answer for 24 hours before reacting — that is śraddadhāna in your stage.
  • Audit your feed for one day: how much of what you 'hear' (scrolling, podcasts, group chats) did you choose with discrimination, vs. what was pushed at you? Replace 30 minutes of algorithmic input with one chapter of Bhāgavatam or Gītā, read with the posture of Parīkṣit — eager, not skimming.
  • In your friend group, notice the difference between performative skepticism ('I question everything') and genuine inquiry. This week, when something true is being said — by a professor, a parent, a scripture — practice the small tapas of *not* immediately countering it. Let it land first.
householder
  • Parīkṣit asks the sage to speak vistṛtam — in detail. With your spouse this week, pick one area of family life (a child's struggle, a financial decision, an elder's care) where you have been making fast, surface-level choices, and deliberately slow down to hear it 'in detail' — including what your spouse is *not* explicitly saying.
  • Identify one bona fide source of Kṛṣṇa-conscious guidance (a sādhu, a śāstra, a recorded class by a senior devotee in the paramparā) and commit to 20 minutes of hearing daily, with the children present at least twice a week. Children inherit śraddhā by osmosis, not by lecture.
  • Notice where you have been treating spiritual life as one more agenda item to 'cover' (chanted — check, read — check). Reset to Parīkṣit's mood: this is what you actually want, especially the parts you would not have thought to ask for. Offer the day's earnings, decisions, and worries to Kṛṣṇa each evening before sleep.
senior
  • Parīkṣit hears the Bhāgavatam in his final seven days. With however many years remain, formally restructure your time so that hari-kathā — hearing and discussing Kṛṣṇa's activities — becomes the main course, not a side dish around medical appointments and grandchildren's schedules.
  • Do not assume that decades of hearing have made you a sarvajña. Sit again as a śraddadhāna under a qualified speaker — even one younger than you — on a līlā you think you 'already know.' Watch what humility loosens in you that mere experience could not.
  • Mentor one younger seeker in the specific posture of this verse: not by telling them what to think, but by modeling how to ask — 'tell me even what I did not know to ask.' Your legacy is not your accomplishments; it is the quality of inquiry you transmit.
Common Pitfalls
  • Treating śraddhā as a feeling that must arise on its own, rather than a discipline cultivated by repeatedly placing oneself before a sarvajña-guru and asking, as Parīkṣit does, to be told even what one did not know to ask.
  • Approaching Kṛṣṇa's pastimes as mythology or moral allegory — extracting 'lessons' while quietly disbelieving the personal, transcendental Kṛṣṇa whose viceṣṭita is being narrated.
  • Hearing only the verses or pastimes one has already chosen, and calling that vistṛtam — when the verse explicitly asks for the elaboration, including 'that of which I have not inquired,' from one who actually knows.
Discussion Prompts
  • Parīkṣit asks Śukadeva to narrate even what he did not ask — share with your partner a time when a teacher (parent, guru, mentor) gave you something you did not request, and you later realized was exactly what you needed. Where in your current sādhana are you only willing to receive what you have already pre-approved?
  • The verse hinges on śraddhā — full faith — as the qualification to hear kṛṣṇa-viceṣṭitam. Disagree honestly with your group: is śraddhā a prerequisite for hearing, or does hearing produce śraddhā? Where have you experienced each direction in your own life?
  • Parīkṣit is seven days from death and uses that time to ask for *more* hari-kathā, not less. If you were given seven days, would you actually want expanded narration of Kṛṣṇa's activities — or would you reach for family, unfinished work, bucket-list items? Be honest with your partner about what that reveals.
Role Play

You are leading a weekly Bhāgavatam study group. A long-time member, Ravi, raises his hand and says: 'I have been coming for three years. I have heard the Kṛṣṇa-līlā section many times. Tonight I would like to skip ahead to the philosophical chapters — the līlās feel repetitive to me now.' Another member, Anjali, who is new, looks visibly deflated; she came tonight specifically to hear about Kṛṣṇa's childhood. Using SB 10.1.12 as your anchor, respond to Ravi in front of the group. Address (a) what Parīkṣit's posture of śraddadhāna asks of a 'senior' student, (b) the difference between having heard a līlā and having actually received it, and (c) how you will proceed with tonight's class without shaming Ravi or patronizing Anjali.

Objections & Responses
  • This is intellectually lazy — Parīkṣit says 'tell me everything, even what I did not ask,' which sounds like surrendering critical thinking. A modern reader should interrogate sources, not swallow them whole on faith.
    Notice what Parīkṣit is *not* doing: he is not abandoning discrimination — he is exercising it by choosing his speaker carefully (sarvajña, one who knows everything about Kṛṣṇa) and by choosing his subject carefully (kṛṣṇa-viceṣṭitam, not gossip, not speculation). Discriminative wisdom is upstream of śraddhā, not opposed to it. Once you have rightly identified a qualified source on a worthy subject, continuing to 'interrogate' is no longer rigor — it is ahaṅkāra protecting itself from being changed. The Gauḍīya line distinguishes śāstra-cakṣus (seeing through scripture, with a bona fide teacher) from blind belief; Parīkṣit models the former. Faith here is not the suspension of intelligence but its fulfillment — intelligence that has finally found something worthy of trust.
  • Why should I want to hear more pastimes of one particular deity from one particular tradition? Isn't this just sectarian devotion dressed up as universal spirituality?
    The verse itself answers this: Parīkṣit asks specifically for kṛṣṇa-viceṣṭitam — Kṛṣṇa's activities — not 'divine activities in general.' Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavas do not apologize for this specificity, and we should not flatten it for comfort. Kṛṣṇa is not one deity among many; He is the svayaṁ bhagavān, the Personality of Godhead from whom even other forms of divinity expand. To say 'tell me about the Absolute in general' is, in practice, to hear about no one — and a relationship cannot grow with no one. Real bhakti requires a real Person to whom you surrender (īśvara-praṇidhāna). The 'universal spirituality' objection often hides a deeper resistance: the impersonal Absolute makes no claim on me, but Kṛṣṇa, as a Person, does. That is precisely why Parīkṣit, on the eve of death, wants Him in vistṛtam — expanded detail — not abstraction.
Analogy

Parīkṣit asks for everything — even what he didn't think to ask — like a thirsty man holding out a bowl and saying, 'fill it past the rim, I'll trust your hand to know when to stop.' Śraddhā is the bowl; the speaker's discretion supplies what the hearer would not have known to ask. This is the disciple's posture: define the topic (Kṛṣṇa), define the source (your guru), then surrender the content.
