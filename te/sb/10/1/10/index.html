Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.1.10

Lord Kṛṣṇa lived both in Vṛndāvana and in Mathurā. What did He do there? Why did He kill Kaṁsa, His mother’s brother? Such killing is not at all sanctioned in the śāstras.
Purport

One’s maternal uncle, the brother of one’s mother, is on the level of one’s father. When a maternal uncle has no son, his nephew legally inherits his property. Therefore, why did Kṛṣṇa directly kill Kaṁsa, the brother of His mother? Mahārāja Parīkṣit was very much inquisitive about the facts in this regard.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Lord Kṛṣṇa lived both in Vṛndāvana and in Mathurā. What did He do there? Why did He kill Kaṁsa, His mother's brother? Such killing is not at all sanctioned in the śāstras.
Commentary

Madhu purya referes to mathura and dvaraka—the cities of the descendents of Madhu. Why did he kill his mother’s brother? He should not have done that, since Kamsa was his mother’s brother.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Please tell me Kṛṣṇa’s activities in those places. This is expressed in two verses. Vraja refers to Mahāvāna and Vṛndāvana. Madhupūri means Mathurā and Dvārakā, the cities of the Madhu dynasty. But while living in these places he went elsewhere and performed pastimes as well. Those pastimes should also be narrated. Keśava means “he who subordinates (vayate) Brahmā (ka) and Śiva (īśa) by his powers. Or va can mean “to exist.” The meaning is then “he who has Brahmā and Śiva as his excellent servants.” Therefore, many astonishing activities are possible in Kṛṣṇa. Why did he kill Kaṁsa? Addhā means “in person.”

Concepts
Jiva
Personal Guidance
Parīkṣit's question shows that Kṛṣṇa's pastimes never violate dharma — what looks like transgression is the Lord's protective līlā, and we must inquire deeply rather than judge.
Practical Applications
  • Bring one apparent contradiction in Kṛṣṇa's pastimes to a senior devotee today instead of silently doubting.
  • Refuse to evaluate Kṛṣṇa's acts by ordinary social rules — read the verse aloud and consciously set aside mundane judgment.
  • Imitate Parīkṣit Mahārāja: open your Bhāgavatam with an honest, specific question and read until that question is answered.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my life am I treating a familial or social tie — like Kaṁsa being Kṛṣṇa's maternal uncle — as a reason to tolerate behavior that is actually demonic, and what is that tolerance costing the souls around me?
  • When I appeal to śāstric rules or family duty to avoid a hard, necessary action, am I actually protecting dharma, or am I protecting my own comfort and reputation under the cover of dharma?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • You have a 'Kaṁsa' in your phone — an account, a group chat, a habit-loop that you know is corroding you, and you tolerate it because cutting it feels harsh. Today, name it specifically and cut it. That is sva-dharma, not cruelty.
  • In your friend group there is probably someone whose 'jokes' or pressure pull you away from sādhana, study, or self-respect. Real compassion is not laughing along; it is the quiet firmness of not participating, even at social cost. Practice this once this week and notice what survives.
  • When elders or relatives push you toward choices that contradict your conscience or your spiritual life, do not confuse politeness with dharma. Devakī and Vasudeva were silent under Kaṁsa for years; Kṛṣṇa was not. You can be respectful and still refuse — write out, in one sentence, the line you will not cross this semester.
householder
  • Identify the 'Kaṁsa' inside your household economy — a financial habit, a media intake, a social obligation that everyone tolerates because it is 'family.' As the head of the household, you are responsible for ending it. Schedule the conversation this week.
  • Raising children in māyā's current means you cannot only be gentle; sometimes love looks like a firm 'no' that the child resents today and thanks you for in twenty years. Distinguish between protecting their bodily comfort and protecting their soul — the second is your actual duty.
  • In career and money decisions, you will face moments where loyalty to a relative, partner, or boss requires you to compromise truth or devotional principle. Decide in advance — not in the heat of the moment — which lines belong to Kṛṣṇa and which to convenience. Write them down and tell your spouse.
senior
  • Look honestly at the relationships and possessions you are still 'protecting' out of habit. Some of them are Kaṁsas — they drain prāṇa, time, and remembrance of Kṛṣṇa. Loosening these now, deliberately, is not loss; it is the vairāgya proper to your āśrama.
  • Mentor a younger person this month with the firmness of Kṛṣṇa, not the indulgence of a grandparent. Tell them one true thing they will not hear from anyone else — about death, about māyā, about the futility of fruit-attachment. Real compassion serves their soul, not their mood.
  • Meditate daily on the fact that the Lord ultimately removes every Kaṁsa — including the body itself, which has tyrannized the soul through every birth. Welcome this not with dread but with the dhīra-bhāva of one who has been preparing. Begin offering, each evening, one attachment you are ready to release.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading Kṛṣṇa's killing of Kaṁsa as a license for personal vengeance or 'righteous anger' against difficult relatives — forgetting that only the Supreme Personality of Godhead acts beyond ordinary śāstric prescription, never the conditioned soul.
  • Concluding that rules of varṇāśrama and family respect are optional or outdated, when in fact Parīkṣit's very question shows that those rules remain binding for us; Kṛṣṇa's transcendental act is not a template for our conduct.
  • Treating material compassion (preserving Kaṁsa's bodily life and the niceties of the uncle-nephew bond) as higher than real compassion (delivering the soul and protecting the devotees), and thus hesitating to take firm action where firmness is what genuine love requires.
Discussion Prompts
  • Parīkṣit asks why Kṛṣṇa, the Supreme Personality of Godhead, broke the śāstric injunction against killing one's maternal uncle. In your pair, take opposite sides: one of you defends that Kṛṣṇa is bound by śāstra like anyone else; the other argues śāstra exists to serve Him. Where does the disagreement actually sit — in the nature of dharma, or in the identity of Kṛṣṇa?
  • Kaṁsa was Devakī's brother — family by blood, demon by conduct. Discuss a real situation in your own life where loyalty to a relative collided with loyalty to truth or to Kṛṣṇa. Did you act like Kṛṣṇa (decisive intervention) or did you hide behind 'family is family'? Be specific, not abstract.
  • Real compassion serves the soul; material compassion protects the body. Kṛṣṇa killing Kaṁsa was the highest mercy to Kaṁsa himself. Argue with a partner: is there anyone in your life right now to whom you owe spiritual firmness rather than social politeness — and what is the cost of continuing to confuse the two?
Role Play

Two students are assigned roles. Student A plays Mahārāja Parīkṣit on his last seven days, genuinely troubled: 'My grand-uncle Kṛṣṇa killed His own mother's brother. If even He can override śāstra, what stops any of us from inventing exceptions for ourselves?' Student B plays Śukadeva Gosvāmī and must answer without flattening the question — establishing Kṛṣṇa's supreme position, distinguishing Kaṁsa-the-demon from Kaṁsa-the-uncle, and showing why the apparent exception is actually the deepest fidelity to dharma. After five minutes, swap roles. The teacher then asks: which side was harder to hold, and why?

Objections & Responses
  • This is just divine-command ethics dressed up in Sanskrit. If Kṛṣṇa can kill His uncle and call it dharma, then 'dharma' means whatever the powerful want it to mean. That is dangerous theology.
    The objection assumes Kṛṣṇa is one moral agent among many, subject to a dharma that stands above Him. The Gauḍīya line, and Parīkṣit's own framing, holds the opposite: dharma proceeds from Kṛṣṇa, not the reverse — dharmaṁ tu sākṣād bhagavat-praṇītam. The killing of Kaṁsa is not Kṛṣṇa breaking the rules; it is Kṛṣṇa, as the source of the rules, acting in His personal capacity as protector of devotees (Devakī, Vasudeva, the saintly) and liberator of demons. Notice that Parīkṣit does ask the question — the tradition does not suppress the difficulty, it works through it. And critically, no human being can claim this exemption. We are bound by śāstra precisely because we are not Kṛṣṇa. The danger the skeptic fears is real — but it is the danger of impersonators, not of Kṛṣṇa Himself.
  • Modern readers find the violence disturbing. Couldn't Kṛṣṇa have reformed Kaṁsa, exiled him, shown a non-violent path? Killing a relative seems to glorify exactly the family-violence we are trying to outgrow.
    This is material compassion speaking — the impulse to spare the body at all costs. Kaṁsa had spent years murdering infants, terrorizing saints, and personally hunting Kṛṣṇa from before His birth; reform was offered repeatedly through Akrūra, through Nārada, through Kaṁsa's own father Ugrasena, and refused. When the Lord finally killed him, Kaṁsa attained liberation — constant fear-meditation on Kṛṣṇa is itself a form of absorption, and dying by His hand grants what years of sādhana cannot. That is real compassion: serving the soul, not soothing the body. The verse is not glorifying family-violence; Parīkṣit explicitly flags that ordinary śāstra forbids it. It is showing that the Lord's intervention, when it comes, serves the eternal welfare of even His apparent victim. The question for us is not 'should Kṛṣṇa have been gentler' but 'where am I confusing niceness with love?'
