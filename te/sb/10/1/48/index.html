Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.1.48

As long as he has intelligence and bodily strength, an intelligent person must try to avoid death. This is the duty of every embodied person. But if death cannot be avoided in spite of one’s endeavors, a person facing death commits no offense.
Purport

It is natural for a person facing untimely death to try his best to save himself. This is one’s duty. Although death is sure, everyone should try to avoid it and not meet death without opposition because every living soul is by nature eternal. Because death is a punishment imposed in the condemned life of material existence, the Vedic culture is based on avoiding death ( tyaktvā dehaṁ punar janma naiti ). Everyone should try to avoid death and rebirth by cultivating spiritual life and should not submit to death without struggling to survive. One who is not trying to stop death is not an intelligent human being. Because Devakī was face to face with imminent death, it was Vasudeva’s duty to save her, as he was trying his best to do. He therefore considered another way to approach Kaṁsa so that Devakī would be saved.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
As long as he has intelligence and bodily strength, an intelligent person must try to avoid death. This is the duty of every embodied person. But if death cannot be avoided in spite of one's endeavors, a person facing death commits no offense.
Commentary

As long as a person has power and intelligence (yavad buddhi balodayam), he will try to avoid death. I cannot use strength to avoid Devaki’s death at Kamsa’s hands, but I can use my intelligence. Asau refers to death.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

This verse shows how he thought, with the understanding that Kaṁsa was evil. Death should be avoided as long as one has excellence of bodily strength and intelligence. Previously his intelligence was normal but now it acted in a special manner. There is no fault of using deceit for a person dependant on his body, where no alternatives are possible.

Concepts
Jiva
Personal Guidance
As long as intelligence and strength remain, an embodied soul must strive to avert untimely death — surrender begins where genuine effort ends, not before.
Practical Applications
  • Identify one health, safety, or spiritual risk you have been passively ignoring, and take one concrete protective step today.
  • Refuse the fatalism of 'whatever happens, happens' — schedule the doctor visit, fix the brake, or have the hard conversation you have been postponing.
  • Each evening, ask whether today's struggle to preserve life and dharma was offered to Kṛṣṇa, the way Vasudeva strategized to save Devakī.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my life am I quietly accepting a slow 'death' — of my sādhana, my health, my relationships — without using the buddhi and bala Kṛṣṇa has given me to actively oppose it?
  • When my efforts to avert a real danger fail, do I collapse into guilt or fatalism, or can I rest in the knowledge that a sincere endeavor itself fulfills my duty as an embodied soul?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • When you are tempted to 'check out' — quitting a hard course, ghosting a difficult friendship, numbing yourself with hours of scrolling — recognize this as a small surrender to a kind of death. Use your buddhi (which is still sharp) and bala (which is still strong) to stay engaged.
  • Your body and mind are at peak strength right now. Treat sleep, food, exercise, and screen-discipline as sva-dharma — protecting the instrument Kṛṣṇa gave you for sādhana. Skipping meals to study, or pulling all-nighters on your phone, is a slow violation of this verse.
  • When peer pressure pushes you toward a substance, a relationship, or an online space that quietly damages you, name it: 'this is something an intelligent person would resist.' You are allowed — required, even — to fight for your own life and clarity.
householder
  • Take the unromantic, intelligent steps to protect your family's life: regular health check-ups for your spouse and parents, term insurance, an emergency fund, a written will. Avoiding these out of squeamishness about mortality is the very negligence this verse condemns.
  • When a child or spouse is in crisis — mental health, addiction, a bad relationship — do not retreat into 'it is their karma.' Vasudeva did not. Use every ounce of buddhi and bala to intervene wisely, then offer the result to Kṛṣṇa.
  • In your career, distinguish between the deaths worth resisting (loss of integrity, loss of sādhana time, loss of family presence) and the 'deaths' you should accept (loss of status, loss of a promotion that would have devoured you). Fight hard for the first; let the second go.
senior
  • Do not romanticize giving up. If a treatable condition arises, treat it — your continued presence is itself service to your family and your sādhana. The verse explicitly says no offense if death cannot be avoided despite endeavor; the offense is in not endeavoring.
  • At the same time, use this season to put your affairs in order so that those you leave behind are not burdened: documents, accounts, instructions, a clear will, conversations you have been postponing. This is buddhi applied to the inevitable.
  • Mentor a younger devotee or family member in this exact balance — fighting intelligently for life while remaining unattached to its fruits. Your lived example of Vasudeva's poise, neither clinging nor capitulating, is a teaching no lecture can replace.
Common Pitfalls
  • Twisting 'death is sure' into a fatalistic excuse for neglecting health, livelihood, or the protection of dependents — mistaking inaction for detachment.
  • Reading the verse as permission to fight only physical death, while ignoring the deeper death of forgetting Kṛṣṇa — the very condition Vedic culture exists to end (tyaktvā dehaṁ punar janma naiti).
  • Using 'no offense if death cannot be avoided' to justify giving up too early, before genuinely exhausting one's intelligence and strength as Vasudeva did for Devakī.
Discussion Prompts
  • Vasudeva is negotiating with a demon to save his wife's life. Where is the line between intelligent self-preservation and compromising with adharma? Share a moment when you faced this exact tension — and disagree openly if your partner would have drawn the line differently.
  • Prabhupāda says 'one who is not trying to stop death is not an intelligent human being.' How do you reconcile this with the verses on detachment from the body and accepting Kṛṣṇa's will? When does 'accepting God's plan' become spiritual laziness disguised as surrender?
  • The verse implies a duty to fight for life 'as long as one has intelligence and bodily strength.' Apply this to a real case: a terminally ill family member refusing further treatment, or someone in deep depression refusing help. Is non-resistance to death ever legitimate, or always a failure of duty?
Role Play

Two students pair up. Student A is a devotee whose elderly parent has just been diagnosed with a serious but treatable illness. The parent says, 'I have lived my life. I want to chant and prepare for death — no hospitals, no treatment.' Student B plays the parent, holding firm. Student A must, in five minutes, respond as a Gauḍīya practitioner who has internalized SB 10.1.48 — honoring the parent's spiritual aspiration without colluding in a premature surrender that the verse calls a failure of buddhi. Then switch roles. Debrief: where did you feel pulled toward sentimental 'acceptance' versus the harder duty Vasudeva models?

Objections & Responses
  • If the soul is eternal and death is just a change of body (BG 2.13), why bother resisting death at all? Isn't this verse contradicting the Gītā's whole teaching on detachment from the body?
    The Gītā teaches that the dhīra is not bewildered by the body's passage — it does not teach that he hastens it or welcomes it carelessly. Human birth is rare and is the one form in which bhakti and liberation are possible; to throw it away is to squander Kṛṣṇa's gift. Detachment from the body means not identifying with it, not neglecting it. Vasudeva himself knew the soul is eternal, yet he fought for Devakī's life because the human form is the platform for sādhana. Resisting untimely death is not attachment — it is reverence for the opportunity Kṛṣṇa has given.
  • This sounds like it justifies any survival tactic — lying, manipulating, even compromising with evil people, as Vasudeva did with Kaṁsa. Doesn't bhakti demand we'd rather die than act unethically?
    Notice what Vasudeva actually did: he used buddhi to find a path that preserved life without abandoning truth or dharma. He did not become Kaṁsa's accomplice; he negotiated within Kṛṣṇa's larger plan. The verse rules out one thing — passive submission to death when intelligence and strength remain — not the cultivation of viveka about how to act. A devotee uses every faculty Kṛṣṇa has given to remain in a position to serve. Martyrdom-romanticism, where one preens about dying rather than negotiating, can itself be a subtle form of ahaṅkāra. The harder, more humble path is Vasudeva's: stay alive, stay faithful, keep serving.
