Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.1.31-32

Devakī’s father, King Devaka, was very much affectionate to his daughter. Therefore, while she and her husband were leaving home, he gave her a dowry of four hundred elephants nicely decorated with golden garlands. He also gave ten thousand horses, eighteen hundred chariots, and two hundred very beautiful young maidservants, fully decorated with ornaments.
Purport

The system of giving a dowry to one’s daughter has existed in Vedic civilization for a very long time. Even today, following the same system, a father who has money will give his daughter an opulent dowry. A daughter would never inherit the property of her father, and therefore an affectionate father, during the marriage of his daughter, would give her as much as possible. A dowry, therefore, is never illegal according to the Vedic system. Here, of course, the gift offered as a dowry by Devaka to Devakī was not ordinary. Because Devaka was a king, he gave a dowry quite suitable to his royal position. Even an ordinary man, especially a high-class brāhmaṇa, kṣatriya or vaiśya, is supposed to give his daughter a liberal dowry. Immediately after the marriage, the daughter goes to her husband’s house, and it is also a custom for the brother of the bride to accompany his sister and brother-in-law to exhibit affection for her. This system was followed by Kaṁsa. These are all old customs in the society of varṇāśrama-dharma, which is now wrongly designated as Hindu. These long-standing customs are nicely described here.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Devakī's father, King Devaka, was very much affectionate to his daughter. Therefore, while she and her husband were leaving home, he gave her a dowry of four hundred elephants nicely decorated with golden garlands. He also gave ten thousand horses, eighteen hundred chariots, and two hundred very beautiful young maidservants, fully decorated with ornaments.
Commentary

Parivarham means dowry. Yane means at the time of the journey

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

The marriage festivities are described in three verses so show how it was impossible for Kaṁsa to show cruelty at that time. Verses 31 and 32 are one sentence. There were four hundred elephants with gold garlands or shining like a line of gold. There were three varieties of chariots, six hundred of each type. The girls were of late kaiśora age decorated with the best cloth and ornaments. Devaka gave all this with the greatest faith (prādāt) at the time of departure (yāne). Another version has yānaiḥ (with palanquins). The young girls were in the palanquins.

Personal Guidance
Wealth and opulence rightly flow within varṇāśrama as expressions of affection and duty — receive and give them as service, never as the goal.
Practical Applications
  • Notice one possession or gift you cling to today, and mentally re-offer it to Kṛṣṇa as something passing through your hands, not owned by you.
  • Speak to a family member you have a duty toward — parent, sibling, child — and perform one concrete act of vatsala-affection rooted in their long-term spiritual welfare, not just bodily comfort.
  • Refuse to measure your worth or another's by the scale of dowry, salary, or assets; instead, honor the varṇāśrama role each person is fulfilling today.
Reflection Questions
  • When I give to those I love — children, spouse, family — am I giving in a way befitting my position and capacity, like Devaka, or am I quietly withholding out of attachment to accumulation?
  • Where in my life am I confusing the lavishness of a material send-off with genuine well-wishing for the soul of the one I am sending forth?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Look at what your parents have spent on you this year — tuition, phone, rent, food. Write the actual number down. Sit with the fact that this is your dowry-equivalent flowing daily, and ask whether your use of it honors the vātsalya behind it or treats it as entitlement.
  • Notice the next time a friend posts a wedding, engagement, or 'soft launch' on Instagram and you feel the comparison tighten in your chest — Devakī left home with four hundred elephants and still walked into Kaṁsa's chariot. Material send-off is not the same as a protected life. Name what you actually want underneath the envy.
  • If you are in a relationship or dating, have one honest conversation this week about money, family expectations, and what each side's parents will or will not give. Most of your generation avoids this until it explodes. Treat it as sva-dharma, not awkwardness.
householder
  • Sit with your spouse this month and write down, concretely, what you intend to give each child — not vaguely 'a good education' but the actual ṣaṭ-śatam of your household: house, savings, wedding contribution, business seed. Decide it now, in sattva, not later under emotional pressure.
  • Audit one place where your 'giving' to your children has slid from vātsalya into control — paying for something so you can dictate the choice, or withholding so they stay dependent. Devaka gave and let her go to her husband's house; the giving and the releasing were a single act.
  • When extended family pressures you about dowry, wedding spending, or 'what people will say,' practice answering from varṇāśrama clarity rather than middle-class fear. The verse shows lavish giving as legitimate; it does not show giving to satisfy in-laws' demands or social envy. Know the difference out loud.
senior
  • Make or revise your will this quarter. Devaka gave at the moment of his daughter's leaving; you are now at the moment of your own leaving. Specify, name, sign — do not leave your children to fight over an unspoken estate. This is vātsalya as paperwork.
  • Identify one possession you have been holding 'for the grandchildren' that you could actually hand over now, while you can see their face receive it. Vairāgya is not refusing to give; it is giving while you are still here to bless the giving.
  • Mentor one younger householder in your community on how to navigate marriage, dowry, and family wealth without either modern contempt for tradition or rigid pretense that nothing has changed. Your decades of watching families succeed and fail are themselves a dowry — pass them on before you go.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading this verse as a license for ostentatious display — turning weddings and family rituals into spectacles of wealth rather than affectionate, sva-dharma-appropriate giving.
  • Dismissing varṇāśrama customs like dowry as 'outdated Hindu culture,' missing Prabhupāda's point that these are long-standing Vedic structures that nourished family affection and social order.
  • Mistaking material opulence (elephants, horses, maidservants) as the measure of an affectionate father, while ignoring that the deepest gift a parent gives is orientation toward Kṛṣṇa — the very atmosphere into which Devakī was about to bring the Lord Himself.
Discussion Prompts
  • Devaka gave Devakī a staggering dowry — four hundred elephants, ten thousand horses. Share with your partner: where in your own family is wealth being transferred (or withheld), and what is it actually communicating about love, duty, or control? Disagree honestly — is opulent giving an act of vātsalya or a subtle bondage?
  • Prabhupāda defends the dowry system as part of varṇāśrama-dharma, while modern law in many places treats it as exploitative or illegal. In small groups, take opposing sides: is the contemporary critique a corruption of the original Vedic intent, or has the institution itself rotted beyond repair? Be specific about what you would actually do if your daughter were marrying next year.
  • Devaka acts from duhitṛ-vatsalaḥ — affection for his daughter — yet the very next verses show this affection sitting beside Kaṁsa's demonic envy in the same family. Discuss: how do you account for one household producing both a tender father and a tyrant brother? What does this say about the limits of material affection as a spiritual qualification?
Role Play

Two students take roles: a father (kṣatriya-like, financially comfortable) and his 24-year-old daughter who is about to marry. The father wants to give her a substantial dowry — a house, a car, savings — citing this verse and the long Vedic custom. The daughter has read modern critiques and tells him it makes her feel like property being transferred, and that her in-laws will treat her as a balance sheet. A third student plays a Gauḍīya teacher whom the family approaches for guidance. The teacher must neither flatten Prabhupāda's purport into modern egalitarianism nor weaponize 'tradition' to silence the daughter's concern. Run the scene for ten minutes, then switch roles so each student must defend a position they did not pick.

Objections & Responses
  • Prabhupāda's defense of dowry is exactly the kind of thing that discredits the tradition for educated modern people. Dowry has destroyed countless women's lives in India — bride-burnings, harassment, suicide. How can a teacher in good conscience repeat that 'a dowry is never illegal according to the Vedic system'?
    The objection is fair if we collapse two different things. What Prabhupāda is defending is an affectionate father giving his daughter her share of family wealth at the moment she leaves the natal home — because under varṇāśrama she does not inherit later. That is duhitṛ-vatsalaḥ, the father's love operating within a structure that protected her economic standing. What modern India has produced is the opposite: a demand made by the groom's family, extracted under threat, with the bride as hostage. That is not dowry in the Vedic sense, that is extortion dressed in the clothes of tradition. A Gauḍīya teacher should name this distinction clearly, not defend the abuse, and not pretend the verse is silent on what real vātsalya looks like.
  • This whole verse is just an inventory of wealth — elephants, horses, chariots, maidservants. What possible spiritual instruction can we extract from a Bronze Age dowry list? Isn't this exactly the kind of passage that proves the Bhāgavatam is more cultural artifact than scripture?
    The Bhāgavatam never separates the spiritual from the material setting in which devotion has to be lived. Vyāsa is showing us a household at the moment of transition — a daughter leaving, a father giving, a brother accompanying — because Kṛṣṇa's appearance is being staged inside an actual family with actual economic and social texture, not in a philosophical vacuum. The 'inventory' is the point: even in opulence, even in royal abundance, the next breath of the narrative is Kaṁsa's envy and the death of Devakī's children. The verse trains us to see that material provision, however lavish, never secures the soul — and that real vātsalya, if it stops at giving elephants and not Kṛṣṇa, leaves the daughter unprotected in the only way that finally matters.
