Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.1.44

Important Words
  • droham — envy
    Droha = malice, treachery. Distinct from dveṣa (hatred) in being directed at one's own — especially family, dependents, friends. Kaṁsa's act is paradigmatic droha: betraying the bonds his own family-position created. Bhāgavata's social ethics single out droha as among the gravest faults.
  • ācaret — one should act
    Optative of ā-car, 'to practice, conduct.' Imperative force: 'one should never carry out.' The verse moves from descriptive (envy harms) to prescriptive (do not envy) — Vasudeva's argument culminates in a direct ethical order.
  • kṣemam — welfare
    Kṣema = security, well-being. Same word in BG 9.22 (yoga-kṣema, the Lord's promise to supply and secure His devotee). Vasudeva appeals to Kaṁsa's kṣema — his own welfare. The verse models speaking to the listener's actual concerns even when those concerns are limited.
  • drogdhuḥ — of one who is envious of others
    Genitive of drogdhṛ — 'the one who envies, who commits droha.' Vasudeva names the perpetrator from outside, even though Kaṁsa is the perpetrator in front of him. The Bhāgavata's pastoral rhetoric: speak of the case in third person so the listener can hear what he cannot yet say.
Therefore, since envious, impious activities cause a body in which one suffers in the next life, why should one act impiously? Considering one’s welfare, one should not envy anyone, for an envious person must always fear harm from his enemies, either in this life or in the next.
Purport

Instead of being inimical toward other living entities, one should act piously by engaging in the service of the Supreme Lord, thus avoiding a fearful situation both in this life and in the next. In this regard, the following moral instruction by the great politician Cāṇakya Paṇḍita is very meaningful: tyaja durjana-saṁsargaṁ bhaja sādhu-samāgamam kuru puṇyam aho rātraṁ smara nityam anityatām One should give up the company of devils, demons and nondevotees and should always associate with devotees and saintly persons. One should always act piously, thinking that this life is temporary, and not be attached to temporary happiness and distress. The Kṛṣṇa consciousness movement is teaching all of human society this principle of becoming Kṛṣṇa conscious and thus solving the problems of life forever ( tyaktvā dehaṁ punar janma naiti mām eti so ’rjuna ).

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Therefore, since envious, impious activities cause a body in which one suffers in the next life, why should one act impiously? Considering one's welfare, one should not envy anyone, for an envious person must always fear harm from his enemies, either in this life or in the next.
Commentary

a person covered by ignorance (sa tatha vidha) should not commit violence. For a person who commits violence to others (drogdhu) there is fear of harm from Yama and others (paratah). In this verse Vasudeva uses bheda to instill fear.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

“You have explained how one achieves another body and the difficulties encountered from karma. If you have this knowledge why do you make efforts to protect your body? But if I lack that knowledge then I will make efforts to protect my body.” This verse answers such a doubt. Since pious acts produce good results and being absorbed in the body produces ignorance, even if you cannot do the pious acts and give up absorption in the body, you must give up violence to others. Why? You should desire your benefit in this and the next world since the person who commits violence must fear others. This is use of bheda or dissention (mental doubt) of two types: showing fear in this life and the next. It should be understood that at the beginning of topics of Kṛṣṇa, the goal scripture is to teach knowledge of ātmā and detachment to make the materialist give up his absorption in his body.

Śrīdhara Svāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

In order to speak about topics of Kṛṣṇa, Vasudeva first spoke of the inevitability of death, the ātmā’s acceptance of various bodies, the variety of bodies, and the illusion of attachment to enjoyment. Without such knowledge, with investigation of one’s true benefit, the taste for topics of the Lord will not arise. That should be understood in later conversations also. The person, taking birth in the manner described (saḥ), should not commit violence, desiring his future benefit. If he does, he will suffer similar violence in the future. Seeing the suffering in others, one should imply similar suffering on oneself. He should rather show compassion. Or the person, because he has experience suffering (tathāvidhaḥ) should not commit violence and cause suffering to others. There is fear of suffering from karma, and fear from seeing the suffering of others.

Personal Guidance
Envy plants the seed of fear in this life and the next — guard your welfare by refusing enmity and engaging in Kṛṣṇa's service instead.
Practical Applications
  • Notice the one person you most resent today, and consciously refuse to wish them harm — even silently in thought.
  • Replace one act of subtle hostility (a barbed comment, a withheld kindness) with a small service offered in Kṛṣṇa's name.
  • Before sleep, audit the day for any droha you carried, and surrender that fear-producing seed at Kṛṣṇa's feet through a round of japa.
Reflection Questions
  • Toward whom am I quietly nursing droha — a coworker, a sibling, an old friend — and what fear of retaliation (in this life or the next) am I refusing to connect to that grudge?
  • When I justify a cutting word, a withheld kindness, or a small act of revenge as 'just deserved,' whose kṣema (welfare) am I actually undermining — theirs, or my own future self who will inherit the reaction?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Audit your phone for one week: how many minutes do you spend looking at the social media of one specific person whose success quietly burns you? That number is your droha-meter. Replace half of those minutes with chanting or reading — watch how your sleep changes.
  • In your friend group there is almost certainly one person being subtly excluded — the WhatsApp side-chat, the inside jokes, the plans 'accidentally' not shared. Notice whether you are participating. This week, do one concrete act of inclusion toward that person, without announcing it. Cāṇakya's bhaja sādhu-samāgamam starts with refusing to be the durjana yourself.
  • Before your next exam or competition, write down the names of classmates you secretly hope will do worse than you. Then offer those exact people to Kṛṣṇa — pray for their success by name. Notice the resistance in your chest. That resistance is the ahaṅkāra the verse is pointing at.
householder
  • Identify the one family member — a sibling, a cousin, an in-law — whose phone calls you avoid because of an old wound. The verse says you are carrying parato bhayam (fear from that quarter) into your own home, your own children's atmosphere. Pick up the phone this week. You do not need to resolve the grievance; you need to stop feeding it.
  • In your career, name the colleague or competitor whose failure would relieve you. Then ask honestly: how much of your strategic thinking is actually shaped by wanting them to lose, rather than wanting your own work to flourish? Reorient one decision this week toward your actual sva-dharma instead of toward beating them.
  • Your children are watching how you talk about people who are not in the room. Every cutting remark about a relative, a neighbor, a boss is teaching them droha as a household norm. For one month, institute a rule for yourself only (not announced): you will not speak ill of anyone absent at the dinner table. Watch what fills the silence.
senior
  • Make a list of the people you are still angry with — former business partners, an estranged sibling, a spouse long gone, a parent who hurt you. The verse says drogdhur vai parato bhayam — the envious carry fear across the boundary of death itself. You do not have decades to keep this cargo. Write one letter this week, sent or unsent, that releases one name from the list.
  • When younger people in your family or community succeed in ways you did not — financially, spiritually, in marriage, in children — notice the subtle sting. That sting at sixty or seventy is the same droha as at twenty, only better disguised. Bless them aloud. Mentorship is incompatible with envy; pick one.
  • You are closer to the next body than to the last one. Use the time you have left to associate deliberately with sādhus (in person, in books, in kīrtana) and to withdraw from gatherings where gossip and grievance are the main currency. Cāṇakya's smara nityam anityatām — remember constantly that this is temporary — is no longer abstract for you. Let it shape this afternoon's calendar.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reducing this verse to worldly diplomacy — 'don't make enemies because they'll come after you' — and missing that the bhayam Kṛṣṇa names is the karmic reaction shaping the next body, not merely social blowback.
  • Outsourcing droha to systems and institutions: refusing to harbor personal envy while cheerfully participating in collective hostility (office politics, online pile-ons, partisan contempt) as if group action dilutes individual karma.
  • Mistaking non-droha for sentimental tolerance of adharma — confusing the verse with material 'compassion' that avoids ever correcting or restraining the wicked, when real ahiṁsā means refusing envious harm while still acting piously in Kṛṣṇa's service, including Cāṇakya's instruction to give up the company of durjana.
Discussion Prompts
  • Share with your partner one person you currently feel envy or low-grade hostility toward. Without naming them, describe the 'fear of harm' (parato bhayam) you secretly carry because of that grudge — the sleep you lose, the relationships you reroute around them, the energy you spend rehearsing arguments. Is the cost worth the satisfaction?
  • Prabhupāda quotes Cāṇakya: tyaja durjana-saṁsargaṁ bhaja sādhu-samāgamam — give up bad company, seek sādhu-saṅga. Disagree productively: is there anyone in your life right now whom your family or community would call durjana but whom you defend? On what grounds? And conversely, are there 'devotees' you avoid because they make you uncomfortable?
  • The verse says the envious person fears harm in this life and the next. Modern people accept the first half easily (enemies create stress) but reject the second (next-life consequences). Discuss in your group: does the practical instruction of this verse change at all if you bracket the rebirth claim? Or does removing it gut the teaching?
Role Play

Two students play colleagues at a startup. Player A has just been passed over for a promotion that went to Player B, who is younger, less experienced, and (Player A is convinced) politically connected to leadership. Player A has been quietly sabotaging Player B's projects for three weeks — withholding information in meetings, CC-ing Player B's manager on minor mistakes. Today, Player B walks over to Player A's desk and asks, sincerely, 'Hey, have I done something to upset you? It feels like something's off between us.' Play the next four minutes of conversation. After the scene, the class discusses: Where did Player A's droha (envy/malice) originate — in Player B's actions, or in Player A's own ahaṅkāra? What does ātmanaḥ kṣemam anvicchan ('seeking one's own welfare') actually require of Player A here? What is the bhakti-yukta response, not just the polite one?

Objections & Responses
  • This sounds like spiritualized cowardice. The verse is essentially saying 'don't make enemies because they might hurt you back.' That's not ethics, that's risk management dressed up as dharma. A real moral teaching would say 'don't harm others because harm is wrong,' full stop — not 'because you'll suffer for it.'
    Read the verse again carefully — Kṛṣṇadāsa Kavirāja and Vyāsa are not naive. The verse uses self-interest (ātmanaḥ kṣemam anvicchan) as the entry point precisely because that is where the conditioned soul actually lives. The Bhāgavatam meets us at the level of our actual motivations, not the level we pretend to operate at. Once you genuinely see that droha boomerangs — that the 'enemy' you nurse in your heart is colonizing your own consciousness — you stop from a place of clarity, not fear. And Prabhupāda extends it immediately in the purport: the positive program is service to the Supreme Lord, sādhu-saṅga, and remembering anityatām (the temporariness of this life). It is not 'play it safe.' It is 'play the long game — the game that spans janmas.'
  • Telling people not to harbor enmity sounds nice in a temple, but in the real world some people genuinely deserve our opposition — abusers, exploiters, people destroying the planet. Doesn't this verse counsel a passive, doormat spirituality that lets evil flourish?
    There is a precise distinction in the tradition between droha (personal envy, malice, the wish that another suffer) and dharma-yuddha (righteous opposition to adharma). Arjuna had to fight Bhīṣma and Droṇa — but the entire Gītā is Kṛṣṇa training him to fight without droha in his heart. The verse is not forbidding firm action against wrongdoing; it is forbidding the inner posture of envy that masquerades as moral outrage. Test yourself: when the person you 'oppose for justice' fails or suffers, is there a flicker of pleasure? That flicker is droha, and it is what generates the next body of suffering. Real opposition to evil flows from daya (compassion) — even for the wrongdoer's eternal soul — not from personal grievance dressed in ethical clothing.
Analogy

Vasudeva closes his philosophical excursus and returns to the practical: do not envy anyone, for the envious must always fear retaliation in this life or the next. Envy is like building one's house with paper walls in a city of arsonists — sooner or later, someone strikes the match. The verse converts metaphysics into prudent self-interest, the only language a Kaṁsa still understands.
