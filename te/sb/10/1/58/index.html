Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.1.58

What is painful for saintly persons who strictly adhere to the truth? How could there not be independence for pure devotees who know the Supreme Lord as the substance? What deeds are forbidden for persons of the lowest character? And what cannot be given up for the sake of Lord Kṛṣṇa by those who have fully surrendered at His lotus feet?
Purport

Since the eighth son of Devakī was to kill Kaṁsa, one might ask what the need was for Vasudeva to deliver the first-born child. The answer is that Vasudeva had promised Kaṁsa that he would deliver all the children born of Devakī. Kaṁsa, being an asura, did not believe that the eighth child would kill him; he took it for granted that he might be killed by any of the children of Devakī. Vasudeva, therefore, to save Devakī, promised to give Kaṁsa every child, whether male or female. From another point of view, Vasudeva and Devakī were very pleased when they understood that the Supreme Personality of Godhead, Kṛṣṇa, would come as their eighth son. Vasudeva, a pure devotee of the Lord, was eager to see Kṛṣṇa appear as his child from the eighth pregnancy of Devakī. Therefore he wanted to deliver all the children quickly so that the eighth turn would come and Kṛṣṇa would appear. He begot one child every year so that Kṛṣṇa’s turn to appear would come as soon as possible.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
What is painful for saintly persons who strictly adhere to the truth? How could there not be independence for pure devotees who know the Supreme Lord as the substance? What deeds are forbidden for persons of the lowest character? And what cannot be given up for the sake of Lord Kṛṣṇa by those who have fully surrendered at His lotus feet?
Commentary

Now the question arises, "How can I, who am afraid to tell a lie (sadhu), tolerate the murder of my own son before my eyes?" He gives the answer in this verse. Is it not intolerable for the sadhu? How could Vasudeva offer all the children, even without Kamsa ordering, instead of offering just the eighth child to Kamsa? It is not reasonable that a householder be indifferent to his children. The next phrase therefore replies. Vasudeva is not ignorant like the normal person in the material world. For a person with knowledge, devotion and detachment like Vasudeva, is this to be expected? On the other hand, can Kamsa kill his child when he brings that child to him? Is there no mercy in his heart? In answer to this he says, what can a wicked person not do? In order to avoid fault, should Vasudeva give up householder dharma and not produce any children? He answers: He who has desire to see the Lord in the form of his son, who holds the lord in his hear (drtatma) stays in his household life and bears a son. He could have affection for no other son. So let the eighth child be born quickly. "Let me willingly offer up the other children year after year, and take responsibility for their death, out of eagerness to see the Lord."

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Nu indicates conjecture. What is difficult for the devotees to tolerant? They tolerate everything. The other sentences can be given similar meaning. Śrīdhara Svāmī’ has another explanation for the words. The unique quality of each individual is pointed out, not just a normal quality. What is intolerable for the person who keeps his promise (sādhūnām)? This indicates that Vasudeva gave up his desire to raise his sons. The fourth sentence should not repeat the meaning of the second sentence. Therefore in answer to the question “Why did Devakī give up her child?” it is said “What is difficult to renounce for those with determination.” “What is desired by the learned?” involves things which are unattained. “What is difficult to give up for the determined?” involves things which have been attained. That is the difference. Another explanation is as follows. In order to confirm the tolerance of the devotee, the other statements are made as examples.

Śrīdhara Svāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Nu indicates rhetorical expression. What is difficult for the devotees to tolerate? They tolerate everything. The other questions in the verse can be given a similar meaning. Śrīdhara Svāmī’ has another explanation for the words. Or in order to confirm the tolerance of the devotee, the other statements are made as examples. Or it was mentioned that Vasudeva gave his son with great pain (kṛcchreṇa) in the last verse. “How could he bear that pain?” This verse answers. What do the devotees not tolerate? “Why did he not go to another place?” Those who know that the Lord will appear from Devakī, or those who know glories of Mathuṛa, do not think of other places. “But one should not think that he was attached to his house.” Those who have controlled minds, who are not attached to their house (dhṛtātmānām) can give up anything. What is difficult for them to give up, just as, what will a rascal not do? Or “Why did Devakī not offer the child?” Vasudeva feared Kaṁsa would again try to kill her. What will the rascals not do?

Personal Guidance
For one surrendered at Kṛṣṇa's lotus feet, nothing is too painful to bear, nothing too dear to give up — His service is the one true measure.
Practical Applications
  • Name one comfort or possession you have been quietly refusing to offer in Kṛṣṇa's service, and offer it today.
  • Notice the next moment of inconvenience in your sādhana and refuse to treat it as 'unbearable' — recall Vasudeva delivering each child to Kaṁsa.
  • Speak to Kṛṣṇa directly this morning and tell Him plainly: 'There is nothing I will withhold from You today.'
Reflection Questions
  • When I say something is 'too painful' or 'impossible' to bear, what does that confession reveal about where I have placed my identity — in the body and its comforts, or in the soul's relationship with Kṛṣṇa?
  • What is the one thing I am still refusing to give up for Kṛṣṇa's service, and what story am I telling myself about why a 'real' surrendered soul would still be allowed to keep it?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Identify one promise you made to a friend group or peer that is quietly costing you — a commitment to drink, to gossip, to perform a certain image online. Vasudeva kept his word because it served the larger plan; ask honestly whether your word is serving anything larger than your fear of being dropped.
  • During this exam season, practice samatā: when a result comes back — high or low — give yourself sixty seconds before posting, texting, or comparing. The dhṛtātmā does not let the swing of one mark dictate the swing of his mind. Let the result land, breathe, then act.
  • Pick one thing on your phone that you tell yourself you 'cannot give up' — an app, a feed, a notification stream. For one week, give it up as an offering to Kṛṣṇa, not as a productivity hack. Notice whether the 'cannot' was real, or whether it dissolved the moment surrender entered the picture.
householder
  • When the next family decision arrives that pits your given word against your convenience — a commitment to your spouse, a promise to a child, a vow to a parent — choose Vasudeva's path. Keep the word, even at cost, and watch what kind of trust it builds in your home. Sādhus do not find such things duḥsahaṁ.
  • Sit with your spouse this week and name, out loud, the one thing in your shared life — a possession, an ambition, a grudge, a habit — that you have been treating as dustyajaṁ. Don't decide to give it up yet; just let it be named between you and offered to Kṛṣṇa. Naming is the first crack in the 'cannot.'
  • In your work this week, watch for the small kadarya temptation — the shortcut that hurts no one visible, the rounded number on the invoice, the silence when honesty would cost you. The verse cuts both ways: what is forbidden for a person of low character? Nothing. Catch the moment you almost slip into that 'nothing' and choose the harder line.
senior
  • Make a written list of three attachments — to a property, a role, a recognition, a relationship dynamic — that you have been holding tightly into this stage of life. For each, ask: if Kṛṣṇa asked for it tomorrow, through circumstance, would I be a dhṛtātmā or would I resist? Begin loosening the grip on one of them this month.
  • Find one younger person — a grandchild, a neighbor, a junior colleague — and tell them, concretely, one story from your life where keeping your word cost you something and was still the right choice. Mentoring in this tradition is not advice-giving; it is showing that the dhīra's path is actually walkable.
  • Each evening, take five minutes to mentally hand back the day — its accomplishments, its irritations, its small humiliations — to Kṛṣṇa. At this stage, the practice is not acquiring more; it is rehearsing the final letting-go, so that when the body is finally asked, nothing will be dustyajaṁ.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading the verse as four detached aphorisms rather than as one rhetorical sweep — each line is meant to expose where I sit on the spectrum from kadarya (lowest character) to dhṛtātman (fully surrendered), not to admire saints in the abstract.
  • Romanticizing Vasudeva's willingness to hand over his children as poetic surrender, while quietly exempting my own attachments (career, reputation, relationships, comfort) from the same standard of dustyajaṁ kim — 'what cannot be given up?'
  • Flipping the verse's logic into license — concluding that because nothing is forbidden to the lowest character, 'breaking rules for Kṛṣṇa' justifies whim or sentiment, when the verse is actually contrasting depravity with the disciplined renunciation of the dhṛtātman.
Discussion Prompts
  • Vasudeva agreed to hand over each newborn child to Kaṁsa to keep his word. Share with your partner: was this dhṛti (steadfastness in vow) or moral cowardice? Where in your own life have you confused 'keeping a promise' with 'avoiding the harder right action'? Push each other — don't let the easy answer win.
  • The verse says nothing is dustyajaṁ — impossible to give up — for one fully surrendered to Kṛṣṇa. Name one thing in your life right now you genuinely believe you cannot give up. Then let your group press you: is it really Kṛṣṇa-centered surrender that's missing, or are you using 'I'm not advanced enough' as cover for not wanting to let go?
  • Compare: the kadarya (lowest character) for whom no deed is forbidden, and the dhṛtātmā (self-controlled devotee) for whom no sacrifice is impossible. Both are described as 'unrestrained.' Discuss with your group: what is the actual difference between the two, and how do you tell them apart in someone — including yourself?
Role Play

A teacher splits the class into trios. One student plays Vasudeva, one plays Devakī (silently weeping in the background), one plays a modern friend who has just been told the plan: 'I'm going to hand over our firstborn to Kaṁsa because I gave my word.' The friend's job is to argue every modern objection — 'break the promise, run away, lie, fight him' — for five minutes. Vasudeva must respond from the verse's logic: what is duḥsahaṁ for a sādhu, what is dustyajaṁ for a dhṛtātmā. Then swap roles. Debrief: where did the modern logic feel strongest? Where did Vasudeva's steadiness actually hold? What did playing Devakī — the one who suffers the consequence — reveal that the debate alone missed?

Objections & Responses
  • This verse seems to glorify Vasudeva for handing infants over to a murderer. Isn't this exactly the kind of religious passivity that lets evil flourish? A modern ethical person would have hidden the children, fought, or died trying — not coolly delivered them.
    The verse is not asking us to admire passivity; it is pointing to a specific kind of strength — the dhīra who has weighed the situation and chosen the path that preserves both his vow and the larger plan of Kṛṣṇa's appearance. Vasudeva was not naive; he was a pure devotee who knew the eighth child would set everything right. His daksya (resourcefulness) was active, not passive — he negotiated, he kept Devakī alive, he positioned himself to receive Kṛṣṇa. The modern impulse to 'do something dramatic' often serves the doer's self-image (ahaṅkāra) more than the actual welfare of others. Real compassion serves the soul and Kṛṣṇa's plan, not the bodily comfort we feel by 'taking a stand.' The verse is not a license for passivity; it is a rebuke of our assumption that loud action is always nobler than steady, surrendered action.
  • The claim that 'nothing is impossible to give up for those surrendered to Kṛṣṇa' sounds like spiritual bypassing. In practice, this kind of teaching has been used to coerce people into giving up healthy attachments — to their families, their careers, their own well-being. Why should I trust it?
    The verse is descriptive, not prescriptive — it describes what becomes natural for one who has actually tasted Kṛṣṇa-bhakti, not a standard you must force yourself to meet today. Prabhupāda is clear: vairāgya without bhakti becomes dry and often cruel; renunciation pushed ahead of realization breeds hypocrisy. The Gauḍīya line is yukta-vairāgya — engage everything in Kṛṣṇa's service rather than artificially renounce it. So the householder is not asked to abandon family; she is asked to see family as Kṛṣṇa's, to serve them as such, and to find that attachments naturally loosen as taste develops. Coercion is the symptom of a teacher who has skipped the bhakti and gone straight to the renunciation — that is not this tradition. The verse asks: what would surrender look like in your actual stage of life? Not: prove your surrender by destroying what you have.
