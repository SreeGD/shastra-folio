Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.1.18

Important Words
  • gauḥ — the shape of a cow
    The cow is doctrinally privileged in Vaiṣṇava dharma — protected, worshipped, and identified with the Earth's giving nature. Kṛṣṇa is govinda, 'one who gives pleasure to the cows.' Bhū-devī's choice of form is itself an appeal Kṛṣṇa cannot refuse.
  • aśru-mukhī — with tears in the eyes
    Aśru (tear) + mukha (face) + ī (feminine). The Bhāgavata's narrative grammar treats tears as a kind of mantra — they reach Bhagavān faster than argument. Gajendra's tear-lotus, Draupadī's sari-tears, Bhū-devī's weeping: each summons direct intervention.
  • khinnā — very much distressed
    Past participle of khid, 'to be afflicted, depressed.' The verse names the state precisely — not anger, not strategy, but depression. The Lord moves toward khinna devotees because khinnatā empties the self of its usual self-management.
  • karuṇam — piteously
    From karuṇā, 'compassion-arousing.' The same word names karuṇa-rasa in dramatic theory. Bhū-devī weeps in the register that produces compassion in the hearer — and Bhagavān, the supreme rasika, is the supreme respondent.
Mother earth assumed the form of a cow. Very much distressed, with tears in her eyes, she appeared before Lord Brahmā and told him about her misfortune.
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Mother earth assumed the form of a cow. Very much distressed, with tears in her eyes, she appeared before Lord Brahmā and told him about her misfortune.
Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

What was her appearance? She took the form of a cow to invoke pity. She was distressed. She cried with shaking voice. Instead of rudatī sometimes krandantī is found. Her face was covered with tears. Viṣṇu Purāṇa and Hari-vaṁśa describe her speaking pitifully because of pain (khedāt karuṇa-bhāśiṇī). Brahmā is described as vibhoḥ because he endowed with powers of controlling the universe since he is empowered by the Lord. She directly (sam) expressed her suffering to Brahmā. Instead of sam sometimes svam (her suffering) is found. tat sāṁpratam amī daityāḥ kālanemi purogamāḥ / martya-lokaṁ samākramya bādhante 'harniśaṁ prajāḥ // kālanemir hato yo 'sau viṣṇunā prabhaviṣṇunā / ugrasena-sutaḥ kaṁsaḥ saṁbhūtaḥ sa mahāsuraḥ // āriṣṭo dhenukaḥ keśī pralaṁbo narakas tathā / suṁdo 'suras tathāty ugro bāṇaś cāpi baleḥ sutaḥ // tathānye ca mahāvīryā nṛpāṇāṁ bhavaneṣu ye / samutpannā durātmānas tān na saṁkhyātum utsahe // akṣohiṇyo hi bahulā divya-mūrti-dharāḥ surāḥ / mahābalānāṁ dṛptānāṁ daityendraṇāṁ mamopari // tad bhūribhāra pīḍārtā na śaknomy amareśvarāḥ / bibhartum ātmānam aham iti vijṣāpayāmi vaḥ // kriyatāṁ tan mahābhāgā mama bhārāvatāraṇam / yathā rasātalaṁ nāhaṁ gaccheyam ativihvalā // Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.1.22-.28 The demon Kālanemi previously attacked the earth and afflicted the people constantly. Killed by powerful Viṣṇu, he was born as the powerful demon Kaṁsa, the son of Ugrasena. Āriṣtā, Denuka, Keśī, Pralamba, Naraka, Sunda, and fierce Bāṇa, the son of Bali, as well as other great warriors have been born in the houses of kings. It is not possible to count them. They have numerous armies, O shining form! O lord! Pained by the great weight, I cannot bear the burden of these proud demon kings of great strength. I inform you of this. Please relieve me of this burden so that I do not go to Rasātala in distress.

Personal Guidance
When the burden of adharma grows unbearable, even the earth weeps to Brahmā — Kṛṣṇa hears such cries and descends; align your life with that protection.
Practical Applications
  • Notice today where your own conduct adds weight to the earth — wasted food, careless speech, indulgence — and refuse one such burden before sundown.
  • Speak to Kṛṣṇa directly about a real distress in your life, plainly as Bhūmi did, instead of carrying it alone in private rumination.
  • Identify one duty (kartavya) toward a dependent — a parent, child, junior, or animal — that you have been neglecting, and discharge it concretely today.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my own life have I, like Bhū-devī, silently borne the weight of others' adharma rather than approaching the proper authority and naming the misfortune plainly?
  • Whose burden am I currently treating as 'not my problem' — and what does the earth's tearful appeal to Brahmā reveal about my kartavya to speak up on behalf of those who cannot defend themselves?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Name the 'burden' on you right now — the exam pressure, the friend group dynamic, the algorithm you cannot stop scrolling — and do what Bhū-devī did: take it, in plain words, to one senior person (parent, mentor, devotee), not to your group chat.
  • Audit one platform you use daily for whether it is daivī or āsurī in spirit (BG 16.1 vs 16.4) — does it cultivate truthfulness, cleanliness, non-violence, or the opposite? Cut your time on the āsurī one in half this week, not as self-improvement but as ahiṁsā toward your own consciousness.
  • When you see a peer being crushed — bullied, isolated, slipping — do not perform 'awareness' on social media. Real karuṇā in this verse is appeal to the right authority. Tell a teacher or a parent, by name, what you have seen. That is your sva-dharma as a witness.
householder
  • Identify the one decision in your family or career where you have been carrying the burden alone out of pride. This week, formally bring it to a senior devotee or your iṣṭa-guru in the mood of Bhū-devī — not to be rescued, but to surrender the weight to its proper level.
  • In raising your children, distinguish material compassion (smoothing every discomfort) from real compassion (forming a soul who can bear dharma). Pick one comfort you have been over-providing — screens, snacks, conflict-avoidance — and withdraw it this month as tapas on their behalf.
  • Examine your work: is your livelihood adding to the earth's burden — through the product, the supply chain, the way colleagues are treated — or relieving it? You do not have to quit tomorrow, but name it honestly to your spouse this week and decide one concrete corrective step (BG 18.1, kartavya).
senior
  • You have watched many āsurī tides rise and fall in your lifetime. Sit with one younger person this month and tell them, without bitterness, what you have seen — this is your Brahmā-role now: receiving the earth's complaint and pointing to Kṛṣṇa, not solving it yourself.
  • Loosen one attachment this season — a possession, a grudge, a role you still cling to — explicitly as preparation for the body's next transition (BG 2.13). Offer the loosening to Kṛṣṇa rather than calling it 'downsizing.'
  • Where you still hold authority — in family, trust, congregation — ask whether you are exercising it as daivī-sampat (humility, non-violence, truthfulness) or whether ahaṅkāra has crept in under the name of experience. Name one place to step back and let a younger devotee carry the weight, with your blessing rather than your control.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading the earth's distress as mere ecological metaphor and reducing the verse to a generic 'care for the planet' message, missing that her grief is over the burden of asuric rulers and adharma — a moral condition, not an environmental one.
  • Treating Brahmā as the final refuge in the story, when in fact he himself turns to Kṣīrodakaśāyī Viṣṇu — students stop short at the demigod and miss that real relief comes only from Kṛṣṇa, the supreme Personality of Godhead.
  • Confusing material compassion (wanting the earth's bodily discomfort to end) with real compassion (wanting the souls oppressing her to be reformed or removed) — and so responding with sentimental activism rather than sober, dharma-restoring action.
Discussion Prompts
  • Mother earth approached Brahmā, not Kṛṣṇa directly. Share with your partner: when has it been right for you to take a problem to a senior devotee or guru rather than 'going straight to Kṛṣṇa' yourself? Where do we use 'I'll just pray about it' as an excuse to skip the chain of authority?
  • The verse describes the burden of demoniac (āsurī) leadership crushing the earth herself. Discuss honestly: name one institution, company, or government you participate in that you suspect is closer to āsurī-sampat than daivī-sampat (BG 16.1, 16.4). What is your sva-dharma inside it — reform, withdrawal, or something else? Expect to disagree.
  • Mother earth wept. Pair up and answer differently: is grief a sign of weakness, of attachment, or of bhakti? When the demigods and the earth weep, are they failing the dhīra-standard of BG 2.13, or showing us something the dhīra alone cannot show?
Role Play

Two students role-play. Student A is a mid-level manager at a company whose product they have come to believe is actively harming users — addictive design aimed at teenagers. Student B is the senior devotee Student A has come to for guidance, in the role of Brahmā receiving Bhū-devī. Student A must describe the 'burden' concretely — what is being crushed, by whom, and what they have already tried. Student B must not jump to a quick answer; instead, listen fully, then guide Student A toward the next step in the chain (do they stay and reform? leave? escalate? whom do they appeal to above themselves?). After ten minutes, swap. Class then debriefs: where did the 'Brahmā' rush to fix? Where did the 'earth' leave out the real burden?

Objections & Responses
  • This is mythology — a personified earth crying to a four-headed god. Why should a modern, scientifically literate person take this seriously as guidance for life?
    We are not asked to take it as geology; we are asked to take it as ontology. The verse claims the cosmos is personal at every level — earth is not inert matter but a conscious being under Kṛṣṇa's arrangement, and so are you. If consciousness is fundamental, the appropriate response to overwhelming adharma is not a policy paper but an appeal up the chain to a higher conscious authority. The 'mythological' frame is precisely what trains us to see our own crises the same way: not as mechanical problems to optimize, but as appeals we make, and that are made through us, to Kṛṣṇa. Strip the personalism out and you are left with the impersonalist drift the Gauḍīya line specifically rejects.
  • If Kṛṣṇa is supreme and all-powerful, why does the earth have to go to Brahmā at all? Why this bureaucratic detour instead of Kṛṣṇa just acting?
    Because Kṛṣṇa governs through varṇāśrama and through devatās — He honors the structure He has set up rather than constantly bypassing it. Bhū-devī's appeal through Brahmā is not a detour; it is kartavya — duty-through-proper-channels — and it is also how Kṛṣṇa orchestrates His descent. Practically, this teaches us two things. One: when adharma presses on you, you are not meant to demand a private miracle; you are meant to take the burden to the next legitimate authority — guru, śāstra, sādhu — and trust the line. Two: Kṛṣṇa's response, when it comes, is often slower and grander than the impatient prayer wanted — here, an entire avatāra. The chain of command is not an obstacle to His intervention; it is the form of it.
Analogy

Mother Earth assumes the form of a cow because the cow is the image of one who gives milk and is repaid with the knife. Her tears are not a performance; they are the precise testimony of a giver who has been turned into prey. The Bhāgavata loves this image — when the giver weeps, Bhagavān moves.
