Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.1.13

నైషాతిదు:సహా క్షున్మాం త్యక్తోదమపి బాధతే । పిబన్తం త్వన్ముఖామ్భోజచ్యుతం హరికథామృతమ్ ॥ ౧౩ ॥

Important Words
  • ati-duḥsahā — extremely difficult to bear
    Ati + duḥ-saha — 'unbearable to the highest degree.' Parīkṣit is not minimizing his bodily condition. The verse's force is precisely that he has acknowledged the seriousness of physical thirst and yet finds it overruled by kṛṣṇa-kathā.
  • tyakta-udam — having given up drinking water
    Tyakta (abandoned) + uda (water). The vow of prāyopaveśana — fasting unto death. Parīkṣit is showing what is possible only when the medicine is being drunk: even the body's most foundational appetite goes quiet.
  • tvat-mukha-ambhoja-cyutam — emanating from your lotus mouth
    Tvat (your) + mukha (mouth) + ambhoja (lotus) + cyuta (fallen, dropping). Each word of kathā is a drop of nectar falling from a lotus. The source matters: the same word in another mouth is not the same word. Paramparā is the lotus from which the nectar drops.
  • hari-kathā-amṛtam — the nectar of topics concerning Kṛṣṇa
    Hari + kathā + amṛta. Amṛta = the deathless. Hari-kathā is not metaphorically nectar — it is literally what the gods drink for immortality (BG 4.9). The verse insists on the chemistry: hear, and saṁsāric thirst literally cannot persist.
Because of my vow on the verge of death, I have given up even drinking water, yet because I am drinking the nectar of topics about Kṛṣṇa, which is flowing from the lotus mouth of your Lordship, my hunger and thirst, which are extremely difficult to bear, cannot hinder me.
Purport

To prepare to meet death in seven days, Mahārāja Parīkṣit gave up all food and drink. As a human being, he was certainly both hungry and thirsty, and therefore Śukadeva Gosvāmī might have wanted to stop narrating the transcendental topics of Kṛṣṇa; but despite his fast, Mahārāja Parīkṣit was not at all fatigued. “The hunger and thirst from my fast do not disturb me,” he said. “Once when I felt very thirsty, I went to the āśrama of Śamīka Muni to drink water, but the muni did not supply it. I therefore wrapped a dead snake over his shoulder, and that is why I was cursed by the brāhmaṇa boy. Now, however, I am quite fit. I am not at all disturbed by my hunger and thirst.” This indicates that although on the material platform there are disturbances from hunger and thirst, on the spiritual platform there is no such thing as fatigue. The entire world is suffering because of spiritual thirst. Every living being is Brahman, or spirit soul, and needs spiritual food to satisfy his hunger and thirst. Unfortunately, however, the world is completely unaware of the nectar of kṛṣṇa-kathā. The Kṛṣṇa consciousness movement is therefore a boon to philosophers, religionists and people in general. There is certainly a charming attraction in Kṛṣṇa and kṛṣṇa-kathā. Therefore the Absolute Truth is called Kṛṣṇa, the most attractive. The word amṛta is also an important reference to the moon, and the word ambuja means “lotus.” The pleasing moonshine and pleasing fragrance of the lotus combined to bring pleasure to everyone hearing kṛṣṇa-kathā from the mouth of Śukadeva Gosvāmī. As it is said: matir na kṛṣṇe parataḥ svato vā mitho ’bhipadyeta gṛha-vratānām adānta-gobhir viśatāṁ tamisraṁ punaḥ punaś carvita-carvaṇānām “Because of their uncontrolled senses, persons too addicted to materialistic life make progress toward hellish conditions and repeatedly chew that which has already been chewed. Their inclinations toward Kṛṣṇa are never aroused, either by the instructions of others, by their own efforts, or by a combination of both.” ( Bhāg. 7.5.30 ) At the present moment, all of human society is engaged in the business of chewing the chewed ( punaḥ punaś carvita-carvaṇānām ). People are prepared to undergo mṛtyu-saṁsāra-vartmani, taking birth in one form, dying, accepting another form and dying again. To stop this repetition of birth and death, kṛṣṇa-kathā, or Kṛṣṇa consciousness, is absolutely necessary. But unless one hears kṛṣṇa-kathā from a realized soul like Śukadeva Gosvāmī, one cannot relish the nectar of kṛṣṇa-kathā, which puts an end to all material fatigue, and enjoy the blissful life of transcendental existence. In relation to the Kṛṣṇa consciousness movement, we actually see that those who have tasted the nectar of kṛṣṇa-kathā lose all material desires, whereas those who cannot understand Kṛṣṇa or kṛṣṇa-kathā regard the Kṛṣṇa conscious life as “brainwashing” and “mind control.” While the devotees enjoy spiritual bliss, the nondevotees are surprised that the devotees have forgotten material hankerings.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Because of my vow on the verge of death, I have given up even drinking water, yet because I am drinking the nectar of topics about Kṛṣṇa, which is flowing from the lotus mouth of your Lordship, my hunger and thirst, which are extremely difficult to bear, cannot hinder me.
Commentary

" Because you are afflicted by hunger and thirst, you should rest a while." In response to this advice from Sukadeva, Pariksit speaks this verse. "It was due to hunger and thirst, which cause loss of discrimination., that I placed the snake around the neck of the muni. Though I have given up food and water, hunger and thirst are no longer afflicting me. Why? I am drinking the nectar of the pastimes of Hari." The present tense is used because if one stops hearing for an instant, hunger will manifest. The word ambhoja (lotus) suggests the sweetness of the topics of Hari, and its intoxicating effect. Because of this, Pariksit has forgotten his intolerable hunger and thirst. Also by the word amrta there is the suggestion that the lotus mouth, like the moon, drops nectar. The lotus suggests that the mouth of Sukadeva is fragrant like the lotus, and as the moon, it becomes the bestower of joy, destroying the darkness in the hearts of the listeners, and that Pariksit is like the cakora bird longing for the moonlight, being thirsty for the pastimes of Hari. All of this expresses the great attachment of Pariksit for topics of the Lord.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

The penance for my offense again a great brāhmaṇa was giving up my life. I have even given up drinking water. But my life continues because of the nectar of the topics you recite. Otherwise I would already be dead. That is expressed in this verse. The hunger and thirst are now unbearable, not endurable by my will power. The cause was my thirst which made me throw a dead snake around the neck of the sage. Because my thirst arose from lack of water which caused by offense, I have given up drinking water. Kṣut (hunger) also refers to thirst in this verse. But I do not feel any pain from giving up water. Or, though for others it is intolerable, though I am soft from many luxuries I will not avoid giving up water. It cannot create an obstacle in hearing the Lord’s topics. Placing the word na first gives greater emphasis to it. The cause is the nectar of the topics of the Lord, Hari, who takes away all suffering. The topics make me forget the material world because of their intoxicating effect and sweetness. I drink, or relish with great attachment, that nectar. Those topics are most astonishing for they have destroyed my hunger and thirst. The special nature of the nectar is described. I drink it without cessation (present tense is used). It is not possible to stop drinking it. Or, after beginning to drink I feel as if I have just started even now. And it flows from your mouth, beautiful as a lotus in form, a vessel filled with great pleasure. This indicates the special quality of Śukadeva’s speaking. Great sweetness arises in the topics of the Lord spoken by the great devotee. Or the unusual nature of the lotus is shown by having nectar flow from it. As nectar flows from the lotus, the topics of the Lord flow from Śukadeva’s mouth. The statement shows the great attachment of Parīkṣit for the topics.

Personal Guidance
Hunger and thirst belong to the body; when the soul drinks kṛṣṇa-kathā from a realized speaker, even bodily distress cannot disturb you.
Practical Applications
  • Set aside one fast-from-distraction window today and fill it with hearing kṛṣṇa-kathā from a bona fide source rather than checking your phone.
  • Notice when bodily urges (hunger, restlessness, fatigue) try to pull you away from sādhana, and stay seated for five more minutes of śravaṇam before yielding.
  • Receive hari-kathā today as a disciple from a guru, not as a consumer of content — sit lower, listen with folded hands, and ask one sincere question.
Reflection Questions
  • When my body's hunger, thirst, fatigue, or restlessness flares up, do I treat it as proof that hari-kathā must wait — or do I test whether deeper absorption in Kṛṣṇa's topics actually quiets the demand, the way Parīkṣit Mahārāja's fast became weightless in Śukadeva's presence?
  • Parīkṣit had seven days; what 'seven days' am I pretending I don't have — what finite window in this life am I filling with carvita-carvaṇa (chewing the chewed) instead of with the nectar from a realized soul's lips?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • During your next exam-week or late-night study session, deliberately swap one 20-minute scroll/Netflix break for 20 minutes of hearing Bhāgavatam or a Prabhupāda lecture — and notice whether your hunger for the phone actually quiets, the way Parīkṣit's hunger did.
  • Pick the one friend-group conversation you keep replaying in your head (the crush, the rejection, the comparison) — Prabhupāda calls this 'chewing the chewed.' This week, every time it starts replaying, redirect by quietly reciting one line of kṛṣṇa-kathā you have memorized.
  • Before your next big social-media post or DM, ask: am I doing this because I am spiritually thirsty and looking for likes to fill it? Try one full day where, when that thirst rises, you open the Bhāgavatam app instead of Instagram — and journal what changed.
householder
  • Institute a fifteen-minute family kṛṣṇa-kathā at dinner — read one verse aloud before anyone eats. Watch over a month whether your children's complaints about food, and your own fatigue from the workday, soften the way Parīkṣit's hunger softened.
  • When a financial fear or career anxiety wakes you at 3 a.m. — the householder's real hunger and thirst — do not reach for the phone or the fridge. Hear or recite kṛṣṇa-kathā for ten minutes and test, honestly, whether the gnawing is fed by something other than the next promotion.
  • Before your next major family decision (school, house, job, in-law conflict), spend one evening hearing kṛṣṇa-kathā with your spouse before discussing it — not to manufacture agreement, but to first feed the soul so the decision is not driven by ahaṅkāra and viṣaya.
senior
  • Reorganize your daily schedule so that hari-kathā occupies the hours you used to give to work — mornings, not the leftover tired evenings. Parīkṣit knew his time was short; treat your remaining years with the same precision.
  • Identify one bodily comfort you have been quietly clinging to — a particular food, a temperature, a chair, a routine — and loosen it deliberately this month, using kṛṣṇa-kathā as the replacement nourishment, as a small rehearsal for the larger letting-go ahead.
  • Find one younger person — grandchild, neighbor, junior colleague — and become for them what Śukadeva was for Parīkṣit: a steady source of kṛṣṇa-kathā. Your legacy is not your estate or your stories; it is whether someone tasted the nectar through you.
Common Pitfalls
  • Romanticizing the austerity (giving up food and water) as the cause of Parīkṣit's steadiness, when the verse plainly states the cause is drinking hari-kathāmṛta — imitating his tapas without his hearing produces only depletion, not transcendence.
  • Trying to manufacture this same relish from books, recordings, or one's own speculation alone, ignoring Prabhupāda's point that the nectar must flow from the lotus mouth of a realized soul like Śukadeva — bypassing guru-bhakti and then wondering why kṛṣṇa-kathā feels dry.
  • Using 'spiritual topics make me forget hunger' as a license to neglect the body's legitimate needs in ordinary life — Parīkṣit was preparing for death in seven days, not modeling a daily routine; confusing his exceptional vow with sādhana for a householder leads to bravado, not bhakti.
Discussion Prompts
  • Parīkṣit says hunger and thirst cannot disturb him while he drinks kṛṣṇa-kathā. Share with your partner: when has hari-kathā actually displaced a bodily craving in your life — and when have you only pretended it did? Be honest about the difference.
  • Prabhupāda calls modern life 'chewing the chewed' (punaḥ punaś carvita-carvaṇānām). In small groups, name one specific 'chewed' pleasure you keep returning to, and debate: is the answer to renounce it, or to crowd it out with stronger nectar? Where do members disagree?
  • Parīkṣit had only seven days left. If you knew you had seven days, would you spend them hearing kṛṣṇa-kathā — or with family, unfinished work, bucket-list items? Surface the real disagreement in your group: is Parīkṣit's choice universal sva-dharma, or specific to his stage and realization?
Role Play

You are a hospital chaplain visiting a 58-year-old devotee just diagnosed with a terminal illness, given roughly six months. Her adult children are pressuring her to 'fight,' pursue every experimental treatment, and 'stay positive.' She asks you privately whether it would be selfish to instead spend her remaining time hearing Bhāgavatam daily with a sādhu, as Parīkṣit did — eating less, traveling less, withdrawing from family events. One student plays the devotee, one plays a skeptical adult child who walks in mid-conversation, one plays the chaplain drawing on SB 10.1.13. Run it for ten minutes, then switch roles. Afterward, discuss: where did 'real' compassion (serving the soul) and 'material' compassion (easing bodily distress) collide?

Objections & Responses
  • This is just spiritual bypassing dressed up in Sanskrit. Parīkṣit was literally starving himself to death and calling it bliss — that's eating-disorder logic, not transcendence. A healthy person eats, drinks, and then hears scripture.
    Parīkṣit was not denying the body out of self-hatred or escape; he was a king who had ruled, fathered children, fought wars, and was now facing a curse-fixed death in seven days. His fast was a deliberate, stage-appropriate tapas at the end of life, witnessed and approved by Śukadeva Gosvāmī himself. Prabhupāda is careful in the purport: on the material platform hunger and thirst do disturb us — that is normal. The verse does not instruct healthy gṛhasthas to stop eating. It shows that kṛṣṇa-kathā has real, substantive nourishing power for the soul — power most of us have never tested because we have never gone even one meal hungry while hearing. The cure for bypassing is not to dismiss the verse but to honor varṇāśrama: eat as your āśrama requires, and then test, in small honest ways, whether hari-kathā actually feeds something food cannot reach.
  • Calling Kṛṣṇa consciousness a 'boon to philosophers and religionists' sounds like the same exclusivism every religion uses. Why is this kathā uniquely the nectar? Plenty of people find peace in Sufi poetry, Zen, therapy, or nature — and they're not 'chewing the chewed.'
    The Gauḍīya claim is specific, not generic. Kṛṣṇa is the Supreme Personality of Godhead — not a symbol, not 'the Self,' not one face of a formless Absolute — and kṛṣṇa-kathā is nectar because it is direct contact with Him through sound, coming through paramparā from a realized soul like Śukadeva. Other paths can produce genuine sattva, calm, even ethical refinement; we honor that. But sattva is not the same as bhakti, and peace is not the same as prema. The test the verse offers is empirical, not dogmatic: does the practice actually loosen viṣaya — the grip of sense objects — or does it just reframe the same hankerings in nicer language? Parīkṣit's hunger genuinely lost its bite. That is the bar. Anyone is welcome to bring their own path to that bar; we are simply reporting what works for us, and inviting them to taste.
Analogy

Parīkṣit, fasting in body but feasting on Kṛṣṇa-kathā, is like a man who has set down a cup of stale water because someone just handed him a glass from a flowing river. The thirst doesn't fight him; it simply moves to its true object. Hunger and thirst, the verse says, cannot interrupt — not because they are suppressed, but because they have been outclassed.

Stories
Haridāsa Ṭhākura's three-lakh names

Haridāsa Ṭhākura, the nāmācārya of the Caitanya line, chanted three lakhs (300,000) of holy names daily — sitting in a cave during plague, in a sandbox where a snake was discovered (and which he refused to leave), through Muslim persecution that beat him through twenty-two market-places.

His biographers report that he ate only after completing his rounds; many days, by his own admission, he forgot. Body's needs did not assert themselves when the tongue was tasting the name. Parīkṣit's hunger-and-thirst-suspended state is not unique — it is what hari-kathā-amṛtam does to anyone who actually drinks it.
