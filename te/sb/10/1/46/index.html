Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.1.46

Important Words
  • sāmabhiḥ — by attempts to pacify him
    Instrumental plural of sāma — the first of the four diplomatic methods (sāma, dāna, bheda, daṇḍa). Vasudeva employed sāma in verses 37-45. The verse confirms his method, then registers its failure.
  • bhedaiḥ — by moral instructions that one should not be cruel to anyone else
    Bhedaiḥ — instrumental plural of bheda, 'distinction, division.' The second diplomatic method: pointing out distinctions (this is a sister, this is the wedding hour, this is dharma). Vasudeva used bheda in verses 44-45. Also failed.
  • dāruṇaḥ — he who was the most fiercely cruel
    Dāruṇa — fierce, hard like wood (dāru). The verse names Kaṁsa as wood — unmoved by all four winds of persuasion. The Bhāgavata's natural taxonomy: some hearts are dāruṇa, and the only response to dāru is fire (śaraṇa to the Lord, who acts when persuasion does not).
  • puruṣa-adān — the Rākṣasas, man-eaters
    Puruṣa (man) + adān (eaters). Rākṣasic kinship — Kaṁsa follows in their footsteps. Bhāgavata anthropology distinguishes deva, manuṣya, and rākṣasa modes; Kaṁsa, by birth in a human dynasty, has fallen into rākṣasa-mode by choice.
Śukadeva Gosvāmī continued: O best of the Kuru dynasty, Kaṁsa was fiercely cruel and was actually a follower of the Rākṣasas. Therefore he could be neither pacified nor terrified by the good instructions given by Vasudeva. He did not care about the results of sinful activities, either in this life or in the next.
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Śukadeva Gosvāmī continued: O best of the Kuru dynasty, Kaṁsa was fiercely cruel and was actually a follower of the Rākṣasas. Therefore he could be neither pacified nor terrified by the good instructions given by Vasudeva. He did not care about the results of sinful activities, either in this life or in the next.
Commentary

Though being instructed by Vasudeva (bodhyamano’pi) with pacifying and fear instilling words, Kamsa, who keeps company with raksasas (purusadan) did not give up his intent to kill her.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Kaṁsa, surrounded by Aghāsura and others Rākṣasas, has adopted their mentality. For such persons sāma is useless. He addresses Parīkṣit as the head of the Kurus, since he was disappointed that Kaṁsa did not give up his intentions through sāma.

Personal Guidance
When the heart is hardened by rākṣasic conduct, even Vasudeva's gentle counsel cannot land — guard your own hearing of Kṛṣṇa's word before it calcifies.
Practical Applications
  • Notice one piece of sound counsel you've recently brushed aside, and act on it today before the refusal hardens into a habit.
  • Speak to one person under your protection (child, junior, dependent) with sāma — gentle, reasoned words — instead of force or threat.
  • Refuse the rākṣasic logic that 'consequences in this life or the next don't matter' — name one act you will not do today precisely because of its karmic weight.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my own life have I, like Kaṁsa, hardened myself against good counsel from someone who genuinely wishes me well — and what rākṣasa-like attachment am I protecting by refusing to listen?
  • When I weigh an action, do I actually consider its consequences in this life and the next, or have I quietly adopted the puruṣāda outlook that 'results don't matter' so long as I get what I want now?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Identify the one person in your friend group or DMs you keep trying to 'explain yourself' to — about your practice, your choices, your boundaries — who only mocks or twists it. This week, stop explaining. Not in anger; in clarity. Vasudeva spoke once, fully, and then stopped negotiating.
  • Audit your social media feed for accounts that are openly puruṣāda in flavor — contemptuous of the vulnerable, proud of cruelty, indifferent to consequence. Notice that no amount of your 'engagement' in the comments will reform them; it only feeds the algorithm. Unfollow today, and use that reclaimed attention for one round of japa or one chapter of reading.
  • When peer pressure shows up — at a party, in a group chat, at a hostel — remember that Vasudeva did not match Kaṁsa's energy. He stayed in his own dharma even at the edge of a sword. Practice this in low stakes now: hold your stance once, plainly, without escalation and without retreat, so the muscle is built for higher stakes later.
householder
  • Identify the relationship in your extended family or workplace where you have been over-functioning with sāma and bheda — endlessly explaining, warning, smoothing — to someone whose pattern has not changed in years. Decide, with your spouse, what one tactic you will retire this month. Redirect the freed energy into your children's sādhana and your own.
  • In raising children, distinguish the moments that call for instruction (a child genuinely confused, asking, capable of receiving) from moments that call for protective structure (a child or teen in a Kaṁsa-adjacent influence who cannot currently hear words). For the second, act like Vasudeva eventually had to act for Kṛṣṇa — change the environment, do not just keep talking.
  • Money and career: notice where you keep trying to reason with a boss, client, or business partner who is anuvrataḥ to a different value system — extraction, image, fear. Stop expecting them to be persuaded by your dharmic framing. Either restructure the arrangement or exit it; offer the result to Kṛṣṇa each evening rather than bargaining with the puruṣāda each morning.
senior
  • Look back at the relationships in which you spent decades trying to talk someone into seeing — a sibling, a child, an old friend. Without bitterness, formally release the project of persuading them. Pray for them by name daily; stop arguing with them in your head. That released energy is precisely what is needed for the inward turn of vānaprastha-bhāva.
  • In mentoring younger devotees or family, do not become the elder who keeps offering sāma and bheda to a generation that has not asked. Speak fully when invited; otherwise, let your steadiness — your dhīra presence, your unhurried japa, your refusal to panic — do the teaching that words cannot.
  • Mortality clarifies the verse's last line: he did not care about results in this life or the next. You do. Use the time remaining to settle every account where you have been negotiating with a Kaṁsa-figure — internal or external — and turn the freed attention toward Kṛṣṇa's name, so that when the body falls, the mind is not still mid-argument with someone who was never going to listen.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading this verse as being only about 'other people like Kaṁsa' — villains out there — and missing the small Kaṁsa inside oneself that resists sāma (gentle reasoning) and bheda (sober warning) from parents, guru, or scripture.
  • Confusing fearlessness (abhaya) with Kaṁsa's reckless disregard for sinful reaction; true abhaya rests on surrender to Kṛṣṇa, not on numbness to karmic consequence.
  • Misapplying 'compassion' by continuing to placate or appease a hardened person (a Kaṁsa-figure in one's life) with sāma-bheda tactics long past the point Vasudeva himself stopped — failing to recognize when protection (rakṣā) of the innocent must take precedence over negotiation with the cruel.
Discussion Prompts
  • Vasudeva used sāma (gentle persuasion) and bheda (pointing out distinctions and consequences) with Kaṁsa, and both failed. Share a relationship in your life where you have been doing the same — pleading and warning a person who is structurally incapable of hearing it. What is the honest cost to you of continuing to try? Disagree with each other: when is persistence devotion, and when is it ahaṅkāra dressed as daya?
  • The verse calls Kaṁsa puruṣādān anuvrataḥ — a follower of the man-eaters, indifferent to consequences in this life or the next. Do you actually believe people like this exist today, or is it more comfortable to believe everyone is reachable with the right words? Push each other: name a public figure or someone you know whom you suspect is genuinely in this category, and defend the claim.
  • Vasudeva is trying to save his sons by reasoning with a tyrant. He is mixing real compassion (for the souls about to be killed) with material strategy (talking Kaṁsa down). Where in your own life are you confusing these two — using spiritual language to negotiate a material outcome you want? Be specific with your partner; do not let each other off with abstractions.
Role Play

Two students. Student A plays a parent whose adult child has fallen in with a circle that is openly cruel — mocking the vulnerable, contemptuous of dharma, indifferent to consequences. Student A has tried kindness, has tried laying out the consequences, has tried bringing in respected elders. Nothing lands. Student A now comes to Student B, a slightly older godbrother/godsister, and asks: 'Do I keep trying? Am I giving up on my own child if I stop?' Student B must respond from this verse — without flattening the pain, without offering cheap detachment, and without pretending that more sāma and bheda will eventually work on someone who is anuvrataḥ to a different current. Run it twice, swap roles. Then debrief: where did each 'B' slip into either sentimental hope or cold dismissal, and what would the dhīra-bhāva of Vasudeva — who kept his vow but did not keep negotiating — actually look like?

Objections & Responses
  • This verse looks like it justifies giving up on people. 'He's a Rākṣasa, nothing works, move on.' That sounds like the opposite of compassion — it sounds like writing people off, which is exactly what abusive families and cults do.
    Notice what Vasudeva actually does and does not do. He does not stop loving his sons; he does not stop protecting them; he does not stop acting. What he stops is the specific tactic of verbal persuasion with a person who has shown, in word and deed, that he is anuvrataḥ — vowed — to a different stream. Real daya is serving the soul, which sometimes means the painful clarity of seeing that this particular person, in this particular moment, is not in a condition to receive what you are offering. The Gauḍīya line never says abandon the jīva — Mahāprabhu wept for the most fallen. It says: stop confusing the strategy of sweet talk with the substance of compassion. Keep praying for the person; stop bargaining with the Rākṣasa.
  • Calling someone a Rākṣasa or a 'follower of the man-eaters' is exactly the kind of dehumanizing language that leads to violence. Modern ethics has moved past this — every person has dignity and is reachable.
    The verse is not licensing contempt; it is naming a category honestly. Śāstra distinguishes daiva and āsura propensities (BG 16) not to dehumanize anyone but to help us see clearly what we are dealing with — including in ourselves. The modern axiom that 'everyone is reachable with enough empathy' is not actually a finding; it is a hope, and it has a cost: it leaves the vulnerable (Vasudeva's unborn children, Devakī in the chariot) exposed because we will not name the predator in front of us. Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavism holds both ends of the rope — every soul is part-and-parcel of Kṛṣṇa and ultimately reclaimable, and right now this person is acting as a puruṣāda and must be treated accordingly. Refusing the second truth in the name of the first is not compassion; it is cowardice with good branding.
Analogy

Kaṁsa, addressed with every dharmic appeal Vasudeva could marshal, remains unmoved — like a wall that has been spoken to with reason, with prudence, with tenderness, and that responds to none of them. The Bhāgavata names him here as following puruṣa-adān, the man-eaters' line. The verse closes Vasudeva's argument: persuasion has been honored to its limit; the asura's nature is its own ceiling.
