Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.1.43

జ్యోతిర్యథైవోదకపార్థివేష్వద:సమీరవేగానుగతం విభావ్యతే । ఏవం స్వమాయారచితేష్వసౌ పుమాన్గుణేషు రాగానుగతో విముహ్యతి ॥ ౪౩ ॥

Important Words
  • jyotiḥ — the luminaries in the sky
    From jyut, 'to shine.' Same root as jyotis (light). The soul is its own jyotis — self-luminous (BG 13.18, jyotiṣām api taj jyotiḥ). The verse contrasts the soul's true jyotiḥ-nature with the distorted shapes the agitated mind makes of him.
  • sva-māyā-raciteṣu — in the situation created by one's mental concoctions
    Sva (own) + māyā (illusion) + racita (constructed). 'In the constructions made by one's own māyā.' The verse points to a personal māyā — the conditioned soul's own mental construction-set, distinct from the cosmic māyā in verse 42.
  • rāga-anugataḥ — according to his attachment
    Rāga (attachment, coloring) + anugata (following). The shapes the soul takes are determined by his rāga. Gauḍīya: this same rāga, redirected to Kṛṣṇa, becomes rāgānuga-bhakti — the topmost bhakti is rāga turned toward its rightful object.
  • vimuhyati — becomes bewildered by identification
    Vi + muh, 'thoroughly deluded.' From the same root as moha, mohini, mūḍha. The verse names the operative result: vimuhyati — bewilderment of identity. The bewilderment is curable; bhakti is its medicine.
When the luminaries in the sky, such as the moon, the sun and the stars, are reflected in liquids like oil or water, they appear to be of different shapes — sometimes round, sometimes long, and so on — because of the movements of the wind. Similarly, when the living entity, the soul, is absorbed in materialistic thoughts, he accepts various manifestations as his own identity because of ignorance. In other words, one is bewildered by mental concoctions because of agitation from the material modes of nature.
Purport

This verse gives a very good example by which to understand the different positions of the eternal spiritual soul in the material world and how the soul takes on different bodies ( dehāntara-prāptiḥ ). The moon is stationary and is one, but when it is reflected in water or oil, it appears to take different shapes because of the movements of the wind. Similarly, the soul is the eternal servant of Kṛṣṇa, the Supreme Personality of Godhead, but when put into the material modes of nature, it takes different bodies, sometimes as a demigod, sometimes a man, a dog, a tree and so on. By the influence of māyā, the illusory potency of the Supreme Personality of Godhead, the living entity thinks that he is this person, that person, American, Indian, cat, dog, tree or whatever. This is called māyā. When one is freed from this bewilderment and understands that the soul does not belong to any shape of this material world, one is situated on the spiritual platform ( brahma-bhūta ). This realization is sometimes explained as nirākāra, or formlessness. This formlessness, however, does not mean that the soul has no form. The soul has form, but the external, agitating form he has acquired because of material contamination is false. Similarly, God is also described as nirākāra, which means that God has no material form but is sac-cid-ānanda-vigraha. The living entity is part and parcel of the supreme sac-cid-ānanda-vigraha, but his material forms are temporary, or illusory. Both the living entity and the Supreme Lord have original, spiritual forms ( sac-cid-ānanda-vigraha ), but the Lord, the Supreme, does not change His form. The Lord appears as He is, whereas the living entity appears because material nature forces him to accept different forms. When the living entity receives these different forms, he identifies with them, and not with his original, spiritual form. As soon as the living entity returns to his original, spiritual form and understanding, he immediately surrenders to the supreme form, the Personality of Godhead. This is explained in Bhagavad-gītā (7.19) . Bahūnāṁ janmanām ante jñānavān māṁ prapadyate. When the living entity, after many, many births in different forms, returns to his original form of Kṛṣṇa consciousness, he immediately surrenders unto the lotus feet of the supreme form, Kṛṣṇa. This is liberation. As the Lord says in Bhagavad-gītā (18.54) : brahma-bhūtaḥ prasannātmā na śocati na kāṅkṣati samaḥ sarveṣu bhūteṣu mad-bhaktiṁ labhate parām “One who is thus transcendentally situated at once realizes the Supreme Brahman and becomes fully joyful. He never laments nor desires to have anything; he is equally disposed to every living entity. In that state he attains pure devotional service unto Me.” Surrender unto the supreme form is the result of bhakti. This bhakti, or understanding of one’s own position, is the complete liberation. As long as one is under an impersonal understanding of the Absolute Truth, he is not in pure knowledge, but must still struggle for pure knowledge. Kleśo ’dhikataras teṣām avyaktāsakta-cetasām ( Bg. 12.5 ). Although one may be spiritually advanced, if one is attached to the impersonal feature of the Absolute Truth one must still work very hard, as indicated by the words kleśo ’dhikataraḥ, which mean “greater suffering.” A devotee, however, easily attains his original position as a spiritual form and understands the Supreme Personality of Godhead in His original form. Kṛṣṇa Himself explains the forms of the living entities in the Second Chapter of Bhagavad-gītā, where He clearly says to Arjuna that He, Arjuna and all other living entities, who were previously in their original forms, are separate individual identities. They were individuals in the past, they are now situated in individuality, and in the future they will all continue to maintain their individual forms. The only difference is that the conditioned living entity appears in various material forms, whereas Kṛṣṇa appears in His original, spiritual form. Unfortunately, those who are not advanced in spiritual knowledge think that Kṛṣṇa is like one of them and that His form is like their material forms. Avajānanti māṁ mūḍhā mānuṣīṁ tanum āśritam ( Bg. 9.11 ). Kṛṣṇa is never puffed up by material knowledge and is therefore called acyuta, whereas the living entities fall down and are agitated by material nature. This is the difference between the Supreme Lord and the living entities. In this connection it is to be noted that Vasudeva, who was situated in a transcendental position, advised Kaṁsa not to commit further sinful activities. Kaṁsa, a representative of the demons, was always ready to kill Kṛṣṇa, or God, whereas Vasudeva represents a transcendentally situated person to whom Kṛṣṇa is born (Vāsudeva is the son of Vasudeva). Vasudeva wanted his brother-in-law Kaṁsa to refrain from the sinful act of killing his sister, since the result of being agitated by material nature would be that Kaṁsa would have to accept a body in which to suffer again and again. Elsewhere in Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam (5.5.4) , Ṛṣabhadeva also says: na sādhu manye yata ātmano ’yam asann api kleśada āsa dehaḥ As long as the living entity is entangled in the fruitive activities of so-called happiness and distress, he will receive a particular type of body in which to endure the three kinds of suffering due to material nature ( tri-tāpa-yantraṇā ). An intelligent person, therefore, must free himself from the influence of the three modes of material nature and revive his original, spiritual body by engaging in the service of the Supreme Person, Kṛṣṇa. As long as one is materially attached, one must accept the process of birth, death, old age and disease. One is therefore advised that an intelligent person, instead of being entangled in so-called good and bad fruitive activities, should engage his life in advancing in Kṛṣṇa consciousness so that instead of accepting another material body ( tyaktvā dehaṁ punar janma naiti ), he will return home, back to Godhead.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
When the luminaries in the sky, such as the moon, the sun and the stars, are reflected in liquids like oil or water, they appear to be of different shapes—sometimes round, sometimes long, and so on—because of the movements of the wind. Similarly, when the living entity, the soul, is absorbed in materialistic thoughts, he accepts various manifestations as his own identity because of ignorance. In other words, one is bewildered by mental concoctions because of agitation from the material modes of nature.
Commentary

Now he gives an example to show how the soul, due to association with mind, attains the same destination. Jyotih refers to the light of the sun or moon. Udakesu refers to water, oil or ghee. On the surface of the water the image of the sun is reflected but it changes shape as the wind blows the surface and produces ripples. In the bodies (gunesu) created by the Lord’s maya (svamaya racitesu), or by ones own ignorance, the jiva (puman), following the mind which is filled with desires for enjoyment (raganugata), becomes bewildered and takes up the same desire for enjoyment. By killing Devaki you will obstruct the souls desire for getting enjoyment. Even if you die, you will enjoy in the next body, because you will attain a good body since your good karmas must bear result. And killing her will make you endure suffering. Your death announced in the sky by the future son of Devaki is unavoidable, as it is under the force of karma. Therefore it is better to accept that death. All you can do is have Markandeya perform some ceremonies as preventative measures.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

This absorption in bodies is filled with ignorance. Adaḥ means “situated in the sky without trembling.” Sva-māyā means the Lord’s māyā. The jīva is bewildered by bodies made of the Lord’s māyā.

Śrīdhara Svāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

“Let us not speak of ordinary human bodies. Is not the body of a king very rare?” Though the luminaries are situated in the sky without trembling, they appear to move in their reflections in the water. The jīva similarly becomes bewildered in bodies made by his ignorance (svamāyā) or the Lord’s māyā present in him. The actions of these bodies is illusory. Or the previous verse described the jīva going from body to body. By that, one attains a king’s body. At the time of death by absorption of the mind impelled by karmas about to fructify, with a desire to be a king, one attains a king’s body. Maraṇe yā matiḥ sā gatiḥ: one attains what one thinks of at death. “Let us accept that in another birth one becomes a king. But one must make effort to protect the body in the womb and when the child is small in order that the person may enjoy.” Just as the reflection of the planets in the water follow the movements of the wind on the water and seems to tremble, the jīva, being attached to objects of enjoyment (guṇeṣu), becomes completely bewildered and thinks one object is enjoyable and another is not. If one does not have attachment in childhood or any age, one will not suffer from enjoyment of objects, just as one will be happy by enjoying the objects, by having attachment. How can killing the child solve the problem?

Concepts
Bhava
Personal Guidance
Just as the moon's reflection wobbles in wind-stirred water, the soul's true form is distorted by the guṇas — until bhakti restores you to Kṛṣṇa's service.
Practical Applications
  • Notice one identity-label you wore today (professional role, nationality, body-type) and remind yourself: that is the wobble on the water, not the moon.
  • When agitation arises from rajas or tamas, pause and chant Kṛṣṇa's name once with attention — let the wind on the water still.
  • Refuse to settle for an impersonal 'inner stillness' as the goal; aim today's sādhana explicitly at surrender to Kṛṣṇa, the supreme sac-cid-ānanda-vigraha.
Reflection Questions
  • Which 'reflected shape' — a role, nationality, profession, or relationship label — have I been mistaking for my actual self, and what wind of circumstance keeps that reflection moving?
  • When the agitation of the guṇas stirs my self-image today, what would it look like to recognize myself instead as the eternal servant of Kṛṣṇa rather than as the rippling reflection?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Open your phone right now and look at the last three things you posted or were tagged in. Ask: which 'shape' of me was being reflected there — the funny one, the achiever, the aesthetic one? Notice that none of those shapes is you; they are wind on water. Don't delete them — just stop believing them.
  • Before your next exam or interview, write at the top of your notes: 'The result will reshape my reflection, not my soul.' Then prepare twice as hard, because freedom from the fruit is what frees you to actually focus on the work.
  • Pick one friend group where you perform a version of yourself you don't fully recognize. This week, in one small moment, do not perform. Let the reflection wobble. See whether the soul underneath is still there — it will be.
householder
  • Tonight, name out loud (to yourself or your spouse) the three identities you most defend at home — 'the provider,' 'the good parent,' 'the one who holds it together.' Recognize each as a wind-shape on the same water. Then continue providing, parenting, and holding — but offer the doing to Kṛṣṇa, not to the identity.
  • In the next big family financial or career decision, ask: am I choosing this to protect the reflection (status, image, what relatives will say) or to actually serve the souls in my care? If it is the first, you are the wind agitating your own water. Re-decide.
  • With your children, drop one comparison this week — to a cousin, a classmate, a 'better' family. Each comparison teaches them they ARE their reflection. Replace it once with a moment of recognizing the eternal soul in them: a real conversation, undivided attention, a verse read together.
senior
  • Make a written list of the bodies you have already 'transitioned through' in this single life — the child, the student, the young professional, the parent of small children. Feel the continuity of the same observer behind all of them. The next transition (frailty, death) is one more shape on the same water.
  • Identify one attachment — a possession, a position in the family, a grievance you have nursed for decades — and begin to loosen it deliberately this month. Not by force, but by recognizing it as a reflection you have been defending. Tell one person you are doing this, so it becomes real.
  • Mentor one younger person not by giving them your résumé of accomplishments (that is the reflection talking) but by transmitting what has actually steadied you across all the changing forms. If sādhana and Kṛṣṇa-bhakti are what steadied you, say so plainly. This is the legacy that survives the body.
Common Pitfalls
  • Sliding into impersonalism — using this verse to conclude 'I am formless consciousness' rather than 'I am an eternal spiritual person, part and parcel of Kṛṣṇa, who has falsely identified with material forms.'
  • Treating the teaching as a reason to neglect bodily and social responsibilities ('the body is just a reflection') instead of using it to perform sva-dharma without false identification.
  • Intellectually agreeing that identities like 'American,' 'Indian,' 'professional,' or 'parent' are illusory while continuing to react emotionally — with pride, fear, or anger — every time those identities are touched.
Discussion Prompts
  • The verse compares the soul to the moon's reflection distorted by wind. Share with your partner: which 'wind' (which mode — sattva, rajas, or tamas) most distorts your self-image right now, and how can you tell it is a distortion and not the real you? Disagree honestly if your partner's diagnosis sounds too flattering or too harsh.
  • Prabhupāda says the impersonalist conclusion of nirākāra is incomplete — the soul has a form, and so does Kṛṣṇa (sac-cid-ānanda-vigraha). In your group, argue the case: is it easier or harder for a modern person to surrender to a personal Kṛṣṇa than to an abstract 'consciousness'? Where do you each actually stand, not where you think you should stand?
  • Vasudeva tried to dissuade Kaṁsa from sin by appealing to the logic of this verse — that bewilderment by the modes leads to repeated suffering. Recall a time you tried to warn someone (or were warned) using a spiritual argument. Did it work? Why or why not? What does this verse suggest about the limits of such warnings?
Role Play

Two students play a pair of close friends meeting for coffee after a long gap. Friend A has just lost a major job/role and is in crisis: 'I don't know who I am anymore — I was the [job title], and now I'm nobody.' Friend B is a Gauḍīya practitioner who has been studying SB 10.1.43. Friend B must respond — without quoting Sanskrit or sounding preachy — using only the insight that the identity Friend A is mourning is a wind-blown reflection, not the self. The class then critiques: did Friend B offer real compassion (serving the soul) or material compassion (just soothing the bodily ego)? Swap roles and try again.

Objections & Responses
  • This sounds like classic dissociation dressed up as spirituality — 'you are not your body, not your job, not your relationships.' Isn't this exactly the kind of thinking that lets people avoid responsibility for their actual lives, families, and feelings?
    The verse is not asking you to abandon your post — Vasudeva is in the middle of a marriage, a brother-in-law crisis, and political danger when this teaching unfolds, and he does not run. Knowing the reflection is not the moon doesn't mean you stop tending the water; it means you stop mistaking ripples for catastrophe. A Gauḍīya teacher would say: do your duty harder, not less, but no longer believe that the bodily designation is your essence. Real responsibility begins when you stop performing for the reflection and start serving Kṛṣṇa through the role He has placed you in.
  • Modern psychology and neuroscience say the self IS constructed by the brain and the body — there is no 'eternal soul' separate from the modes. Why should I accept a 16th-century Indian metaphysics over current science?
    Notice the verse's actual claim — it does not deny that the constructed self exists; it says that constructed self is real the way a reflection is real, agitated by causes you can observe (the wind, the modes). Science describes the wind and the water beautifully. It is silent on the moon — on why there is an experiencer at all, why anything is lit up from within. Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavism does not ask you to throw out the data; it asks you to test, in your own contemplative experience, whether the witness of your changing identities is itself one more changing identity, or something steadier. Try it for a week of honest sādhana before deciding the question is closed.
Analogy

When the moon, sun, and stars are reflected in oil or water, they appear distorted by the wind moving the surface. Similarly, the soul, identifying with the agitated mind, sees himself as a thousand fluctuating shapes — angry, lustful, brave, defeated — and takes each one for his actual identity. The verse's diagnosis: the soul is not the wavering reflection; he is the unchanging luminary. The mode-driven mind is just the rippling water.
