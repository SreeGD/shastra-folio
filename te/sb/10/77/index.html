    Important Words
    • upaspṛśya — touching (water)
      Ritual ācamana — the warrior purifies himself before reentering battle. Kṛṣṇa's son enacts the brāhmaṇical discipline even within a kṣatriya-līlā, modeling that no act, even war, is done outside of dharmic remembrance.
    • daṁśitaḥ — fastening His armor
      From √daṁś (to bite, fasten). The varman of a warrior — emblem of the spiritual armoring (kavaca) of nāma and smaraṇa that the devotee fastens before facing māyā's onslaught.
    • dhṛta-kārmukaḥ — having taken up His bow
      Compound: dhṛta (held) + kārmuka (bow). Pradyumna inherits not only His father Kṛṣṇa's beauty but the readiness to wield weapons in protective līlā — the Yadu line never lays down arms in the middle of an unfinished duty.
    • sārathim — to His driver
      The sārathi motif of the Bhāgavatam — Kṛṣṇa Himself is Pārtha-sārathi for Arjuna; here Pradyumna commands his own driver. The driver is the buddhi that must be told plainly where the ātmā wishes to fight.
    Śukadeva Gosvāmī said: After refreshing Himself with water, putting on His armor and picking up His bow, Lord Pradyumna told His driver, “Take Me back to where the hero Dyumān is standing.”
    Purport

    Pradyumna was eager to rectify the discrepancy of His having left the battlefield when His chariot driver carried Him away unconscious.

    — A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
    Gauḍīya Ācāryas
    Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
    Translation
    Śukadeva Gosvāmī said: After refreshing Himself with water, putting on His armor and picking up His bow, Lord Pradyumna told His driver, "Take Me back to where the hero Dyumān is standing."
    Commentary

    In the seventy seventh chapter, Krsna returns to Dvaraka from Indraprastha, and quickly kills Salva riding in his magical vehicle. In order to correct the fault of fleeing from the battlefield incurred by his charioteer doing his duty of protecting him, Pradyumna, skilful in ksatriya dharma, doing acamana, put on his armor.

    Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
    Commentary

    On version has tūpaspṛśya. The word tu indicates a change of topic. The charioteer had taken off his armor in order to revive him. Now he put on his armor (daṁśitaḥ) and took up his bow. This shows his great enthusiasm to fight. The reason for going back was that there was a hero there (vīrasya) waiting to fight.

    Important Words
    • rukmiṇī-sutaḥ — the son of Rukmiṇī
      Compound: rukmiṇī (Kṛṣṇa's first queen, Lakṣmī's expansion) + suta (son). Pradyumna is identified by His mother — Gauḍīyas read this as the standard rasic naming pattern of Vraja and Dvārakā: the son is His mother's, before he is anyone's general.
    • smayan — smiling
      Present participle of √smi. The Yadu warrior's smile in battle — the same mood as Kṛṣṇa's smile while killing Kaṁsa. Anger never becomes ugliness in those who fight for Bhagavān; the smile is the index of being unshaken.
    • nārācaiḥ — with nārāca arrows
      Iron-shafted arrows used by kṣatriyas in serious combat. The exactness of "eight" — one for each of Dyumān's vital points (vss. 3) — shows that even the Lord's son does not waste a shaft; every arrow lands by knowledge, not by spray.
    • pratyavidhyāt — He counter-pierced
      Prati (back) + ava + √vyadh (to pierce). The grammar itself names the reply — Pradyumna does not initiate; He answers. Devotional kṣatriya-dharma is reactive on behalf of those one protects, not aggressive for one's own ends.
    In Pradyumna’s absence, Dyumān had been devastating His army, but now Pradyumna counterattacked Dyumān and, smiling, pierced him with eight nārāca arrows.
    Purport

    Śrīla Viśvanātha Cakravartī comments that Pradyumna challenged Dyumān, saying “Now see if you can strike Me!” After saying this and allowing Dyumān to shoot his weapons, Pradyumna released His own deadly arrows.

    — A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
    Gauḍīya Ācāryas
    Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
    Translation
    In Pradyumna's absence, Dyumān had been devastating His army, but now Pradyumna counterattacked Dyumān and, smiling, pierced him with eight nārāca arrows.
    Commentary

    Saying "Oh, attack as long as you can!" he received Dyuman’s attack and then counter attacked (pratihatya), piercing him with eight arrows (naracaih).

    Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
    Commentary

    He attacked Dyumān who had penetrated his army and pierced him in response to his attacks with special arrows. He smiled as a sign of his perseverance as a warrior. He was not angry. Thus his smiling is praised.

    Śrīdhara Svāmī Gauḍīya
    Commentary

    He attacked Dyumān who had penetrated his army and pierced him in response to his attacks, or on each limb (prati) with special arrows. He smiled as a sign of his perseverance as a warrior or to indicate that it was easy.

    Important Words
    • caturbhiḥ — with four
      Instrumental plural of catur. The fourfold arrow-strike against the four horses — Gauḍīya commentators often read such enumerations as the dismantling of the four-fold mind (manas, buddhi, ahaṅkāra, citta) when bhakti reasserts itself.
    • ketum — flag
      The chariot's banner — the public emblem of a warrior's identity. To shoot down the ketu is to remove the enemy's claim to status before removing his life; māyā's first defeat is always the shattering of its banner of self-importance.
    • śiraḥ — head
      From the root meaning that which is highest. The decisive shaft lands on the seat of false thought — a recurring Bhāgavatam motif, from Hiraṇyakaśipu to Vṛtra to Śālva, that the Lord's weapon ultimately severs the head, the locus of avidyā.

    • gada-sātyaki-sāmba-ādyāḥ — headed by Gada, Sātyaki and Sāmba
      Compound naming three Yadu warriors — Kṛṣṇa's brother, His cousin-disciple, and His son. Gauḍīyas note that the Lord's pastimes are never solitary; He is always surrounded by parikara, eternal associates, who share the work and the glory.

      Compound: sañchinna (cut through) + kandhara (neck). Bhāgavatam imagery of decisive removal — when the Lord's party engages māyā's forces, the result is not negotiation but cleanly cut necks falling into the sea of saṁsāra they came from.

      Compound: tri (three) + nava (nine) + rātra (night). Twenty-seven — the count of nakṣatras, suggesting a complete cycle of time. The battle exhausts a full sidereal round before the Lord Himself arrives to conclude it.

      Intense, dreadful. The text does not soften the duration or the dread — devotional realism: the Yadus' war against Śālva is not made trivial because Kṛṣṇa is its silent center.

    Stories
    Why Kṛṣṇa leaves Indraprastha

    Yudhiṣṭhira had invited Kṛṣṇa to the Rājasūya, where Śiśupāla was slain by the Sudarśana — the climax of Canto 10's seventy-fourth chapter. With the sacrifice complete, the kings hostile to Śiśupāla begin to stir, and Kṛṣṇa reads the omens before anyone else does.

    How can lamentation, bewilderment, material affection or fear, all born out of ignorance, be ascribed to the infinite Supreme Lord, whose perception, knowledge and power are all similarly infinite?
    Purport

    Śrīla Prabhupāda writes: “Lamentation, aggrievement and bewilderment are characteristics of conditioned souls, but how can such things affect the person of the Supreme, who is full of knowledge, power and all opulence? Actually, it is not at all possible that Lord Kṛṣṇa was misled by the mystic jugglery of Śālva. He was displaying His pastime of playing the role of a human being.” All the great Bhāgavatam commentators conclude that grief, illusion, attachment and fear, which arise out of ignorance of the soul, can never be present in the transcendental dramatic pastimes enacted by the Lord. Śrīla Jīva Gosvāmī gives many examples from Kṛṣṇa’s pastimes to illustrate this point. For instance, when the cowherd boys entered the mouth of Aghāsura, Lord Kṛṣṇa was apparently astonished. Similarly, when Brahmā took away Lord Kṛṣṇa’s cowherd boyfriends and calves, the Lord at first began to look for them as if He did not know where they were. Thus the Lord plays the part of an ordinary human being so as to relish transcendental pastimes with His devotees. One should never think the Personality of Godhead is an ordinary person, as Śukadeva Gosvāmī explains in this and the following verse.

Lamentation and other emotions are of two types: caused by ignorance and caused by knowledge. The emotions described here are possible in people who are ignorant, or do not know everything, who are dependent on wrong sources of knowledge. But how is it possible in the supreme lord Krsna who has unlimited knowledge? The spiritual emotions such as lamentation possible in those who have knowledge, who are beyond illusion,. occur in the devotees and in the Lord who is the very form of all sweet rasa arising as sancari bhavas as part of rasa. This can be very clearly seen in Damodara pastimes (Krsna crying), and the purva raga of the gopis during the rasa lila. Fear mentioned in this verse refers to verse eight. Without budging in the slightest, Lord Kṛṣṇa bludgeoned Dantavakra's chest, shattering his heart. Dantavakra had a brother named Vidūratha, and he became distraught at Dantavakra's death. When Lord Baladeva heard that the Pāṇḍavas and Kauravas were about to go to war, in order to remain neutral He left Dvārakā on the pretext of going on pilgrimage. The Lord bathed in sacred places such as Prabhāsa, Tritakūpa and Viśāla, and eventually He came to the holy Naimiṣāraṇya forest, where great sages were performing an extended fire sacrifice. While being worshiped by the assembled sages and offered a seat of honor, the Lord noticed that Romaharṣaṇa Sūta, sitting on the speaker's seat, had failed to stand in deference to Him. Greatly angered by this offense, Lord Balarāma killed Romaharṣaṇa by touching him with the tip of a blade of kuśa grass. The assembled sages were disturbed by what Lord Baladeva had done, and they said to Him, "You have unknowingly killed a brāhmaṇa.     The sinful Śālva fallen, the Saubha smashed, demigods sound kettledrums in the heavens — and at once Dantavakra, enraged by his friends' deaths, rushes the Lord. One demon's removal does not finish the day; māyā always has another emissary at the gate, friend of the last, charging in even before the dust settles.

    Stories
    Dantavakra and the friends-of-Śiśupāla cycle

    Dantavakra is the third of the chief Vidarbha-region asuric kings — Śiśupāla, Śālva, Dantavakra — bound by friendship and shared hatred of Kṛṣṇa. The Bhāgavatam treats them as a triad: Śiśupāla is slain at the Rājasūya (10.74), Śālva here in 10.77, and Dantavakra in the very next chapter (10.78).

    Viśvanātha and Sanātana note that all three are in fact Jaya and Vijaya, Vaikuṇṭha gatekeepers, completing their third and last asura-birth under the curse of the four Kumāras. Hence the seeming relentlessness of their enmity — they need the Lord to finish them, three times each in the case of Jaya, to return home.
