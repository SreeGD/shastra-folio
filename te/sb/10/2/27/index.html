Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.2.27

ఏకాయనోఽసౌ ద్విఫలస్త్రిమూల-శ్చతూరస: పఞ్చవిధ: షడాత్మా । సప్తత్వగష్టవిటపో నవాక్షోదశచ్ఛదీ ద్విఖగో హ్యాదివృక్ష: ॥ ౨౭ ॥

The body [the total body and the individual body are of the same composition] may figuratively be called “the original tree.” From this tree, which fully depends on the ground of material nature, come two kinds of fruit — the enjoyment of happiness and the suffering of distress. The cause of the tree, forming its three roots, is association with the three modes of material nature — goodness, passion and ignorance. The fruits of bodily happiness have four tastes — religiosity, economic development, sense gratification and liberation — which are experienced through five senses for acquiring knowledge in the midst of six circumstances: lamentation, illusion, old age, death, hunger and thirst. The seven layers of bark covering the tree are skin, blood, muscle, fat, bone, marrow and semen, and the eight branches of the tree are the five gross and three subtle elements — earth, water, fire, air, ether, mind, intelligence and false ego. The tree of the body has nine hollows — the eyes, the ears, the nostrils, the mouth, the rectum and the genitals — and ten leaves, the ten airs passing through the body. In this tree of the body there are two birds: one is the individual soul, and the other is the Supersoul.
Purport

This material world is composed of five principal elements — earth, water, fire, air and ether — all of which are emanations from Kṛṣṇa. Although materialistic scientists may accept these five primary elements as the cause of the material manifestation, these elements in their gross and subtle states are produced by Kṛṣṇa, whose marginal potency also produces the living entities working within this material world. The Seventh Chapter of Bhagavad-gītā clearly states that the entire cosmic manifestation is a combination of two of Kṛṣṇa’s energies — the superior energy and the inferior energy. The living entities are the superior energy, and the inanimate material elements are His inferior energy. In the dormant stage, everything rests in Kṛṣṇa. Material scientists cannot give such a thorough analysis of the material structure of the body. The analysis of the material scientists concerns itself only with inanimate matter, but this is inadequate because the living entity is completely separate from the material bodily structure. In Bhagavad-gītā (7.5) the Lord says: apareyam itas tv anyāṁ prakṛtiṁ viddhi me parām jīva-bhūtāṁ mahā-bāho yayedaṁ dhāryate jagat “Besides this inferior nature, O mighty-armed Arjuna, there is a superior energy of Mine, which consists of all the living entities who are struggling with material nature and are sustaining the universe.” Although the material elements emanate from the Supreme Personality of Godhead, Kṛṣṇa, they are separated elements and are sustained by the living elements. As indicated by the word dvi-khagaḥ, the living elements within the body resemble two birds in a tree. Kha means “sky,” and ga means “one who flies.” Thus the word dvi-khagaḥ refers to birds. In the tree of the body there are two birds, or two living elements, and they are always different. In Bhagavad-gītā (13.3) , the Lord says, kṣetra-jñaṁ cāpi māṁ viddhi sarva-kṣetreṣu bhārata: “O scion of Bharata, you should understand that I am also the knower in all bodies.” The kṣetra jña, the owner of the body, is also called the khaga, the living entity. Within the body there are two such kṣetra jñas — the individual soul and the Supersoul. The individual soul is the owner of his individual body, but the Supersoul is present within the bodies of all living entities. Such a thorough analysis and understanding of the bodily structure cannot be obtained anywhere but in the Vedic literature. When two birds enter a tree, one may foolishly think that the birds become one or merge with the tree, but actually they do not. Rather, each bird keeps its individual identity. Similarly, the individual soul and the Supersoul do not become one, nor do they merge with matter. The living entity lives close to matter, but this does not mean that he merges or mixes with it ( asaṅgo hy ayaṁ puruṣaḥ ), although material scientists mistakenly see the organic and inorganic, or animate and inanimate, to be mixed. Vedic knowledge has been kept imprisoned or concealed, but every human being needs to understand it in truth. The modern civilization of ignorance is simply engaged in analyzing the body, and thus people come to the erroneous conclusion that the living force within the body is generated under certain material conditions. People have no information of the soul, but this verse gives the perfect explanation that there are two living forces ( dvi-khaga ): the individual soul and the Supersoul. The Supersoul is present in every body ( īśvaraḥ sarva-bhūtānāṁ hṛd-deśe ’rjuna tiṣṭhati ), whereas the individual soul is situated only in his own body ( dehī ) and is transmigrating from one body to another.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
The body [the total body and the individual body are of the same composition] may figuratively be called "the original tree." From this tree, which fully depends on the ground of material nature, come two kinds of fruit—the enjoyment of happiness and the suffering of distress. The cause of the tree, forming its three roots, is association with the three modes of material nature—goodness, passion and ignorance. The fruits of bodily happiness have four tastes—religiosity, economic development, sense gratification and liberation—which are experienced through five senses for acquiring knowledge in the midst of six circumstances: lamentation, illusion, old age, death, hunger and thirst. The seven layers of bark covering the tree are skin, blood, muscle, fat, bone, marrow and semen, and the eight branches of the tree are the five gross and three subtle elements—earth, water, fire, air, ether, mind, intelligence and false ego. The tree of the body has nine hollows—the eyes, the ears, the nostrils, the mouth, the rectum and the genitals—and ten leaves, the ten airs passing through the body. In this tree of the body there are two birds: one is the individual soul, and the other is the Supersoul.
Commentary

The Lord then asks a question. "If I, endowed with body, sense and dhama am ‘satya’, then is the material world ‘asatya?’" They answer. " The world is true, but subject to destruction in time, whereas you are not destructible by time. You are indestructible." With this intention they recite this verse. Ekayanah means there is one shelter of the tree of the material world, called prakrti. There are two fruits of the tree of the material world: happiness and misery. There are three roots of the tree: sattva, raja and tama. There are four rasas: four the varnas and asramas. There are five types of knowledge: that derived from the five senses. There are six natural qualities (atma) of the tree: the six whirlpools of lamentation, illusion, old age, death, hunger and thirst. There are seven coverings of the tree: skin, flesh, blood, fat, bone, marrow and semen. There are eight branches (vitapa): the five gross elements, mind, intelligence and false ego. There are nine holes in the tree: the holes in the body such as two eyes, two nostrils, two ears, mouth, rectum and genital. Ther are ten leaves: the ten life airs. There are two birds in the tree, the jiva and the Lord.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

A general description simply indicates the proprietors of the body—two birds. They are described in more detail later. Yayor ekaḥ khādati pippalānnam: of the two one bird eats the tree’s fruits. (SB 11.11.6) The jīva and Paramātmā are not limbs of the tree: they are beyond the material realm since they exist from the first like a stream with continuity. The tree is described as ādi-vṛkṣaḥ (from vṛṣc to cut down) because time at all times attacks it. Its fruits are happiness and distress, with four tastes. It has eight branches. The second a in aṣṭa-viṭapaḥ should be lengthened for metrical reasons. The verse is explained according to Śrīdhara Svāmī. The sentence continues for two verses. The five senses of the tree are the five knowledge senses. Plants have senses according to statements: paśyantī pādapāḥ: trees see. (Mahābhārata) The koṣas are skin, flesh, blood, fat, marrow and bones. Or according to others they are hair, blood, flesh, tendons, bone and marrow. The seven dhātus are the previous six plus semen. Sapta-tvag can refer to seven coverings of skin or coverings starting with skin.

SatyaJivaParamatma
Personal Guidance
Your body is a tree rooted in the three guṇas, but two birds sit in it — you and the Supersoul; live aware of Kṛṣṇa beside you.
Practical Applications
  • Before eating, sleeping, or speaking today, pause and acknowledge the second bird — Kṛṣṇa as Paramātmā — seated within your heart alongside you.
  • Notice when you identify with the seven layers of bark (skin, blood, muscle, fat, bone, marrow, semen) and gently re-place yourself as the soul perched in the tree, not the tree itself.
  • Refuse to chase the four 'tastes' (dharma, artha, kāma, mokṣa) as ends in themselves today; offer each effort to Kṛṣṇa, the companion bird who actually sustains the tree.
Reflection Questions
  • Of the four 'tastes' of bodily fruit — dharma, artha, kāma, mokṣa — which one am I most quietly chasing as if it were the goal, forgetting that it's still just a fruit on the tree of the body?
  • The verse says two birds sit in the tree — myself and the Supersoul — yet I usually live as if only one bird were there; in what specific moment today did I forget my eternal Companion was watching and tasting along with me?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • When you are doomscrolling or comparing yourself to classmates on Instagram, name what is happening in the verse's terms: the senses (pañca-vidhaḥ) are feeding the tree, and you are forgetting you are the bird, not the tree. Set a daily limit and treat breaking it as forgetting who you are.
  • Before your next exam or interview, sit for two minutes and identify which of the four tastes (dharma, artha, kāma, mokṣa) you are actually chasing through this performance. If it is purely artha or kāma dressed up as dharma, name it honestly to Kṛṣṇa — that honesty is the beginning of sva-dharma.
  • In your friend group, notice the six circumstances the verse names — lamentation, illusion, old age, death, hunger, thirst. Your friends are already drowning in the first two. Be the one who does not pretend these are solvable by the next relationship, the next internship, the next vacation. That quiet refusal is your daśya — your skill — at this stage.
householder
  • When making a major family decision — a school for the kids, a job change, a house purchase — explicitly map it onto the four tastes. Most householder decisions are 80 percent artha and kāma with a thin coating of dharma. Decide as a couple where the actual proportions are, and where they should be.
  • Your children are watching which bird you identify with. If they only ever see you absorbed in the tree — career, money, status, body — they will conclude that is what a human being is. Once a week, do something that visibly belongs to the second bird: a kīrtana at home, a verse read aloud at dinner, a refusal of an opportunity for a non-material reason.
  • Money, mortgage, school fees, aging parents — these are the seven barks and nine hollows of householder life and they are real. The verse does not ask you to abandon them; it asks you to remember they are bark, not core. Each evening offer the day's earnings and labors to Kṛṣṇa formally, even briefly — this is the householder's version of dvi-khagaḥ awareness.
senior
  • Old age is one of the six circumstances the verse explicitly names. Do not pretend it is not happening, and do not let the wellness industry sell you the fantasy that it can be reversed. Treat each diminishment of the body as evidence the verse was telling the truth — the tree is being pruned, the bird is intact.
  • You have spent decades inside the seven barks and nine hollows. Now is the time to teach the younger people in your family or saṅga what you have learned about which fruits actually nourished and which only looked sweet. Mentor concretely — name the artha and kāma pursuits you regret giving so many years to.
  • Loosen one attachment per month deliberately — a possession, a role, a grievance, an expectation of a child or grandchild. Do not wait for death to pry your fingers open. The dhīra of BG 2.13 prepares now; the verse you are studying tells you exactly which bird you are about to fly home as.
Common Pitfalls
  • Treating the 'tree of the body' analogy as poetic ornament rather than a diagnostic — students admire the imagery but never actually identify which roots (sattva, rajas, tamas) are feeding their current cravings.
  • Sliding into impersonalism by reading 'two birds' as one soul that just needs to 'realize its higher Self' — the verse insists on two distinct living entities (dvi-khagaḥ); the individual soul never merges into the Supersoul or into matter.
  • Using the body's analysis as an excuse for neglect or contempt for the body — Prabhupāda's purport critiques the materialist reduction, but the answer is not to despise the tree; it is to recognize whose tree it is and serve the second bird who shares it.
Discussion Prompts
  • The verse names two birds in the tree of the body — the individual soul and the Supersoul. Share with your partner one specific moment this past week when you sensed the second bird as a witness to your choices. If you have never noticed Him, say so honestly — and let the group push back on what has been blocking that awareness.
  • Prabhupāda says the modern civilization 'is simply engaged in analyzing the body' and concludes the living force is generated from matter. Where in your own thinking — your work, your health regimen, your view of mental illness, your view of death — have you quietly accepted this materialist premise? Disagree with each other: is it really possible to live as if the soul is separate from the body in 2026?
  • The four 'tastes' of the bodily fruit are dharma, artha, kāma, and mokṣa. Most people in this room are mainly chasing two of them. Name which two are actually driving your daily decisions — not which two you wish were driving them — and let the group challenge whether you are honest.
Role Play

Two students are paired. Student A plays a college-aged cousin who has just returned from a neuroscience lecture and announces at the dinner table: 'Consciousness is just neurons firing — when the brain dies, you die. There is no soul, no afterlife, no Supersoul. The ādi-vṛkṣa is a poetic metaphor, nothing more.' Student B, drawing only on SB 10.2.27 and its purport (the dvi-khagaḥ image, the distinction between Kṛṣṇa's superior and inferior energies, asaṅgo hy ayaṁ puruṣaḥ), must respond — not by quoting scripture at the cousin, but by walking him through the tree-of-the-body analysis until he sees what the materialist account actually leaves unexplained. After 7 minutes, switch roles. Then the class reconvenes: where did the Gauḍīya argument land, and where did it bounce off?

Objections & Responses
  • This whole 'tree of the body' scheme — three roots, four tastes, seven barks, eight branches, nine hollows, ten leaves — sounds like ancient numerology dressed up as anatomy. Why should a modern person take this seriously rather than treat it as quaint pre-scientific cosmology?
    The numbers are a mnemonic, not the point. The point is the structural claim underneath: that the body is a composite of identifiable layers (gross elements, subtle elements, sense apertures, vital airs) and that none of these layers is the self. Modern anatomy does not contradict this — it confirms the layering and stops there. The verse goes one step further and asks: who is the one experiencing the layers? That question has not been answered by neuroscience; it has been postponed. The Gauḍīya tradition does not ask you to prefer the verse's count over a biology textbook — it asks you to notice that the textbook never gets to the dvi-khagaḥ at all.
  • If the individual soul and the Supersoul are both birds in the same tree, why not just say they are one — the Advaitin reading is simpler and avoids the awkwardness of a personal Lord sitting inside every body.
    Prabhupāda anticipates exactly this in the purport: 'When two birds enter a tree, one may foolishly think that the birds become one or merge with the tree, but actually they do not. Rather, each bird keeps its individual identity.' The simplicity of the monist reading is purchased by collapsing a distinction the verse explicitly draws — dvi-khagaḥ, two birds, not one. And the practical cost is enormous: if there is no second bird, there is no one to surrender to, no relationship to cultivate, no bhakti. The Gauḍīya line keeps the distinction because the whole life of devotion depends on it. The Supersoul is in every body (īśvaraḥ sarva-bhūtānāṁ); the jīva is only in his own. To merge them is to lose both.
