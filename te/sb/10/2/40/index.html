Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.2.40

O supreme controller, Your Lordship previously accepted incarnations as a fish, a horse, a tortoise, Narasiṁhadeva, a boar, a swan, Lord Rāmacandra, Paraśurāma and, among the demigods, Vāmanadeva, to protect the entire world by Your mercy. Now please protect us again by Your mercy by diminishing the disturbances in this world. O Kṛṣṇa, best of the Yadus, we respectfully offer our obeisances unto You.
Purport

In every incarnation, the Supreme Personality of Godhead has a particular mission to execute, and this was true in His appearance as the son of Devakī in the family of the Yadus. Thus all the demigods offered their prayers to the Lord, bowing down before Him, and requested the Lord to do the needful. We cannot order the Supreme Personality of Godhead to do anything for us. We can simply offer Him our obeisances, as advised in Bhagavad-gītā ( man-manā bhava mad-bhakto mad-yājī māṁ namaskuru ), and pray to Him for annihilation of dangers.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
O supreme controller, Your Lordship previously accepted incarnations as a fish, a horse, a tortoise, Narasiṁhadeva, a boar, a swan, Lord Rāmacandra, Paraśurāma and, among the demigods, Vāmanadeva, to protect the entire world by Your mercy. Now please protect us again by Your mercy by diminishing the disturbances in this world. O Kṛṣṇa, best of the Yadus, we respectfully offer our obeisances unto You.
Commentary

"Please put aside all of our talks of suffering caused by our unsteady minds. Just as you previously protected us by taking various forms, so now remove the burden of the earth. That is the same as protecting us." Speaking thus they then touched their heads to the earth in respect.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

O Lord! The devatās describe the Lord’s ability to protect. The words “Please protect us” should be understood at the end. O best of the Yadus! You have now personally appeared as Kṛṣṇa, but previously also you gave protection. Please remove the earth’s suffering. You say mayā hatāṁs tvaṁ jahi mā vyathiṣṭhā: destroy those already killed by me and do not be disturbed. (BG 11.34) In that manner, by your birth, everything is accomplished. Therefore saying “Please remove the suffering” becomes an emphatic request. The request should be understood to be made out of great longing to see his pastimes before their eyes. Or, just as you appear in these forms and protect us now, you will also protect us in the future. With great longing they make the request for the future also. With that in mind, they say “Please remove the suffering of the earth.” Though you killed Hiraṇyakaśipu, Kālameni and others when you appeared as Narasiṁha and other forms, now they have taken birth again and make the earth suffer. Therefore, now act in such a way that this will not happen again. By this we devotees will gain the greatest benefit, by not seeing these demons. “But giving them all liberation is not suitable.” The devatās respond by offering respects. The deep meaning here is this: by liberating the demons, we will not longer have to see them since they will no longer take birth. Thus we devotees gain the greatest benefit.

Śrīdhara Svāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

O Lord! The devatās describe the Lord’s ability to protect. The words “Please protect us” should be understood at the end. O best of the Yadus! You have now personally appeared as Kṛṣṇa, but previously also you gave protection. Please remove the earth’s suffering. You say mayā hatāṁs tvaṁ jahi mā vyathiṣṭhā: destroy those who are already killed by me and do not be disturbed. (BG 11.34) In that manner, by your birth, everything is accomplished. Therefore saying “Please remove the suffering” becomes an emphatic request. The request should be understood to be made out of great longing to see his pastimes before their eyes. Or, just as you appear in these forms and protect us now, you will also protect us in the future. With great longing they make the request for the future also. With that in mind, they say “Please remove the suffering of the earth.” Though you killed Hiraṇyakaśipu, Kālameni and others when you appeared as Narasiṁha and other forms, now they have taken birth again and make the earth suffer. Therefore, now act in such a way that this will not happen again. By this we devotees will gain the greatest benefit, for we won’t have to see these demons again. “But giving them all liberation is not suitable.” The devatās respond by offering respects. The deep meaning here is this: by liberating the demons, we will no longer have to see them since they will no longer take birth. Thus we devotees will gain the greatest benefit. Or relieve the burden of the earth, by killing the demons, so that the world can worship you (vandanam te), or so that your great devotees the Yadus (te) can worship you nicely (uttama). By removing the demons permanently, it is appropriate that the Lord gives them liberation, since it completely devoid of prema.

Personal Guidance
Kṛṣṇa descends in many forms to relieve the world's burden — our part is not to command Him but to bow down and pray.
Practical Applications
  • Begin the day by offering daṇḍavat-praṇāmas before your āsana, asking Kṛṣṇa to remove the disturbances you cannot remove yourself.
  • Notice when you slip into demanding outcomes from Kṛṣṇa, and replace the demand with man-manā bhava mad-bhakto mad-yājī māṁ namaskuru — think of Him, serve Him, offer obeisances.
  • Name one specific 'bhāra' (burden) weighing on you today and, instead of strategizing alone, formally pray to the Yadūttama to act through the situation.
Reflection Questions
  • When I face a disturbance in my world, do I instinctively try to manage it myself, or do I bow down and pray for Kṛṣṇa's intervention as the demigods did here — and what does my reflex reveal about whom I actually trust?
  • Across the daśāvatāra listed in this verse, Kṛṣṇa repeatedly enters the world to relieve its bhāra (burden); what specific bhāra in my own life am I refusing to surrender to Him because I would rather carry it as proof of my own importance?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • When an exam result, a rejection, or a friend-group fallout hits, resist the reflex to fix it by pulling more strings on social media. Instead, sit for two minutes and recall one specific time Kṛṣṇa's arrangement carried you through something you couldn't see past — then offer obeisances and go to sleep.
  • Kṛṣṇa as yadūttama belongs to a family, a place, a name. This week, name your own situation in prayer with the same specificity — not 'help me grow,' but 'I am in second-year engineering, I have this professor, this roommate, this habit I can't break — please diminish this burden.' Generic prayers train a generic faith.
  • Watch for the impersonalist drift in your own peer talk — 'the universe is telling me,' 'manifest your truth.' These sound spiritual but quietly replace a Person you can bow to with a vibe you can flatter. Catch yourself once a day and rephrase to Kṛṣṇa directly.
householder
  • When a real burden lands on the family — a medical bill, a child's school crisis, a job loss, an aging parent — gather the household, even briefly, and do what the demigods did: recall aloud one or two ways Kṛṣṇa has previously protected this family, then offer obeisances together before discussing logistics. Set the order: surrender first, strategy second.
  • Stop trying to be the avatāra of your own household. The instinct to single-handedly fix every disturbance — financial, emotional, scheduling — is ahaṅkāra in a competent costume. Identify one burden this week that you have been carrying as if Kṛṣṇa were not the actual maintainer, and consciously hand it back through prayer and seva, while still doing your duty.
  • Teach your children the names of the avatāras — not as bedtime mythology but as the family's protective lineage. A child who grows up knowing the household turns to Kṛṣṇa in crisis (not to panic, not to mere problem-solving) inherits something no school transfers.
senior
  • The demigods pray when the burden of the earth has become too heavy. In this stage, recognize that the burden you are meant to set down is your own — control over outcomes, over children's choices, over the shape of the institution you built. Each evening, name one attachment and offer it: vandanaṁ te.
  • Use your remaining years to recite Kṛṣṇa's pastimes and avatāras to those younger than you — not as nostalgia, but as the demigods do here, as living protection. Mentoring in this tradition is not advice-giving; it is reminding the next generation Whom to bow to when their crises come.
  • Mortality is the final 'disturbance in this world' that no strategy diminishes. Practice now what you intend to do then: think of Him, take shelter, offer obeisances. Memorize this verse in IAST and let its cadence — pāsi nas, bhāraṁ bhuvo hara, vandanaṁ te — become the rhythm your breath returns to.
Common Pitfalls
  • Treating the list of incarnations as mythological trivia or comparative-religion data, instead of letting each name strengthen the conviction that the same Personality has been personally protecting devotees across yugas — and is available to me now.
  • Reading 'please diminish the disturbances' as license to petition Kṛṣṇa as a cosmic problem-solver for material inconveniences, while ignoring Prabhupāda's pointed reminder that 'we cannot order the Supreme Personality of Godhead to do anything for us' — our posture is namaskuru, not negotiation.
  • Slipping into impersonalism by abstracting 'the Lord who appears in many forms' into a generic divine principle, when the verse's whole force is personal address: tvam, te, yadūttama — Kṛṣṇa Himself, best of the Yadus, in a specific family, with a specific mission.
Discussion Prompts
  • The demigods list nine previous incarnations before asking Kṛṣṇa to act again. Why do they recite the past avatāras instead of just stating the present crisis? Share with your partner one situation where remembering Kṛṣṇa's past protections changed how you faced a current burden — and one where you forgot, and acted as if you were alone.
  • Prabhupāda says flatly: 'We cannot order the Supreme Personality of Godhead to do anything for us.' Yet most of our prayer life is, honestly, a list of requests. Where is the line between legitimate prayer for protection (as the demigods do here) and trying to put Kṛṣṇa to work for our agenda? Disagree openly — don't settle the tension too quickly.
  • The verse names Kṛṣṇa yadūttama, 'best of the Yadus' — He has accepted a family, a clan, a particular body. Some in the group will find this intimacy beautiful; others will feel the personalist God is 'too small.' Argue both sides honestly, then ask: which side is your daily practice actually built on?
Role Play

Two students. Student A is a long-time devotee whose city is gripped by a real crisis (a flood, a layoff wave, a public scandal touching the temple community). Student B is a non-devotee friend who asks, 'If your Kṛṣṇa is so powerful and has come down nine times before, why doesn't He just fix this? And if He won't, why are you bothering to pray?' Student A must respond using only what is in SB 10.2.40 and its purport — naming the avatāras, distinguishing obeisances from ordering God, and explaining bhāra-haraṇa (diminishing the burden of the world) without inventing pastimes or drifting into impersonalism. After 4 minutes, swap roles. Then debrief: where did the argument want to slide into 'the universe will sort it out'?

Objections & Responses
  • This sounds like mythology stacked on mythology — fish, tortoise, boar, swan. Modern people can't take a literal list of animal incarnations seriously. Isn't this just primitive symbolism we should reinterpret?
    The Gauḍīya line does not offer the avatāras as symbols to be decoded into something more palatable. They are accepted as historical descents of the same Supreme Person, each with a specific mission — Matsya saving the Vedas, Kūrma supporting the churning, Varāha lifting the earth, Nṛsiṁha protecting Prahlāda, and so on. To 'reinterpret' them as metaphor is precisely the impersonalist move Prabhupāda warns against: it keeps God conveniently abstract and leaves us in charge of meaning. The demigods here are not doing literary criticism; they are doing what we are invited to do — recall concrete acts of Kṛṣṇa's mercy and bow down. The test is not whether the list is sophisticated enough for us, but whether we are humble enough for the list.
  • If we 'cannot order' God to do anything, then prayer is pointless. Either He does what He was already going to do, or He doesn't — my obeisances change nothing.
    Notice what the demigods actually do: they don't change Kṛṣṇa's mind, they change their position. They bow. They recite His names and deeds. They request — vandanaṁ te — and entrust the outcome. The shift is in the praying party, not the prayed-to. Kṛṣṇa instructs in the Gītā, man-manā bhava mad-bhakto mad-yājī māṁ namaskuru: think of Me, become My devotee, worship Me, offer obeisances to Me. The 'point' of prayer is not to engineer an outcome; it is to take up the relationship in which protection naturally flows. The demigods know He is already coming as the son of Devakī. They pray anyway — because that is what the protected do.
