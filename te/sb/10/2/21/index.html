Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.2.21

Kaṁsa thought: What is my duty now? The Supreme Lord, who knows His purpose will not give up His prowess. Devakī is a woman, she is my sister, and moreover she is now pregnant. If I kill her, my reputation, opulence and duration of life will certainly be vanquished.
Purport

According to Vedic principles, a woman, a brāhmaṇa, an old man, a child and a cow should never be killed. It appears that Kaṁsa, although a great enemy of the Supreme Personality of Godhead, was aware of the Vedic culture and conscious of the fact that the soul transmigrates from one body to another and that one suffers in the next life according to the karmas of this life. Therefore he was afraid of killing Devakī, since she was a woman, she was his sister, and she was pregnant. A kṣatriya becomes famous by performing heroic acts. But what would be heroic about killing a woman who, while confined in his custody, was under his shelter? Therefore, he did not want to act drastically by killing Devakī. Kaṁsa’s enemy was within Devakī’s womb, but killing an enemy in such a nescient state would not be an exhibition of prowess. According to kṣatriya rules, an enemy should be fought face to face and with proper weapons. Then if the enemy is killed, the victor becomes famous. Kaṁsa very conscientiously deliberated upon these facts and therefore refrained from killing Devakī, although he was completely confident that his enemy had already appeared within her womb.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Kaṁsa thought: What is my duty now? The Supreme Lord, who knows His purpose [paritrāṇāya sādhūnāṁ vināśāya ca duṣkṛtām Bg. 4.8], will not give up His prowess. Devakī is a woman, she is my sister, and moreover she is now pregnant. If I kill her, my reputation, opulence and duration of life will certainly be vanquished.
Commentary

In this verse he speaks more clearly his intentions. "What should I do about Hari (tasmin)? Is it proper to kill him while he is still an embryo? No, because those interested in their own welfare (artha tantra) do not destroy their own good name. By killing him now my reputation of prowess will be destroyed. But if he is born and grows, and then I fight with him, whether I am victorious or defeated, my glory will remain intact. Killing a child in the womb is not an act of bravery. Not only will it destroy my fame, but dharma also gets destroyed." Thus he says by killing a woman, a sister, and a foetus, fame, power and lifespan will be permanently lost. This temporary restriction on his evil nature was because of fear. Thus he began thinking of his reputation through careful discrimination.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

(First meaning is: Viṣṇu will not give up his prowess and will try to kill me. But killing a woman is sinful.) According to Śrīdhara Svāmī there is a second meaning. “A person knowing his own benefit does not give up his prowess. Thus killing a woman is not good since one loses fame, wealth and longevity.” Right now, immediately what should I do? His evil nature became restricted because of fear, and he thought of how to act for his benefit. If I kill her, will it not be a temporary means to protect my life? If I kill her, will I not obtain long lasting infamy and loss of wealth? It is bad to kill a woman, worse if she is my sister, and worst if she is pregnant. And by killing her, it is bad to lose fame, worse to lose wealth and worst to lose long life. By killing her, my life is destroyed. What will be the good of that?

Concepts
JivaGuru
Personal Guidance
Even Kaṁsa paused before harming a pregnant woman — let residual dharma restrain you, but know only surrender to Kṛṣṇa truly purifies the heart.
Practical Applications
  • Notice one situation today where fear of consequences (reputation, loss) is restraining you, and ask whether real dharma — not self-interest — is guiding the choice.
  • Refuse to harm the vulnerable in your circle (a woman, a child, an elder, a dependent) even by speech or neglect, honoring the Vedic principle Kaṁsa himself respected.
  • Speak honestly to Kṛṣṇa in your evening sādhana about the 'enemy in the womb' — the inconvenient truth or duty you are calculating how to avoid.
Reflection Questions
  • Like Kaṁsa, where am I restraining a harmful act not out of karuṇā for the soul but only to protect my own yaśas, śrī, and āyuḥ — and what does that reveal about whose welfare I am actually serving?
  • Which 'enemy in the womb' am I aware of in my own life — a duty, a confrontation, or a truth I keep postponing because I have convinced myself the timing or method is improper — and is that scruple genuine dharma or a sophisticated excuse?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Audit one 'kind' thing you did this week on social media — a supportive comment, a repost, a defense of a friend. Was it karuṇā for that person's soul, or Kaṁsa-style protection of your own image as a 'good person'? Write down the honest answer in your phone notes.
  • Identify one person in your friend group or class whom you've been quietly cruel to — through silence, exclusion, or jokes — while telling yourself you're 'not really doing anything.' Kaṁsa also believed inaction protected him. This week, take one concrete reparative step (a direct apology, an invitation, sitting with them at lunch).
  • When studying for the next exam, notice the moment you tell yourself 'I deserve a break' and reach for a reel. That's the same logic Kaṁsa used: 'I have good reasons not to act now.' Practice naming it out loud — 'this is Kaṁsa-thinking' — and return to the book for 25 more minutes.
householder
  • Identify one family decision (a school for your child, a job change, caring for an aging parent) where you have been delaying because the right action would cost you reputation, money, or comfort — Kaṁsa's exact triad of yaśas, śrī, āyuḥ. Name the cost honestly to your spouse this week, and decide together.
  • In your workplace, locate one situation where you've been 'compassionate' to an underperformer in a way that actually protects your image as a kind manager. Distinguish that from real karuṇā — which sometimes requires a hard conversation that serves the other person's long-term growth and dharma.
  • When disciplining your children, watch for the Kaṁsa-pattern: refraining not because the discipline is wrong, but because their tears will hurt your standing as the 'fun parent' or trigger your spouse's disapproval. Real parental karuṇā serves the child's soul, not your domestic peace.
senior
  • Review your will, your estate, and your stated 'legacy.' How much of it is structured around yaśas (how you'll be remembered), śrī (preserving the family's wealth and status), and āyuḥ (extending your own influence past death)? Rewrite at least one provision so that it serves Kṛṣṇa and the genuine spiritual welfare of your heirs, not your reputation.
  • Identify one younger person — a child, grandchild, or junior at the temple — to whom you have withheld a hard truth because speaking it would damage your standing as the gentle elder. Kaṁsa also chose silence to protect his image. Schedule the conversation this month.
  • Each evening, practice releasing one attachment that the body is anyway about to take from you — a possession, a position, a grudge, an expectation of how others should treat you. Unlike Kaṁsa, who clung to āyuḥ until the last moment, prepare to meet death having already handed these things to Kṛṣṇa.
Common Pitfalls
  • Mistaking Kaṁsa's calculation for compassion: reading this verse as if reputation-management and consequence-avoidance were equivalent to real karuṇā or kartavya, when in fact even an asura can perform the outward act for entirely self-interested reasons.
  • Using Vedic protections (for women, brāhmaṇas, elders, children, cows) as abstract talking points rather than as concrete restraints on one's own conduct today — quoting the rule while ignoring whom one actually harms in speech, work, or family life.
  • Concluding that strategic restraint equals spiritual progress: Kaṁsa refrains from killing Devakī yet remains Kṛṣṇa's enemy, showing that outward 'good behavior' divorced from surrender to the Supreme Personality of Godhead is no shelter at all.
Discussion Prompts
  • Kaṁsa refrains from killing Devakī partly out of fear for his reputation, opulence, and lifespan — not out of real karuṇā. Where in your own life have you done the 'right' thing for the wrong reason, and does the action still count as dharma? Push back on each other if someone says motive doesn't matter.
  • Prabhupāda lists women, brāhmaṇas, the elderly, children, and cows as protected categories in Vedic culture. A modern reader hears this as paternalistic. As a small group, articulate the strongest case for why this stratification is actually karuṇā in practice — and the strongest case against. Don't settle prematurely.
  • Kaṁsa knows the soul transmigrates and that karma follows him into the next life — yet he still plots murder. Discuss: where do you, like Kaṁsa, possess accurate śāstric knowledge that has not yet stopped a behavior you know to be adharmic? Be specific with each other, not abstract.
Role Play

Two students. Student A is a senior manager who has just discovered that a junior colleague — a single mother on a work visa — has been quietly underperforming for months. Firing her is technically the 'kṣatriya' move and is what the company expects. Student B is a fellow Vaiṣṇava friend A confides in over chai. A says, 'I won't fire her — it would destroy her.' B must probe: is A acting from real karuṇā for the soul, or from Kaṁsa-style calculation about A's own reputation as a 'compassionate boss,' fear of HR blowback, and worry about how it will look on social media? Run the conversation for 7 minutes, then switch roles. The class then discusses: what would sva-dharma actually look like here, distinguished from both cruelty and from Kaṁsa's self-protective restraint?

Objections & Responses
  • This verse romanticizes a Vedic culture that gave women, śūdras, and others a 'protected' status which we now recognize as second-class. Why should a modern practitioner take Kaṁsa's hesitation — rooted in that hierarchy — as morally instructive at all?
    Notice what the verse is actually doing: it shows that even Kaṁsa, an avowed enemy of Kṛṣṇa, was restrained by a culture that recognized certain souls as deserving extra shelter. The point is not that women are weaker — it is that a civilization is measured by whom it refuses to harm even when it could. Modern egalitarianism collapses everyone into 'equal combatants,' and the result is not more karuṇā but less — children sue parents, the elderly are warehoused, the unborn are unprotected. Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavism honors varṇāśrama not as a caste cage but as a structure of mutual shelter, where the strong are bound to protect the vulnerable. Kaṁsa's hesitation is the residue of that structure operating even in a demon.
  • Kaṁsa's reasoning is pure self-interest dressed up as ethics — he's worried about yaśas, śrī, and āyuḥ, not about Devakī. Isn't this just proof that 'dharma' is a sophisticated mask for ego?
    Yes — and that is precisely why the verse is included. Bhāgavatam is not naïve about motive. It shows us that dharmic *action* without bhakti can still be driven by ahaṅkāra; Kaṁsa abstains from murder for the same reason a corrupt politician avoids a scandal. The lesson for us is the inverse: even our 'good' restraints — the diet we keep, the temper we control, the donation we make — must be examined for the Kaṁsa-motive hiding underneath. Real dharma matures into bhakti only when the fruit (yaśas, śrī, āyuḥ) is offered to Kṛṣṇa rather than hoarded for the self. The skeptic is right that ethics-without-bhakti is a mask; that is exactly why Kṛṣṇa later tells Arjuna sarva-dharmān parityajya.
