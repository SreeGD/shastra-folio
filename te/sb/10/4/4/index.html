Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.4

Important Words
  • bhrātaram — to her brother
    Devakī addresses Kaṁsa not as tyrant but as brother — invoking sambandha to soften him. The devotee's first instinct, even toward a wrongdoer, is to recall the relation Bhagavān has placed between them.
  • kṛpaṇā — helpless, pitiable
    The word that Yamarāja (SB 6.3) and the Gītā (2.49) use for the bewildered soul. Devakī wears the kṛpaṇa-bhāva not as weakness but as the supplicant's strength — exactly the mood of dīna-prayer Caitanya Mahāprabhu later glorifies (tṛṇād api sunīcena).
  • karuṇam — piteously
    Adverbial — her speech itself is full of karuṇā, compassion. In the Gauḍīya rasa-śāstra, karuṇā for another's welfare is one of the marks of an awakened heart, distinct from sentimental pity.
  • satī — the chaste / saintly one
    Devakī is called satī precisely as she pleads for her child. Her chastity is not biological merely but the integrity of a devotee's heart that does not curse even her tormentor; she stands in her dharma even under the sword.
  • kalyāṇa — all good fortune (to you)
    She blesses Kaṁsa in the same breath she begs from him. This is the saint's response to the offender — pray for his welfare even while resisting his act. Compare Prahlāda's prayer for Hiraṇyakaśipu.
Devakī helplessly, piteously appealed to Kaṁsa: My dear brother, all good fortune unto you. Don’t kill this girl. She will be your daughter-in-law. Indeed, it is unworthy of you to kill a woman.
Purport

Kaṁsa had previously excused Devakī because he thought that a woman should not be killed, especially when pregnant. But now, by the influence of māyā, he was prepared to kill a woman — not only a woman, but a small, helpless newborn child. Devakī wanted to save her brother from this terrible, sinful act. Therefore she told him, “Don’t be so atrocious as to kill a female child. Let there be all good fortune for you.” Demons can do anything for their personal benefit, not considering what is pious or vicious. But Devakī, on the contrary, although safe because she had already given birth to her own son, Kṛṣṇa, was anxious to save the daughter of someone else. This was natural for her.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Devakī helplessly, piteously appealed to Kaṁsa: My dear brother, all good fortune unto you. Don't kill this girl. She will be your daughter-in-law. Indeed, it is unworthy of you to kill a woman.
Commentary

Devaki was shining with satisfaction (devi), because she had been able to hide her son. Krpana indicates she was suffering because she hoped that the girl child could be saved. Sati means she was expert in trying to cheat Kamsa: "This child will be the future bride of your son." Seeing that he was going to take the child by force, she then addresses him as Kalayana, most auspicious to indicate that he should not produce inauspiciousness by committing the sin of killing a girl child.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Her qualification is described as well was his wickedness. The word satī (chaste like Satī, Śiva’s wife) suggests that because of giving her trouble, Kaṁsa and his followers would soon be destroyed. The word devī suggests that by good fortune she was able to hide her son. But she was suffering (kṛpanā) because she feared Kaṁsa would kill the daughter of her dear friend Yaśodā. First she said “This child will be the bride of your son” in order to incite his greed. When he ignored this, since he feared this was the eighth child, and as he prepared to kill the child, she said “She is a woman. Women can do you no harm and a woman should not be killed. This is proper conduct for you. O follower of dharma! (or O long-lived brother!) She said this to extinguish his fear of death. The eighth child is a girl. You need not fear death by her.

Personal Guidance
Real karuṇā moves you to plead for another's life even when your own is safe — the soul's welfare always outweighs the demon's logic of self-interest.
Practical Applications
  • Speak up today for someone vulnerable whose suffering you could conveniently ignore — a stranger, a junior, a child — even at social cost.
  • Notice the moment māyā whispers 'this exception is justified' and refuse to cross a line you would normally hold sacred.
  • When you are personally safe, deliberately turn your attention to someone still in danger and act on their behalf rather than relaxing into your own relief.
Reflection Questions
  • Whose suffering am I willing to plead for even when I am personally safe — and whose suffering do I quietly walk past because intervening would cost me something?
  • Where in my life have I, like Kaṁsa under māyā's grip, redrawn the line of what I am willing to do, telling myself this exception is justified — and what would it take for me to see that line clearly again?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • When a friend group is about to do something cruel to someone weaker — a quieter classmate, a junior, someone online — be the Devakī in the room. Not the dramatic hero; the one who quietly says 'don't, she's somebody's sister, this isn't who we are' even when you'd be safer staying silent.
  • Notice the Kaṁsa-move in yourself: when stress spikes (exam week, breakup, group-chat pile-on), you'll be tempted to do something to a person — ghost them, screenshot them, mock them — that calmer-you would never do. Name it as māyā in the moment, and step back before you press send.
  • Pick one helpless thing in your week — a stray animal, a homesick first-year, a grandparent on a video call no one wants to take — and serve it without telling anyone. Karuṇā that costs nothing to your image is the cleanest karuṇā at this stage of life.
householder
  • Devakī, already safe, still spoke for someone else's child. Audit your household: where are you protecting only your own — your kids' school spot, your team at work, your in-group — and staying quiet when another family's child is being harmed by the same system? Speak once this week where you would normally stay silent.
  • When a family member (spouse, sibling, parent) is about to do something atrocious under pressure — fire someone unjustly, cut off a relative, sign a deal that hurts a vulnerable party — do not lecture them in dharma-jargon. Do what Devakī did: appeal relationally first ('this person is family to us in some way'), then to their own better self ('this is unworthy of you'). Plain language. Kṛpaṇā, not preachy.
  • Teach your children, by demonstration this week, the difference between real compassion and material compassion. Let them see you choose the harder, soul-serving option once — refusing a comfortable lie to a relative, declining to indulge a child's tantrum that would set a bad saṁskāra — and explain afterward, briefly, why.
senior
  • You are in the position Devakī was in after Kṛṣṇa's birth — your own primary duties largely discharged, your own 'child' safely placed. The remaining work is to plead for others' children: mentor a young householder making a Kaṁsa-style mistake, intervene gently for a grandchild whose parents are too busy to see the harm, leave behind a recorded blessing for the not-yet-born of your line.
  • Loosen attachment to being heard. Devakī's plea did not stop Kaṁsa — and yet she had to make it. At your stage, much counsel you offer will be ignored in real time and only honored after you are gone. Speak the karuṇā anyway; offer the result to Kṛṣṇa, not to whether the listener agreed.
  • Sit each evening with the verse 'striyaṁ mā hantum arhasi' — it is unworthy of you to harm the helpless — and apply it inward: which old grudge, which sharp word to the spouse of fifty years, which dismissiveness toward a caregiver, is unworthy of who you are now? Drop one such pattern this week as your offering.
Common Pitfalls
  • Mistaking Devakī's plea as mere sentimental, bodily compassion — when the deeper teaching is the natural protective instinct of a sādhu toward an innocent, even at risk to oneself.
  • Reading Kaṁsa's behavior as a distant demonic abstraction rather than recognizing the same māyā-driven escalation in oneself — where each compromise of conscience makes the next easier.
  • Using 'surrender to Kṛṣṇa's plan' as an excuse for passivity in the face of injustice, forgetting that Devakī, already secure in having delivered Kṛṣṇa, still spoke up to save another's child.
Discussion Prompts
  • Devakī appeals to Kaṁsa using two arguments — 'she will be your daughter-in-law' (a relational, future-tied appeal) and 'it is unworthy to kill a woman' (a dharmic appeal). Which of these would actually move a hardened heart, and which is closer to flattery? Share a moment when you yourself softened a request to a powerful person — were you being satya or strategic?
  • Devakī, having safely birthed Kṛṣṇa, could have stayed silent and protected her own position. Instead she risks her relationship with Kaṁsa to plead for someone else's daughter. Discuss with your partner: name a concrete situation in your present life where staying silent is safer and speaking up costs you something — and whether karuṇā actually demands you speak.
  • Kaṁsa, by the influence of māyā, was 'prepared to kill a woman — not only a woman, but a small, helpless newborn child.' Where in your own life have you watched yourself, under pressure, cross a line you previously thought you would never cross? Disagree honestly in your group: is this māyā, or is it just who we are when stakes are high?
Role Play

Two students play siblings. Sibling A has been quietly enabling a serious wrong by their elder brother (Sibling C, played by the teacher or a third student) — say, financial deception of an aging parent — because confronting him would explode the family. A newborn-equivalent has just entered the situation: a younger cousin who will inherit the consequences. Sibling A must, in front of the group, do what Devakī does: appeal to the wrongdoer directly, kṛpaṇā karuṇaṁ — helplessly, piteously — while invoking both relational ties ('she is family to you too') and dharma ('this is unworthy of you'). The class then critiques: did the appeal sound like flattery, like manipulation, or like genuine karuṇā? Where did satya almost get sacrificed to keep the peace?

Objections & Responses
  • Devakī's argument is just sexist — 'don't kill her because she's a woman.' Why should a woman's life be worth more than a man's, and why are we holding this up as a model?
    Read the verse with the purport in hand. Devakī is not constructing a universal ethics seminar; she is a terrified mother trying every door that might open in a demon's mind. Kaṁsa had previously spared her precisely on the grounds that women, especially pregnant ones, ought not to be killed — so she reaches for the one argument he has historically honored. The Gauḍīya line does not read this as a metaphysical claim that female bodies are more valuable; the soul, as BG 2.13 reminds us, is the same in every body. What the verse does honor is the practical, embodied karuṇā of a mother — and the dharmic intuition that the strong must not strike the helpless, whether infant, woman, elder, or animal. That intuition is sound. The framing is tactical.
  • If Kṛṣṇa is the supreme controller and this child's death is part of the līlā unfolding, Devakī's plea is pointless — even theologically confused. Shouldn't she just surrender and trust the plan?
    This is exactly the impersonalist drift the Gītā warns against. Surrender to Kṛṣṇa is not surrender to fatalism; it is sva-dharma performed with the fruits offered to Him (BG 2.47, BG 12.10). Devakī's sva-dharma in this moment is to plead — she is a mother and a sister, and karuṇā is acting through her. To say 'just trust the plan and stay silent' is precisely the material compassion-versus-real-compassion confusion: it dresses passivity in spiritual clothing. Arjuna tried the same move on the battlefield and Kṛṣṇa rejected it. Devakī acts; the outcome is Kṛṣṇa's. That is the model — not silence, not control, but right action with detached results.
Analogy

A mother bird, watching the hunter take aim at her chick, throws her own wing across the nest. Devakī's plea is that wing — not strategic, not eloquent, only the body of love standing between cruelty and the helpless. Kṛṣṇa later remembers all such karuṇā; the gopīs' tears for Him spring from this same well.

Stories
Kuntī pleading for the Pāṇḍavas

When Duryodhana's plots had nearly consumed her sons, Queen Kuntī did not lecture or scheme — she folded her hands and prayed to Kṛṣṇa: 'Let calamities come again and again, so that we shall see You again and again' (SB 1.8.25). The devotee's response to threat is appeal, not aggression.

Devakī's words to Kaṁsa here are the same posture. She names him 'brother', invokes auspiciousness on him even as he stands ready to strike, and shields the female child with the only weapon a saintly woman keeps — speech soaked in karuṇā. The Gauḍīya tradition reads such pleas as the very fragrance of bhakti.
