Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.14

Important Words
  • ākarṇya — after hearing
    Gerund of ā-√karṇ — to take into the ear. The Lord's potency speaks, and Kaṁsa actually listens for once. Hearing — śravaṇa — is the first limb of bhakti; here it produces a flicker of right action.
  • parama-vismitaḥ — supremely struck with wonder
    Wonder (vismaya) is the sthāyi-bhāva of adbhuta-rasa. Even in an asura, a moment of wonder can soften the heart. The Gauḍīya tradition treats wonder before Bhagavān's līlā as one of the doorways into bhāva.
  • vimucya — having released (them)
    Gerund of vi-√muc — to free, to release. The literal action: he opens Devakī's and Vasudeva's shackles. The Gauḍīya echo: hearing Bhagavān's message produces real liberation; Kaṁsa's gesture is a faint figure of mokṣa.
  • praśritaḥ — humbly
    From pra-śri — bent, bowed, humble. The arrogant king bends. Humility before the Lord's potency is the asura's only chance; if Kaṁsa had stayed in this posture, he would have been saved.
  • abravīt — he spoke
    Simple verb — yet the contrast is everything. Where before he chastised (vinirbhartsya, v.7), now he speaks (abravīt) humbly. One bhāva, one verb-change away from the path of return.
After hearing the words of the goddess Durgā, Kaṁsa was struck with wonder. Thus he approached his sister Devakī and brother-in-law Vasudeva, released them immediately from their shackles, and very humbly spoke as follows.
Purport

Kaṁsa was astonished because the goddess Durgā had become the daughter of Devakī. Since Devakī was a human being, how could the goddess Durgā become her daughter? This was one cause of his astonishment. Also, how is it that the eighth child of Devakī was a female? This also astonished him. Asuras are generally devotees of mother Durgā, Śakti, or of demigods, especially Lord Śiva. The appearance of Durgā in her original eight-armed feature, holding various weapons, immediately changed Kaṁsa’s mind about Devakī’s being an ordinary human. Devakī must have had some transcendental qualities; otherwise why would the goddess Durgā have taken birth from her womb? Under the circumstances, Kaṁsa, struck with wonder, wanted to compensate for his atrocities against his sister Devakī.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
After hearing the words of the goddess Durgā, Kaṁsa was struck with wonder. Thus he approached his sister Devakī and brother-in-law Vasudeva, released them immediately from their shackles, and very humbly spoke as follows.
Commentary

Kamsa was astonished. "How could mortal Devaki give birth to Durga devi? How could Durga’s words be false?"

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

He released them from the prison. The affix vi signifies viśeṣa, special. He released them from prison first and then took off their chains, since he felt somewhat uncomfortable doing this in front of the guards. He released Devakī first since she was a woman, since she was his sister and since she suffered more. Hearing Durgā’s words, he released Devakī and Vasudeva and with humility spoke to them. He was astonished by Durgā’s sudden display of power and her words.

Personal Guidance
When the truth of the divine arrangement breaks through, even a hardened heart pauses — wonder can soften us into humility and right action.
Practical Applications
  • Notice one place today where reality has refused to fit your fearful narrative, and let that wonder loosen your grip.
  • Where you have wronged someone close to you, stop justifying and take one concrete step today to release them — words, apology, or restitution.
  • Refuse the asura's habit of bargaining with Śakti for protection; bow instead before Kṛṣṇa, whose plan even Durgā serves.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my life have I, like Kaṁsa, only softened toward someone after a display of power rather than out of genuine daya — and what does that reveal about the quality of my compassion?
  • What 'shackles' have I placed on people close to me through my ahaṅkāra, and what would it cost me to release them today, before any cosmic correction forces my hand?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Notice the next time you 'apologize' to a parent, teacher, or friend only because you got caught — exam results came back, screen time got checked, a group chat leaked. That is Kaṁsa-praśrita: humble words, unchanged heart. Today, pick one such situation and apologize again, this time without the fear motivating it.
  • Asuras worship Durgā and demigods for results; devotees surrender to Kṛṣṇa Himself. Audit your scrolling for one day: how much of your 'spirituality' on social media is actually demigod-style bargaining (manifest this, attract that, hack your dopamine) versus direct remembrance of Kṛṣṇa? Unfollow three accounts that pull you into the first category.
  • Kaṁsa's humility lasted hours. Pick one resolution you made after a scare — a bad grade, a friendship blow-up, a public embarrassment — and check whether you are still keeping it. If not, do not re-resolve in panic; instead, offer the next attempt to Kṛṣṇa as sādhana, not as self-improvement.
householder
  • Examine one ongoing family grievance — with a sibling, in-law, or business partner — where you, like Kaṁsa, have been technically in the right and emotionally cruel. Today, do the unshackling: return what you have been withholding (a call, a sum of money, an acknowledgment) before any wonder forces your hand.
  • Kaṁsa's repentance was real for an hour and false by morning because it was rooted in viṣaya-fear, not in Kṛṣṇa. Examine your own household resolutions — about screen time with the kids, about how you speak to your spouse when tired, about money — and ask which are wonder-driven (after a fight, a scare, a sermon) and which are anchored in daily sādhana. Move one resolution from the first column to the second this week.
  • Devakī and Vasudeva accept release without bitterness, having lost six children. In your own marriage and parenting, where are you still collecting interest on an old wound — a thing your spouse said five years ago, a way your child once disappointed you? Today, formally release one such 'shackle,' offering the resentment to Kṛṣṇa rather than re-litigating it at dinner.
senior
  • At this stage, the people who have wronged you are aging too, and some are arriving — like Kaṁsa — with late, frightened apologies. Decide in advance, before the next such conversation, how you will receive it: with daya for the soul, with realism about the body, without needing the apology to be perfect for your peace to be intact.
  • Kaṁsa's wonder did not save him because it was not surrender. As mortality comes into closer view, examine whether your own spiritual life is wonder-driven (intensifying around scares, scans, funerals) or steadily rooted in daily remembrance of Kṛṣṇa. Establish or deepen one fixed daily practice — japa, reading, ārati — that does not depend on fear to sustain it.
  • You have spent decades shackling and being shackled — by roles, expectations, grievances, possessions. Choose one shackle this week and unlock it deliberately: forgive a debt, write a will-clarification, hand over a responsibility to a younger family member, release a resentment in writing. Do it as vairāgya offered to Kṛṣṇa, not as estate planning.
Common Pitfalls
  • Mistaking Kaṁsa's wonder-struck humility for true śraddhā — awe at a śakti display is not the same as surrender to Kṛṣṇa as the Supreme Person; students drift into Durgā- or demigod-centered devotion thinking it is equivalent.
  • Reading the 'release from shackles' as a permanent change of heart and concluding that fear-based or wonder-based reform is sufficient — Kaṁsa's later behavior shows that without rooting out ahaṅkāra, contrition collapses back into atrocity.
  • Using the verse to justify material compassion — 'I removed their physical bondage, so I have done my duty' — while ignoring that real daya means serving the soul, not merely relieving the body's discomfort.
Discussion Prompts
  • Kaṁsa releases Devakī and Vasudeva only after he is struck with wonder by Durgā — not because his heart softened on its own. Share with your partner: when has fear or astonishment, rather than genuine compassion, made you behave well? Was that change real, or cosmetic? Where is the line?
  • Prabhupāda points out that asuras are typically devotees of Durgā and Śiva, not of Kṛṣṇa directly. Discuss: in your own life, where do you find yourself approaching the Supreme through intermediaries — spirituality-as-self-improvement, demigod-style bargains, transactional prayer — rather than direct surrender to Kṛṣṇa? Push back on each other's easy answers.
  • Kaṁsa offers humility and apology in this verse, yet within hours he reverses and resumes killing infants. Debate in your group: is Kaṁsa's praśrita (humble) speech here genuine sva-dharma stirring in him, or is it tāmasic calculation dressed up as repentance? How would you tell the difference in someone you know — or in yourself?
Role Play

Two students pair up. Student A plays a relative who has wronged Student B for years — withheld inheritance, spread rumours, blocked an opportunity. Suddenly A has had a frightening experience (a health scare, a vivid dream, a near-accident) and now arrives at B's door, visibly shaken, releasing the 'shackles' — returning what was taken, apologizing humbly, asking forgiveness. Student B must respond in real time: do you accept at face value, like Devakī and Vasudeva initially did? Do you probe whether this is wonder-driven panic or real change of heart? Do you forgive without trusting? After 5 minutes, swap roles. Then debrief: what did Kaṁsa's later relapse teach you about how to receive such an apology with both daya and discrimination?

Objections & Responses
  • This whole episode — a goddess flying out of a baby, a tyrant suddenly humbled — reads like mythology. Why should a modern, rational person take practical guidance from it?
    Set aside the cosmology for a moment and look at the human mechanics, which are exact. A man in power has been crushing a vulnerable family for years, justifying it by a prophecy. The instant a force larger than him intervenes, his behavior flips — humility, apology, release of the prisoners. Then, when the fear fades, he relapses. That arc is not mythological; it is the most common shape of human 'repentance' you will ever observe — in addicts, in abusers, in yourself after a near-miss. The Bhāgavatam preserves it because it is diagnostic. The question the verse forces on us is not 'did Durgā literally appear?' but 'is my own change of heart wonder-driven and temporary, or is it rooted in surrender to Kṛṣṇa and therefore stable?' That is a question a rational person cannot afford to dismiss.
  • Devakī and Vasudeva were just released from prison after years of having their babies murdered. The verse moves on as if a humble apology settles the matter. Isn't this spiritually irresponsible — telling victims to accept the abuser's tears?
    The verse is not prescribing what victims should do; it is reporting what happened, and the next several verses show Kaṁsa relapsing precisely so we will not draw that lesson. Gauḍīya teaching distinguishes real compassion — serving the soul — from material sentimentality that papers over ongoing harm. Devakī and Vasudeva accept the release because they are devotees who see Kṛṣṇa's hand in every turn, not because they are naïve. We are not asked to imitate their outward behavior; we are asked to imitate their inward steadiness — the anahankārah that does not need revenge to feel whole, and the śraddhā that trusts Kṛṣṇa's protection more than the persecutor's promises. Forgiveness of the soul, vigilance about the body: both, together. That is the Gauḍīya pivot, and it is the opposite of spiritual bypassing.
Analogy

A bolt of lightning across a dark hall shows the king for a moment all the furniture he has been knocking over for years. Yoga-māyā's words are that lightning. Kaṁsa, struck with wonder, sees — briefly — what his rage has been doing. He even releases the shackles. The verse asks: do we wait for our own thunderclap, or remember Kṛṣṇa before it strikes?

Stories
Bharata's wonder at the deer

King Bharata, who had renounced his kingdom to meditate on the Lord, became attached to a fawn whose mother had died. Through that attachment he was bound to another birth as a deer, and only at the moment of leaving that body did he wake in wonder, remembering his fall and resolving never again to be deluded (SB 5.8–9).

Kaṁsa's parama-vismita here is a lesser, more flickering cousin of Bharata's awakening. Wonder is one of bhakti's possible doorways — adbhuta-rasa can soften a stone heart for a moment. Whether one walks through that doorway or lets it close again is the difference between Bharata and Kaṁsa.
