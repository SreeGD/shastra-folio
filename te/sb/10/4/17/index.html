Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.17

దైవమప్యనృతం వక్తి న మర్త్యా ఏవ కేవలమ్ । యద్విశ్రమ్భాదహం పాప: స్వసుర్నిహతవాఞ్ఛిశూన్ ॥ ౧౭ ॥

Important Words
  • daivam — fate / providence / a celestial voice
    Here specifically the bodiless prophecy. Daivam is the Lord's external arrangement (māyā-śakti as karma); it tells truth, but partial truth. Only paramātmā-vāṇī through guru-paramparā tells the whole.
  • anṛtam — untruth
    Literally 'non-truth'. Anṛta is the opposite of ṛta (cosmic truth-order). Kaṁsa charges daiva itself with anṛta — a startling claim, but the Bhāgavatam lets it stand because partial truths function as lies when wielded by the unsurrendered.
  • martyāḥ — mortals
    Those subject to death. Kaṁsa contrasts mortal liars with the celestial liar; the verse's irony is that he himself is now firmly a martya, an upcoming corpse, and his self-deceptions have been more damaging than any celestial deception.
  • yat-viśrambhāt — by trust in which
    Ablative compound: 'because of trust in which'. The fault is not in the daiva but in the misplaced viśrambha. Trust must be calibrated to the source; the asura trusts vāṇīs but not Vaiṣṇavas — exactly inverted.
  • pāpaḥ — sinful (I)
    Adjective for Kaṁsa himself. He calls himself pāpa — the second self-naming in this section (after pāpmanā in v.15). The repetition is honest; but again, without Bhagavān's Name, the confession does not become cleansing.
Alas, not only human beings but sometimes even providence lies. And I am so sinful that I believed the omen of providence and killed so many of my sister’s children.
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Alas, not only human beings but sometimes even providence lies. And I am so sinful that I believed the omen of providence and killed so many of my sister's children.
Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Not only am I at fault. The cause is the devatās. The devatās lie. There is a contradiction to the śruti statement satyam eva devā amrṭam manuṣyāḥ: the devatās are truthful and humans lie. Humans are not so sinful by lying (eva). I am sinful. Otherwise why should I believe her words?

Śrīdhara Svāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

The fault does not lie with me alone – it lies with the devatas too. The devatās lie. Not only humans lie. Marteṣv anṛtaṁ pratiṣṭhitam: lying is fixed in mortals. Because of this even devatās can lie. I have committed sin or am cruel (pāpaḥ).

Concepts
Jiva
Personal Guidance
Even celestial omens can mislead — only Kṛṣṇa's word is fully trustworthy; reckless faith in lesser signs breeds grievous regret.
Practical Applications
  • Notice when you justify a harsh action by appealing to a 'sign' or omen, and pause to test it against Kṛṣṇa's direct instruction in śāstra.
  • Refuse to outsource grave moral choices to horoscopes, hunches, or 'providence' — bring them before guru, sādhu, and śāstra today.
  • Speak honestly to one person you have wronged under the cover of being 'right,' and own the ahaṅkāra that drove the act.
Reflection Questions
  • When my plans collapse, do I quietly blame 'fate' or 'circumstance' to soften my own ahaṅkāra — and whose suffering have I caused while hiding behind that omen?
  • Whose voice or sign have I been treating as infallible providence to justify actions my own conscience already flagged — and what would it cost me to face that I, the doer, am responsible?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Notice the next time you justify a harsh action toward a friend or sibling by saying 'but they said' or 'the group chat said' — that is your daiva-voice. Pause and ask whether you would have done it without that cover.
  • When an exam result, a rejection, or a follower count makes you feel betrayed by 'the universe,' resist the Kaṁsa move of blaming providence; instead write down, plainly, what you actually wanted and why you outsourced the decision to a sign.
  • Today, apologize to one person without using the word 'but' and without bringing in any third party (parents, peers, circumstances) — practice the anāhaṅkāra Kaṁsa cannot manage.
householder
  • Audit one major family or career decision from the last year where you said 'we had no choice' — name the actual people harmed or set aside, and own your part without invoking market conditions, doctors, or in-laws as your providence.
  • Before disciplining your children this week, check whether you are acting on dharma or on a 'forecast' (their future failure, what neighbors will say); the same false-daiva that misled Kaṁsa misleads anxious parents.
  • When money or career news 'lies' to you — a deal collapses, a promotion vanishes — formally place the result at Kṛṣṇa's feet that same evening, before you let the household absorb your blame-shifting.
senior
  • Review the regrets you carry into this stage of life and ask, honestly, how many begin with 'I was told' or 'it seemed meant to be'; bring those to japa rather than to repeated retelling, where they harden into Kaṁsa-like self-pity.
  • When mentoring younger family members, refuse to pass on the verdicts of 'fate' you once accepted — speak instead of where surrender to Kṛṣṇa would have served you better than obedience to omens, status, or expert opinion.
  • As mortality approaches, practice loosening śraddhā in every proxy — horoscopes, prognoses, legacy plans — and place it directly in Kṛṣṇa, so that when the body's own 'providence' seems to lie, you do not die rehearsing Kaṁsa's complaint.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading Kaṁsa's remorse as a model of repentance — it is the lament of a frightened doer, not śaraṇāgati; do not confuse fear-of-consequence with surrender to Kṛṣṇa.
  • Using 'even providence lies' as license for cynicism toward śraddhā itself — the verse exposes a demon's misreading of an omen, not a defect in the Lord or in genuine guru-śāstra-sādhu.
  • Outsourcing nyāya to omens, astrology, or 'signs' to avoid the harder work of discrimination — and then, like Kaṁsa, lamenting the harm only after the children are already slain.
Discussion Prompts
  • Kaṁsa blames 'providence' for misleading him into killing his sister's children. Share with your partner: when have you blamed fate, an omen, an astrologer, or 'a sign' for a choice you made — and was that blame honest, or a way to dodge ahaṅkāra-driven action you had already decided on?
  • Kaṁsa's repentance arises only after he believes the prophecy was wrong — i.e., from relief, not from real sorrow over the children. Debate in pairs: is remorse that comes from changed circumstances real repentance at all, or only its counterfeit? Where in your own life have you confused the two?
  • The verse exposes a man who placed śraddhā in a celestial voice rather than in Kṛṣṇa, and was 'rewarded' with the deaths of innocents. Discuss: in our age, what are the equivalent 'daiva voices' — algorithms, news, gurus, intuitions — that we trust enough to commit harm for? Where do we and our group disagree about which voices deserve śraddhā?
Role Play

Two students play Kaṁsa and Devakī in the moments after this verse is spoken. Kaṁsa, having just been told the eighth child has been hidden away, walks into the prison cell and offers this 'apology' to his sister — claiming providence lied, claiming his own sinfulness, asking forgiveness. Devakī must decide, in real time, whether to accept the words at face value, expose the self-pity beneath them, or stay silent. After three minutes, swap roles. The teacher then asks each pair: where did the apology slip from contrition into self-justification, and how could you tell?

Objections & Responses
  • Kaṁsa is at least taking responsibility — he calls himself pāpaḥ. Why are we treating his confession with suspicion instead of honoring it as a moment of genuine moral growth?
    Look closely at the grammar of his confession. He says 'I am sinful that I believed providence' — the sin he names is gullibility, not infanticide. He still treats the children as collateral in a cosmic miscalculation. A Gauḍīya teacher reads this as the classic pattern of the asura: self-criticism deployed as a fresh layer of self-defense. Real anāhaṅkāra would not need to first declare daiva a liar; it would simply mourn the children. The verse is preserved precisely so we can learn to recognize this counterfeit in ourselves — the apology that protects the ego it pretends to dismantle. True nyāya, righteous judgment, begins with refusing to grant ourselves the discount Kaṁsa grants himself.
  • The whole episode hinges on a prophecy that any modern person finds embarrassing — a disembodied voice predicting a killer. Doesn't that make this verse a relic, useful only as mythology, not as guidance for our lives?
    Set the celestial voice aside for a moment and notice the structure: a man receives information he believes is authoritative, acts on it with violence against the vulnerable, and later — when the information appears to fail — calls the source itself a liar rather than examining his own use of it. That structure is not mythological; it is the daily news cycle, the boardroom decision, the family quarrel justified by 'I was told.' The prophecy is the verse's mirror: whatever you treat as your daiva — market data, a doctor's prognosis, a trusted teacher, your own gut — will eventually seem to lie, and in that moment you will discover whether your real śraddhā was in Kṛṣṇa or in the proxy. The Gauḍīya teacher's point is not that omens speak; it is that surrender (BG 18.66) was always the only reliable refuge, and Kaṁsa's tragedy is that he never sought it.
Analogy

A traveler trusts a forest signpost that turns out to be the hand of a thief. Kaṁsa trusted an asarīrī-vāṇī, a bodiless voice — a daivam — and on its word he slaughtered. The Bhāgavatam pointedly says even daiva can deceive when one has no proper guru. Only Bhagavān's word, transmitted through paramparā, is wholly trustworthy.

Stories
Yudhiṣṭhira and the Yakṣa's pond

When the Pāṇḍavas were thirsty in exile, four brothers in turn drank from a pond ignoring the Yakṣa's warning, and four fell dead. Only Yudhiṣṭhira paused, answered the Yakṣa's questions humbly, and saved his brothers. The lesson the Mahābhārata draws: do not act on voices, omens, or signs without testing them against dharma and a higher authority.

Kaṁsa here laments that he trusted a daivam — the prophecy that Devakī's eighth child would kill him. The voice was real; his interpretation and his response were not. The Gauḍīya tradition insists on guru-śāstra-sādhu as the threefold lens; without it, even truth can be misused, as Kaṁsa misused this one.
