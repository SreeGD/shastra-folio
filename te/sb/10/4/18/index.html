Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.18

Important Words
  • mā śocatam — do not lament
    Imperative dual — addressed to both Devakī and Vasudeva. Same instruction Kṛṣṇa gives Arjuna (BG 2.27, 2.30). But spoken by Kaṁsa it is rhetoric; spoken by Bhagavān it is the truth that frees.
  • mahā-bhāgau — O most fortunate ones (dual)
    Vocative dual. Kaṁsa names them as great-fortuned — and unwittingly speaks the truth: they are great-fortuned, because Kṛṣṇa Himself is their son. The asura speaks better than he knows.
  • sva-kṛtam — what is done by oneself
    Compound — one's own karma. Each soul reaps its own action. The Gauḍīya frame: karma is real but not final; bhakti can override it, and only bhakti.
  • daiva-adhīnāḥ — subject to fate / providence
    Compound: 'under-the-rule-of daiva'. Conditioned souls are under daiva; the surrendered devotee is under Bhagavān directly. The verse states the general law; the chapter so far has been showing exceptions to it.
  • ekatra — in one place / together
    Adverb. Bodies cannot remain ekatra forever; the souls behind them are eternal, but the assemblage of one body around them is brief. Cherish the togetherness as a temporary gift from Bhagavān.
O great souls, your children have suffered their own misfortune. Therefore, please do not lament for them. All living entities are under the control of the Supreme, and they cannot always live together.
Purport

Kaṁsa addressed his sister and brother-in-law as mahā-bhāgau because although he killed their ordinary children, the goddess Durgā took birth from them. Because Devakī bore Durgādevī in her womb, Kaṁsa praised both Devakī and her husband. Asuras are very devoted to the goddess Durgā, Kālī and so forth. Kaṁsa, therefore, truly astonished, appreciated the exalted position of his sister and brother-in-law. Durgā is certainly not under the laws of nature, because she herself is the controller of the laws of nature. Ordinary living beings, however, are controlled by these laws ( prakṛteḥ kriyamāṇāni guṇaiḥ karmāṇi sarvaśaḥ ). Consequently, none of us are allowed to live together for any long period. By speaking in this way, Kaṁsa tried to pacify his sister and brother-in-law.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
O great souls, your children have suffered their own misfortune. Therefore, please do not lament for them. All living entities are under the control of the Supreme, and they cannot always live together.
Commentary

"You are very fortunate (maha bhagau) because Durgadevi has come as your daughter. You should not lament for your sons who have reaped the results of their actions. Even considering that they live, living entities cannot stay together for very long."

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

You two are most fortunate to understand the truth! No jīvas stay in one place at all times. Because of hatred and other faults they come together and separate, going to different planets according to their karmas.

Concepts
Jiva
Personal Guidance
Bodies meet and part by Kṛṣṇa's daiva — grieve less for the perishable form, and serve the eternal soul within those entrusted to you today.
Practical Applications
  • Name one relationship where you are clinging to physical togetherness, and offer that bond to Kṛṣṇa instead of to time.
  • Speak to a family member today as a soul on loan from Kṛṣṇa — not as a permanent possession of yours.
  • Refuse the asura's pacification — false consolation that flatters; seek instead the dhīra's steadiness rooted in daivādhīna.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my own grief over a loss have I quietly assumed that I, rather than the daiva (Supreme will), am the rightful keeper of those I love?
  • Whose company am I clinging to as if it were permanent — and what does the verse's 'na sadaikatra' (never always together) ask me to release today?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • When a friend group fractures mid-semester or a relationship ends, sit with the truth that no human grouping is permanent — daivādhīna — without using that truth as an excuse to ghost people or skip the hard goodbye.
  • Watch out for the 'Kaṁsa move' on social media: people quoting karma or 'everything happens for a reason' under a tragedy post to avoid actually feeling anything. Notice when you do it; write the comment, then delete it, and just sit with the loss.
  • If you've hurt someone — a roommate, a sibling, an ex — do not reach for spiritual-sounding logic ('it was meant to be,' 'their karma') to skip apology. Real samata begins after you've owned your part.
householder
  • When making family decisions about aging parents, schools, or job moves that will scatter the household, remember jāntavo na sadaikatra — togetherness is on loan. Plan with care; grasp without panic.
  • If you lose a pregnancy, a parent, or a job, refuse the two extremes: collapsing into lamentation as if you were the controller, and bypassing grief with pat philosophy. Grieve in front of Kṛṣṇa — offer the tears, then return to duty.
  • Audit the 'consolations' you offer your spouse and children. Are you, like Kaṁsa, using correct words to manage their feelings and protect yourself? Practical compassion is sitting with their pain, not explaining it away.
senior
  • Each separation now — a friend's death, children moving abroad, your own narrowing world — is a rehearsal for the final separation. Use them: loosen one grip a day, mentally offering the relationship back to Kṛṣṇa, who is its real owner.
  • When younger family members grieve, resist the elder's temptation to deliver Kaṁsa-style wisdom from above. Sit lower than them in the room. Speak only after listening; sometimes do not speak at all.
  • Make your legacy a teaching of daivādhīna by example, not lecture: how you handle your own diagnoses, downsizing, and dependencies will instruct your grandchildren more than any verse you quote.
Common Pitfalls
  • Weaponizing 'daivādhīna' to dismiss others' grief — telling a mourning friend 'it was destiny' instead of sitting with them in compassion as a fellow soul under the Supreme's control.
  • Imitating Kaṁsa's pacifying rhetoric: using the language of surrender to the Supreme to excuse harm we have caused, rather than to genuinely soften our own attachment.
  • Confusing 'they suffered their own karma' with cold fatalism — letting the truth of sva-kṛtaṁ bhujaḥ (reaping one's own acts) numb the heart rather than humble it before Kṛṣṇa's control.
Discussion Prompts
  • Kaṁsa, the very man who just smashed their newborns, now consoles Devakī and Vasudeva with seemingly correct philosophy — 'living entities cannot always live together, all are under the Supreme.' Where in your own life have you used (or heard others use) accurate-sounding spiritual logic to evade responsibility for harm? How do we tell the difference between genuine samata and self-serving rationalization?
  • The verse leans on daivādhīna — that beings are under the control of the Supreme and cannot remain together. To one student this lands as liberating (loosening attachment); to another it sounds fatalistic (denying grief, denying agency). Take opposite sides honestly with your partner and argue it out — does daivādhīna comfort the bereaved or silence them?
  • Prabhupāda points out that asuras are devoted to Durgā and Kālī, and Kaṁsa's astonishment at Devakī producing the goddess pivots his whole speech. What does it mean that demoniac mentality can be deeply 'religious' and even worship powerful deities, yet remain demoniac? Where do we see this in our own circles — religiosity without surrender to Kṛṣṇa?
Role Play

One student plays a hospice chaplain visiting a family whose young child has just died. Another plays the grieving parent. A third plays an older relative who, wanting to help, offers exactly Kaṁsa's line: 'Don't lament — they suffered their own karma; living beings can't stay together forever; it was God's will.' The chaplain must respond — not by contradicting the philosophy (which is technically correct) but by discerning when such words serve the soul and when they bypass real grief. After five minutes, swap: now the relative is sincere rather than deflecting. Does the same sentence land differently? Why?

Objections & Responses
  • This verse essentially says 'your kids died because of their own karma, so stop crying.' That's victim-blaming dressed up as theology — and it lets Kaṁsa, the actual murderer, off the hook. Why should I respect a tradition that puts these words in a hero's mouth?
    Notice who is speaking: Kaṁsa, the asura. Vyāsa is not endorsing the speech as pastoral counsel; he is exposing how an asura uses true principles falsely. Karma and daivādhīna are real, but they are meant to steady the sufferer, never to absolve the perpetrator. Kṛṣṇa Himself takes birth precisely to remove Kaṁsa — the universe does not let him 'off the hook.' Prabhupāda's purport flags Kaṁsa's flattery and his asuric devotion to Durgā, signaling that the philosophy is correct but the mouth is foul. The Gauḍīya reading is: receive the truth, reject the manipulator.
  • Saying everyone is under daiva and cannot stay together sounds like a passive, fatalistic worldview. Doesn't this discourage medicine, justice, building families — anything that resists separation and death?
    Daivādhīna is not fatalism; it is the recognition that ultimate outcomes are not in our hands, while right action absolutely is. Kṛṣṇa in the Gītā commands Arjuna to fight — not to shrug. We treat the patient, raise the child, pursue the just cause, and we do not consider ourselves the cause of results (BG 2.47). What this verse loosens is not effort but the illusion of control and the despair of permanent togetherness. A Vaiṣṇava grieves — Devakī and Vasudeva grieve — and still acts in sva-dharma, knowing Kṛṣṇa is the controller, not Kaṁsa, and not us.
Analogy

Travelers in a caravan walk together for a stretch of road, share food at a wayside, and then each turns into his own lane at evening. Bodies are these traveling companions — assembled by daiva, parted by daiva. Grieve less for the lane that bends away; love instead the soul that walks beside you for as long as Bhagavān grants the road.

Stories
Yamarāja's instruction to the widows

In the Bhāgavatam (Canto 7), Yamarāja consoles a group of mourning widows by telling them the story of the soul taking many bodies, like a passenger changing carriages. He concludes with the verse na hanyate hanyamāne śarīre — the soul is not slain when the body is slain (echoing Gītā 2.20).

Kaṁsa here, ironically, gives nearly the same instruction to Devakī and Vasudeva. The teaching is correct sāṅkhya; what is missing — and what Yamarāja delivers and Kaṁsa does not — is that mere knowledge of the soul's indestructibility, without surrender to Kṛṣṇa, does not bring peace. Kaṁsa preaches the medicine he refuses to swallow.
