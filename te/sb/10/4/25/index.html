Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.25

When Devakī saw her brother actually repentant while explaining ordained events, she was relieved of all anger. Similarly, Vasudeva was also free from anger. Smiling, he spoke to Kaṁsa as follows.
Purport

Devakī and Vasudeva, both highly elevated personalities, accepted the truth presented by Kaṁsa that everything is ordained by providence. According to the prophecy, Kaṁsa would be killed by the eighth child of Devakī. Therefore, Vasudeva and Devakī saw that behind all these incidents was a great plan devised by the Supreme Personality of Godhead. Because the Lord had already taken birth, just like a human child, and was in the safe custody of Yaśodā, everything was happening according to plan, and there was no need to continue their ill feeling toward Kaṁsa. Thus they accepted Kaṁsa’s words.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
When Devakī saw her brother actually repentant while explaining ordained events, she was relieved of all anger. Similarly, Vasudeva was also free from anger. Smiling, he spoke to Kaṁsa as follows.
Commentary

Forgiving Kamsa for his offenses, Devaki gave up her anger and lamentation. (rosam ca indicates lamentation as well as anger.)

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

She tolerated the wicked acts of her repentant brother and gave up anger and lamentation. Ca represents lamentation. The other version has kṣānta-roṣā (pacified anger). Vasudeva spoke while smiling. The word ha means “clearly.” He smiled because of the words he would speak.

Concepts
Jiva
Personal Guidance
When another sincerely repents and providence becomes visible, lay down anger — Kṛṣṇa's plan was already in motion before your grievance.
Practical Applications
  • Identify one person you are still resenting, and today release the anger by recognizing the larger plan of Kṛṣṇa already at work behind the offense.
  • When someone offers genuine repentance, receive it without re-litigating the wound — meet contrition with the smile Vasudeva offered Kaṁsa.
  • Refuse to nurse a grievance once its conditions have passed; ask whether continued ill feeling is serving the soul or only feeding ahaṅkāra.
Reflection Questions
  • When someone who has wronged me shows genuine repentance, what hidden grievance do I keep nursing instead of releasing — and what is that grip costing my own bhakti?
  • Where am I failing to see the Lord's ordained plan behind a painful event, and what would change in my conduct today if I trusted that Kṛṣṇa is already arranging the outcome?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • When a friend who wronged you (ghosted, talked behind your back, leaked a screenshot) finally apologizes and means it — practice releasing the inner anger today, even if you do not yet rebuild the friendship to its old level. Roṣa-tyāga is not the same as trust-restoration.
  • Notice the social-media reflex to keep receipts and stay 'justifiably' angry. Pick one grudge from this term — a teacher, a classmate, a parent — and ask whether holding it is actually serving your soul, or just feeding ahaṅkāra.
  • Before your next argument with a sibling or roommate, try Vasudeva's move: smile first, then speak. See whether the conversation lands differently when roṣa is not driving the words.
householder
  • When your spouse, in-law, or business partner finally admits a fault you have been waiting years to hear acknowledged — receive it without extracting more. Devakī did not make Kaṁsa grovel; she let the repentance be enough for the inner release, while keeping practical wisdom about what comes next.
  • In a family conflict this week — about money, a child's schooling, an elder's care — name out loud where you can see Kṛṣṇa's hand arranging events you did not choose. Let that recognition cool the household's roṣa before the next decision is made.
  • Distinguish, in your own parenting, between protecting your child's body (material compassion, necessary) and protecting your child's soul (real compassion, primary). Do not let one quietly replace the other when the two come into conflict.
senior
  • Identify one long-held grievance — a sibling, a former colleague, a child who disappointed you — that you have been carrying toward the end of this life. Today, in front of your iṣṭa-deva, formally release the roṣa. You do not have to call them; you have to free your own heart before you leave the body.
  • When mentoring a younger person who has wronged someone and is now repentant, model Vasudeva: smile, speak from steadiness, and frame the events in terms of providence — not to excuse the wrong, but to lift the conversation out of the cycle of blame and into sva-dharma.
  • Use the contemplation of your own approaching death to loosen the last knots of ahaṅkāra around old injuries. The dhīra leaves the body without unfinished roṣa; make that your sādhana for this season.
Common Pitfalls
  • Mistaking Devakī and Vasudeva's release of anger for spineless tolerance of wrongdoing — they forgave because they perceived providence, not because offense doesn't matter.
  • Using 'everything is ordained' as a fatalistic shrug to excuse one's own negligence, when the verse actually models alert, intelligent acceptance by elevated devotees who had already done their part.
  • Demanding repentance from offenders before letting go of resentment, rather than cultivating the vinaya and apaiśunam that allow the heart to soften the moment sincerity appears.
Discussion Prompts
  • Devakī and Vasudeva drop their anger the moment Kaṁsa shows real repentance — even though he just murdered six of their infants. Is this forgiveness premature? Where is the line between recognizing providence and excusing harm? Take a position with your partner and defend it.
  • The purport says Vasudeva and Devakī accepted that 'everything is ordained by providence.' Does this collapse human responsibility? If Kaṁsa was an instrument of providence, was he still culpable? Argue both sides — don't settle on a tidy synthesis.
  • Vasudeva *smiles* before speaking to the man who killed his children. Is that smile bhakti-realism or bhakti-bypass? Share a moment when you smiled past a real wound, and whether that was vairāgya or avoidance.
Role Play

Two students play estranged siblings. Sibling A (the 'Kaṁsa') has, over years, repeatedly hurt Sibling B (the 'Devakī') — broken promises about caring for an aging parent, financial betrayals, public humiliations. Sibling A now shows up at Sibling B's home, visibly broken, says: 'I see now. I was driven by things I didn't understand. There is a larger plan I was fighting against. Please forgive me.' Sibling B must decide, in real time, whether to soften, what to say, and what (if anything) to require before reconciling. A third student plays a Gauḍīya elder watching the scene and, after five minutes, intervenes to coach both sides — distinguishing real compassion (serving the soul) from material compassion (smoothing over surface tension), and naming where surrender to providence helps versus where it would be premature.

Objections & Responses
  • This verse normalizes letting abusers off the hook the moment they cry. Modern psychology tells us repentance without accountability is manipulation — Kaṁsa goes on to attempt more murders later in the story. Devakī and Vasudeva were naive.
    Notice what they did and did not do. They released their roṣa — the inner burning — because they recognized the hand of providence behind the events. They did not, however, hand the child back, abandon vigilance, or pretend Kaṁsa was now safe. Kṛṣṇa was already in Yaśodā's safe custody; the protection was already arranged. Gauḍīya teachers distinguish *kṣānti* (the cessation of inner anger that corrodes the heart) from *pramāda* (negligent trust that ignores reality). Devakī forgave the man and still understood the demon. That is dhīra-bhāva, not naïveté. For us, the application is precise: drop the inner roṣa, which is yours to release; do not drop the discernment, which is your duty to keep.
  • Saying 'everything is ordained by providence' is just a religious version of 'everything happens for a reason' — it's a coping mechanism, not a truth. It also lets people avoid the hard work of grief and justice.
    There is a difference between a sentimental slogan and the specific Gauḍīya teaching here. Devakī and Vasudeva are not chanting a platitude to numb themselves; they are recognizing that Kṛṣṇa, the supreme Personality of Godhead, is the actual orchestrator behind apparent chaos — and that recognition is grounded in a personal relationship with Him, not an impersonal Fate. This is why the verse pivots to Vasudeva *speaking* to Kaṁsa: surrender to providence does not silence him, it gives him the steadiness to respond rightly. Grief and justice are not bypassed; they are placed in their proper frame. The cope says, 'don't feel.' The śāstra says, 'feel truly, then act from steadiness, knowing whose plan this is.'
