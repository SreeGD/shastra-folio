Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.33

While being pierced by your arrows, which you discharged on all sides, some of them, who were injured by the multitude of arrows but who desired to live, fled the battlefield, intent on escaping.
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
While being pierced by your arrows, which you discharged on all sides, some of them, who were injured by the multitude of arrows but who desired to live, fled the battlefield, intent on escaping.
Commentary

The devatas, pierced by arrows (asyatah), fled away, giving up the battlefield (utsrjya).

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

This verse indicates that Kaṁsa previously defeated the devatās. This is described in Viṣṇu Purāṇa. kiṁ na dṛṣṭo ‘mara-patir mayā saṁyugam etya saḥ pṛṣṭhenaiva vahan vāṇān apagacchan na vaksāsā mad-rāṣṭre vāritā vṛṣṭir yadā śakreṇa kiṁ tadā mad vānabhinnair jaladair āpo muktā yathepsitāḥ Unseen by me, Indra approached to fight but, wounded by my arrows on his back, not his chest, he fled. When he stopped the rain in my kingdom, with my arrows which pierced the clouds I produced rain by my will. The devatās turned and fled.

Concepts
Jiva
Personal Guidance
When fear of death drives us to flee our post, we abandon kartavya — but the soul, eternal under Kṛṣṇa's shelter, has nothing to escape.
Practical Applications
  • Notice today the one duty you are tempted to flee from out of fear of pain or loss, and stay at that post.
  • Refuse to confuse self-preservation of the body with preservation of the self — offer the day's risks to Kṛṣṇa before you begin.
  • Speak honestly with one person about a responsibility you have been quietly running from, and name the next concrete step back into it.
Reflection Questions
  • When the wounded asuras fled to preserve their bodies at any cost, where do I see myself doing the same — abandoning a sva-dharma post the moment it begins to 'pierce' me?
  • What is the 'arrow' I am currently running from — a duty, a vow, a service — and what does my flight reveal about whom I am actually serving: the soul or the body that wants to live?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Name the one class, club, or friend-group 'battlefield' you have been quietly ghosting this semester because you cannot guarantee you will look good — and decide tonight whether to return or to formally, honestly withdraw. No more silent palāyana.
  • Audit your last week of social media: when you scrolled past a friend in obvious distress, or stayed silent in a group chat where someone was being mocked, that was a small flight from kartavya. Pick one and repair it this week with a direct message, not a like.
  • Before your next exam or interview, notice the jijīviṣā — the panicked 'I just want to survive this' energy. Offer the effort to Kṛṣṇa as yajña before you walk in, and watch how the survival-grip loosens into actual focus.
householder
  • Identify the one family conversation you have been deferring for months — about money, a child's direction, an aging parent, a marriage friction — and put it on the calendar this week. That deferral is your battlefield-flight.
  • Examine your career: are you staying in a role because it is genuine seva for your family's welfare, or because leaving threatens your jijīviṣā — your image, your income trajectory, your story about yourself? Speak it honestly to your spouse, even if you do not act on it yet.
  • When you discipline or guide your children this week, distinguish material compassion (sparing them discomfort) from real compassion (preparing their soul). Choose one moment to hold the harder line as an offering, not a power-move.
senior
  • Make a list of the 'arrows' you have spent decades dodging — relationships unrepaired, apologies unspoken, a dharma-decision postponed. Pick one and address it this month, while you still have the body to do so.
  • Practice loosening jijīviṣā deliberately: each morning, sit for five minutes acknowledging that this body's flight from death is futile, and rest in the soul's relationship with Kṛṣṇa. Let this re-order your day's priorities downward from urgency to devotion.
  • Mentor one younger person — a grandchild, a neighbor, a junior colleague — by telling them honestly about a battlefield you fled in your own life and what it cost. Legacy is not curated success; it is transmitted vairāgya.
Common Pitfalls
  • Romanticizing the fleeing soldiers as 'choosing peace' — missing that their palāyana is rooted in bodily jijīviṣā, the very attachment Kṛṣṇa's arrows are meant to dismantle.
  • Reading the verse as a license to walk away from any hard situation in the name of self-care, rather than recognizing flight from kartavya as the failure mode being diagnosed.
  • Mistaking Kṛṣṇa's piercing as cruelty rather than as the supreme compassion of the Personality of Godhead — confusing material compassion (let them keep their bodies and comforts) with real compassion (cut the ahaṅkāra so the soul can turn toward Him).
Discussion Prompts
  • The verse describes warriors who 'desired to live' and fled. In your own life, where have you 'fled the battlefield' of a kartavya you knew was yours — not out of cowardice exactly, but out of a quiet preference to keep living comfortably? Share one concrete instance with your partner and let them push back on whether the retreat was justified.
  • Kaṁsa's soldiers were performing seva for an asuric master, so their flight is almost a relief — but the verse is told to Kaṁsa as an indictment of his power. Discuss: when is abandoning a post a failure of pratijna, and when is it the first honest act of a soul refusing demonic service? Try to disagree, not just agree.
  • Santosha is usually translated as 'contentment,' but these soldiers display its opposite — a frantic jijīviṣā, a desperate clinging to life at any cost. Where in your daily life does 'wanting to live' override your willingness to stand firm in bhakti? Be specific; do not let your group settle for abstractions.
Role Play

Two students play a pair of co-workers on a project team led by a manager who is cutting ethical corners — falsifying numbers, mistreating juniors. Student A has just decided to quietly transfer out ('palāyana') to protect their career and family. Student B has decided to stay and confront the manager, citing kartavya. They meet for coffee the night before A's transfer is finalized. A must defend the flight as protecting jijīviṣā (livelihood, family); B must press whether this is yajña or self-preservation dressed up as prudence. After 6 minutes, switch roles. The teacher then asks the room: which kind of 'arrows' actually drove A away — external danger, or internal attachment?

Objections & Responses
  • Isn't it simply rational and even compassionate for these soldiers to flee? Self-preservation is a basic instinct. Calling their flight a moral failure feels like spiritual bullying — Kṛṣṇa's brother Balarāma is, after all, killing them.
    Notice the slip from material compassion to real compassion. Yes, the bodily instinct to survive is natural — but the verse is not condemning the soldiers as individuals; it is exposing the structure of asuric service. They fought for Kaṁsa's viṣaya-driven empire, and when the arrows came, the same ahaṅkāra that made them serve a tyrant now made them flee him. A Gauḍīya teacher would say: do not romanticize their flight as wisdom. Real compassion would have been refusing to take up arms for Kaṁsa in the first place. The lesson for us is not 'never flee' but 'examine whose battlefield you agreed to stand on, and why.'
  • This verse is just battlefield narration — a body count. Why are we mining it for personal guidance? Aren't you forcing meaning onto a passage that is really about Kṛṣṇa-līlā, not about me?
    Two things at once. First, every line of Bhāgavatam is, by Vyāsa's design, simultaneously līlā and śikṣā — Sūta is not narrating for entertainment. Second, the personalist line is precisely what saves us from generic moralizing: we are not extracting an abstract principle, we are watching what happens to those who give their loyalty to the wrong master. The soldiers' flight is a mirror. Where have I sworn a small pratijna to a small Kaṁsa — a job, an identity, a habit — and now find myself dodging arrows I should never have been standing under? That question is not imposed on the verse; it is the verse, read as a sādhaka rather than a spectator.
