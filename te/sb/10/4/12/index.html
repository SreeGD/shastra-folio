Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.12

కిం మయా హతయా మన్ద జాత: ఖలు తవాన్తకృత్ । యత్ర క్‍వ వా పూర్వశత్రుర్మా హింసీ: కృపణాన్ వృథా ॥ ౧౨ ॥

Important Words
  • manda — fool
    Vocative — 'O dull one.' Yoga-māyā does not flatter. Manda in śāstra is one whose intelligence is slow because of tamas. The asura is named for what he is, not for what his power suggests.
  • tava anta-kṛt — the one who will end you
    Kṛṣṇa is named here as anta-kṛt — the maker of Kaṁsa's end. The Gauḍīya ācāryas note: Bhagavān is dear to the devotee, but also the certain end of the demon. The same Person, two relations.
  • pūrva-śatruḥ — previous enemy
    Compound — 'enemy from before'. Kaṁsa's enmity with Kṛṣṇa pre-dates this birth (he was Kālanemi previously). The verse hints at the multi-life karmic structure underlying the līlā.
  • hiṁsīḥ — (do not) kill
    Aorist subjunctive with mā — the imperative of prohibition. 'Do not slay.' Yoga-māyā has slipped his grip yet still gives moral counsel to her enemy. Even Bhagavān's potency tries to dissuade the demon from further sin.
  • vṛthā — uselessly
    Adverb. All Kaṁsa's killing has been and will be in vain. The demon's labor against Kṛṣṇa is structurally futile; the verse states it plainly so the reader will not waste his own life in similar pursuits.
O Kaṁsa, you fool, what will be the use of killing me? The Supreme Personality of Godhead, who has been your enemy from the very beginning and who will certainly kill you, has already taken His birth somewhere else. Therefore, do not unnecessarily kill other children.
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
O Kaṁsa, you fool, what will be the use of killing me? The Supreme Personality of Godhead, who has been your enemy from the very beginning and who will certainly kill you, has already taken His birth somewhere else. Therefore, do not unnecessarily kill other children.
Commentary

"What can you gain from killing me? If you kill me, you still cannot prevent your own death. He who will kill you has already been born in some place, which cannot be revealed to you (yatra kva va)." Krpanam refers to Devaki who is suffering. Another reading is krpanan, in which case it refers to many poor babies. Instead of "You should not kill the poor Devaki" the sentence would read "You should not kill the poor innocent children."

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

O fool (manda)! What is the use even if you had killed me? He who will kill you has been born somewhere though I cannot clearly say the place. Therefore I will not kill you today. In a previous birth he killed you. Do not afflict poor Devakī by keeping her imprisoned. Release her along with her husband. You should also offer them some money. Instead of kṛpanām sometimes kṛpnanān is seen. Then the meaning is “Do not kill other helpless children thinking that they will kill you because they are related to the couple.”

Personal Guidance
Kṛṣṇa cannot be killed by any scheme of the envious — surrender to Him, not to your own clever plans for self-protection.
Practical Applications
  • Notice today where you are scheming to control an outcome Kṛṣṇa already governs, and drop one such maneuver before sundown.
  • Refuse to take out your fear on someone weaker — when anxiety rises, name the real source rather than wounding the kṛpaṇa nearest you.
  • Speak Kṛṣṇa's name aloud once when threatened or insulted today, trusting that the ante-kṛt of every false fear has already taken His birth.
Reflection Questions
  • Devakī addresses Kaṁsa as 'manda' (fool) while pleading for the lives of others — where in my life have I tried to reason with a tyrannical impulse (in myself or another) by appealing to its self-interest rather than confronting it directly?
  • The verse turns on the assurance that Kṛṣṇa, the real anta-kṛt, has already been born elsewhere beyond Kaṁsa's reach — what 'kṛpaṇān' (helpless ones, including my own dependents and lower nature) am I still failing to protect because I doubt that Kṛṣṇa's arrangement is already in motion without me?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Notice the next time you are afraid of one specific person or outcome (a rival, a rejection, a grade) and find yourself snapping at someone unrelated — a sibling, a roommate, a group chat. Name it out loud to yourself: 'I am Kaṁsa right now; this person is not my enemy.' Then apologize before bed.
  • When peer pressure pushes you to pile on someone — a classmate being mocked, a junior being hazed, a stranger being dragged on social media — refuse to add the next blow. The pile-on is exactly Kaṁsa's logic: harming the kṛpaṇa to feel safer in the group. Walk away from the thread or change the subject in person.
  • Before a high-stakes exam or interview, write down the actual thing you fear (failing, looking stupid, disappointing parents). Naming the real fear stops you from displacing it onto friends, family, or yourself through self-attack. Then offer the effort to Kṛṣṇa and go in.
householder
  • Audit one running family conflict this week: are you actually angry at your spouse / child / parent, or are you angry at money pressure, a boss, or your own aging — and using the family member as the target? Speak to the real source instead, even if you have to journal it first.
  • In a work decision involving layoffs, vendor changes, or 'cost-cutting,' ask explicitly: am I about to harm kṛpaṇān vṛthā — uselessly hurt the powerless — to protect myself from a fear I have not named? If yes, slow the decision by 48 hours and consult someone with viveka before acting.
  • When raising children, distinguish disciplining the behavior from punishing your own anxiety. Kaṁsa-parenting attacks the child for the parent's fear of the future. Each evening, review: did I correct my child today for their growth, or for my unease?
senior
  • Examine which 'enemies' you are still rehearsing in old age — the relative who wronged you, the colleague who passed you over, the political faction you despise. Kaṁsa died still hunting. Choose one such grudge this week and formally release it before Kṛṣṇa, not because the person deserves it, but because you do not wish to depart with that load.
  • In writing your will, distributing possessions, or advising adult children, watch for the impulse to use legacy as a weapon — rewarding the compliant, punishing the independent. That is Kaṁsa's grip dressed up as 'wisdom.' Aim instead for arrangements that free everyone, including yourself, from ahaṅkāra.
  • Mentor a younger person — a grandchild, a junior colleague, a neighbor's kid — by protecting them from the petty Kaṁsas in their life: bullying bosses, gossiping aunts, anxious parents who attack what they fear. Be, for someone, the Yogamāyā voice that names the foolishness and shields the kṛpaṇa.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading Devakī's plea as mere maternal sentiment (material compassion for bodies) rather than as discriminative wisdom (vivekaḥ) — she is not bargaining for her own child's life but exposing the futility of Kaṁsa's violence in light of Kṛṣṇa's already-accomplished birth.
  • Mistaking 'do not unnecessarily kill the kṛpaṇān' for a generic non-violence ethic, and missing that the verse specifically condemns hiṁsā driven by ahaṅkāra — the false ego's conviction that it can outmaneuver providence by destroying perceived threats.
  • Identifying with Devakī's position of helpless surrender while quietly behaving like Kaṁsa — striking at small, available targets (subordinates, family members, one's own body) because the real 'enemy' (one's mortality, one's anarthas) is beyond one's grasp.
Discussion Prompts
  • Yogamāyā speaks with sharp irony — calling Kaṁsa 'manda' (fool) and mocking his attempt to kill her. Where in your own life have you confused harming the innocent (kṛpaṇān) with eliminating a real threat? Share a specific instance and let your partner press back if you are sanitizing it.
  • Kaṁsa's whole strategy — kill every newborn — is the logic of a man ruled by fear and ahaṅkāra. He cannot distinguish the actual enemy (the Lord) from imagined enemies (helpless infants). Discuss: what is the modern equivalent of this displacement? Where do we attack the wrong target because we cannot face the real one? Disagree with each other if your examples differ.
  • The verse implies that genuine discriminative wisdom (viveka) sees the real cause and refuses to harm the innocent in pursuit of it. But Kaṁsa would say he is being prudent — covering his bases. When does 'prudence' become cruelty in disguise? Bring a real decision (work, family, politics) where the line is genuinely contested.
Role Play

Two students. Student A plays a manager who has been told that 'someone on the team' is leaking information to a competitor. Out of fear for their position, A is preparing to fire three junior employees as a precaution, even though there is no evidence against any of them specifically — 'better safe than sorry.' Student B plays a colleague who has just learned of the plan and must, in five minutes, intervene the way Yogamāyā intervened with Kaṁsa: name the foolishness, expose the displaced fear, and protect the kṛpaṇān (the powerless juniors) without simply moralizing. After the role-play, the class discusses: did B serve A's soul, or merely scold A's behavior? What would real compassion (not material compassion) have looked like?

Objections & Responses
  • This verse is just mythology — a goddess taunting a tyrant. What does it have to do with me? I am not a king, I am not killing infants, the whole frame is alien.
    Set aside the costume and look at the structure. Kaṁsa is any one of us when we are ruled by ahaṅkāra and fear: we identify a threat, we cannot locate it precisely, so we lash out at whoever is in reach — the spouse, the subordinate, the child, the stranger online. Yogamāyā's rebuke is the voice of viveka cutting through that pattern: 'the real enemy is not where you think, and the ones you are harming are kṛpaṇa — powerless and undeserving.' The verse is not about ancient kings; it is about the displacement we do every week. Read it as a mirror, and it becomes uncomfortably immediate.
  • But Yogamāyā is essentially gloating — 'you cannot kill me, He has already escaped.' That sounds less like compassion and more like a taunt. How is this a model of anything spiritual?
    Notice what she actually does. She could simply vanish. Instead she stays long enough to deliver one instruction: mā hiṁsīḥ kṛpaṇān vṛthā — do not uselessly harm the helpless. That is not gloating; that is the sharpest possible act of seva toward Kaṁsa himself and toward the children he would otherwise kill. Real compassion in the Gauḍīya line is not soft reassurance — it is whatever serves the soul, even if it stings the false ego. A Vaiṣṇava teacher will sometimes speak to us exactly the way Yogamāyā speaks to Kaṁsa, because flattery would only deepen our delusion. The taunt is the medicine.
Analogy

A foolish marksman wastes arrows on his own shadow while the true game grazes calmly in the next field. Yoga-māyā tells Kaṁsa exactly that: I am not your enemy — your enemy has already been born elsewhere. The asuric mind never lacks targets; what it lacks is the knowledge that Kṛṣṇa cannot be killed by any of them.

Stories
Hiraṇyakaśipu searching for Viṣṇu

When Hiraṇyakaśipu's brother Hiraṇyākṣa was slain by Varāha, the demon resolved to kill Viṣṇu. He searched every direction, every plane, every realm of beings — and never found Him. Eventually, exhausted of external search, he came home only to discover that Viṣṇu was already inside his own son Prahlāda's heart, and at last inside a pillar of his own throne-hall (SB 7).

Kaṁsa is in the same predicament. He kills infant after infant, ransacks villages, dispatches demons across the land — and Kṛṣṇa, all the while, is in Gokula in plain sight. The demon's plans for finding and killing Bhagavān are exactly the plans by which he loses Him.
