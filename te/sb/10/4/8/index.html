Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.8

Having uprooted all relationships with his sister because of intense selfishness, Kaṁsa, who was sitting on his knees, grasped the newborn child by the legs and tried to dash her against the surface of a stone.
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Having uprooted all relationships with his sister because of intense selfishness, Kaṁsa, who was sitting on his knees, grasped the newborn child by the legs and tried to dash her against the surface of a stone.
Commentary

He threw the child down with great force (apothayet).

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

This verse shows his cruel nature. Jāta-mātrāṁ means the new born child which was still wet with embryonic fluids. Hari-vaṁśa says sā garbha-śayana-kliṣṭā garbhāklinnamūrddhaja: the child, in pain from being in the womb, had its head moist with fluid.

Personal Guidance
When sva-artha (selfishness) uproots sauhṛda (affection), even kinship becomes prey — guard the heart from the ahaṅkāra that turns love into use.
Practical Applications
  • Notice one relationship today where you have quietly reduced a person to their utility for you, and restore them to soul-status in your speech.
  • Refuse to act on a self-serving impulse when it would override a bond of trust — pause, name the sva-artha, and offer the moment to Kṛṣṇa.
  • Speak to a family member today with no agenda, no ask, no leverage — practice sauhṛda untainted by what you want from them.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my life have I, like Kaṁsa, allowed sva-artha (selfish interest) to silently uproot a sauhṛda — a bond of natural affection — and called it pragmatism?
  • What 'newborn' good in me — a fresh impulse toward bhakti, a tender resolution, a vulnerable relationship — am I about to dash against the stone of self-preservation, and what fear is driving the hand?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Notice the next time a friendship in your class or hostel cools right when your interests diverged — a different stream, a different friend group, a competing crush — and ask honestly: was it really 'we grew apart,' or did svārtha quietly uproot sauhṛda?
  • Before you post, repost, or pile-on online today, pause and check: am I about to harm someone's reputation because their existence threatens my self-image, my grades-ranking, my social standing? That is the Kaṁsa-impulse in miniature — the seed of svārtha-unmūlita. Step back.
  • Pick one person whose presence makes you feel smaller — a topper, a more popular classmate, a sibling who gets more attention — and do one concrete act of genuine support for them this week. Watch how strongly the ahaṅkāra resists. That resistance is the verse, lit up inside you.
householder
  • In the next family financial decision — a property, an inheritance, a school choice, an aging parent's care — write down the option that is best for your soul-bond with these people, and the option that is best for your svārtha. If they differ, that gap is exactly the territory this verse is warning you about.
  • When you catch yourself rehearsing a 'reasonable' argument for why a sibling, in-law, or co-parent should accept a decision that mostly benefits you, name it inwardly as svārtha before you speak it aloud. Often, just naming it dissolves the need to win.
  • Once a week, perform one act of seva for your spouse or child that has zero strategic return — not to be remembered, not to be repaid, not even to be noticed. This rebuilds sauhṛda on a non-svārtha foundation while there is still time.
senior
  • Examine your will, your assets, and your stated wishes: is anything there shaped by a quiet desire to keep being important after you are gone — to hold a child, a temple, an institution on a leash? Loosen one such grip this month as a deliberate act of tyāga.
  • When mentoring a younger person — child, grandchild, junior colleague — notice if you are subtly steering them toward decisions that protect your legacy rather than serve their soul. Real compassion at this stage is for their svadharma, not for your self-continuation.
  • Sit each evening with the recognition that the body you have transitioned through is nearing its final passage, and that nothing svārtha-built will cross with you. Use that clarity to forgive one old grievance where you were 'wronged' — most of those wounds, examined now, were two svārthas colliding, not soul against soul.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading this verse as a cautionary tale about 'other people like Kaṁsa' rather than recognizing the same svārtha-driven calculus operating, in subtler form, in one's own decisions today.
  • Mistaking Kaṁsa's failure as merely ethical (he was cruel) and missing the deeper diagnosis — that ahaṅkāra fused to self-interest will sever even the most natural bonds, so the cure is anāhaṅkāra, not better ethics.
  • Using the call to vairāgya and tyāga as cover for cutting off relationships and duties one finds inconvenient — Kaṁsa-style 'detachment' from sauhṛda dressed up as renunciation, when real vairāgya is detachment from viṣaya, not from one's responsibilities to others.
Discussion Prompts
  • Kaṁsa's sauhṛda (affection) for his sister was 'uprooted' the moment her child threatened his position. Where in your own life have you felt affection quietly disappear the moment self-interest was at stake — and did you admit it was happening, or did you rename it 'being practical'?
  • The verse calls Kaṁsa's act svārtha-unmūlita — selfishness as a tree-pulling force. We tend to picture selfishness as small (a stingy moment, a white lie). What is the most 'reasonable-sounding' selfishness you have witnessed in a respected person — family, boss, leader — that, traced to its root, would tear up relationships if pursued? Disagree with each other on where the line sits.
  • If real compassion is for the soul and material compassion is for the body, then Kaṁsa's failure was not just cruelty — it was a total inversion of values. Discuss: is it possible to be a 'good' family man, dutiful employee, kind neighbour, and still be living a Kaṁsa-logic of self-preservation at the level of the soul? Push back on each other; do not let the group settle on a comfortable answer.
Role Play

Two students play siblings who jointly inherit a modest family home. Sibling A (the 'Kaṁsa seat') has just been told by a financial advisor that selling the home immediately, against the dying wish of their father, will secure A's career and lifestyle for the next decade. Sibling B wants to keep the home as a place for the extended family, including A's own children, to gather. A must argue for the sale — using only language that sounds reasonable, responsible, even loving ('I am doing this for the family's future'). B must listen and then name, without insulting, the exact moment svārtha begins to uproot sauhṛda in A's reasoning. A third student observes and reports back where the group itself was tempted to side with A's 'sensible' framing. Then swap roles.

Objections & Responses
  • This verse is just a horror story about an ancient tyrant. What does an infanticidal king have to do with my life? I am not Kaṁsa.
    Gauḍīya teachers read these śāstric portraits as mirrors, not museum pieces. Kaṁsa is not a one-off villain; he is the logical end-point of a principle we all carry — svārtha that, when threatened, will quietly dissolve every bond it once cherished. Most of us never reach the stone-slab; we reach only the small betrayals — the friend dropped when inconvenient, the parent visited less when promotion came, the truth softened to protect a position. The verse is precisely meant to startle us before we drift further down that line. To say 'I am not Kaṁsa' is exactly what Kaṁsa would have said the day before his sister's wedding.
  • Telling people to suspect their own affection as secret selfishness will just make them anxious and cynical. Healthy relationships need trust in our own love, not constant suspicion of it.
    Gauḍīya sādhana does not ask you to distrust love; it asks you to locate it correctly. Sauhṛda built only on shared svārtha is fragile — it will, as in Kaṁsa's case, snap the instant interests diverge. Sauhṛda anchored in seeing Kṛṣṇa in the other — the same eternal soul the Gītā describes in 2.13 — only deepens under pressure, because its root is not in what the other gives me. So the practice is not anxious self-suspicion; it is vairāgya from svārtha as the load-bearing wall of relationship, and the steady transfer of that weight onto bhakti. The result is not less affection but affection that no longer needs to be defended at someone else's expense.
