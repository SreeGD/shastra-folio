Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.28

శ్రీశుక ఉవాచ కంస ఏవం ప్రసన్నాభ్యాం విశుద్ధం ప్రతిభాషిత: । దేవకీవసుదేవాభ్యామనుజ్ఞాతోఽవిశద్ గృహమ్ ॥ ౨౮ ॥

Important Words
  • prasannābhyām — by the two (who were) pleased
    Instrumental dual — 'by those two pleased ones'. Devakī and Vasudeva are prasanna — pure and tranquil. Prasāda is the natural fragrance of a devotee's heart; even a brief proximity to it can quiet a demon.
  • viśuddham — purely / from a purified (heart)
    Adverb / adjective — 'pure'. Their speech was viśuddha — clear of personal grievance, free of self-interest. The Gauḍīya rasa-śāstra holds that bhakti delivered from a viśuddha heart penetrates even hostile listeners.
  • pratibhāṣitaḥ — having been replied to / addressed in return
    Past passive participle. Kaṁsa is the recipient — the one spoken back to. Notice the grammar: he is being acted upon by Vasudeva's words, no longer the agent of the scene.
  • anujñātaḥ — given permission / dismissed
    Past passive participle of anu-√jñā — 'permitted'. Devakī and Vasudeva, the saints, formally permit him to depart. The roles have inverted: those who were prisoners now grant audience-leave to the king.
  • aviśat-gṛham — he entered his home
    Simple but devastating: he goes home. The peace will not last the night. The Bhāgavatam often closes a sub-scene on a deceptively quiet line so the reader feels the brittle stillness before the next storm.
Śukadeva Gosvāmī continued: Thus having been addressed in purity by Devakī and Vasudeva, who were very much appeased, Kaṁsa felt pleased, and with their permission he entered his home.
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Śukadeva Gosvāmī continued: Thus having been addressed in purity by Devakī and Vasudeva, who were very much appeased, Kaṁsa felt pleased, and with their permission he entered his home.
Commentary

They spoke very sincerely to Kamsa with pleasing words (visuddham). Another reading of the word is visraddham. In that case, it means that they spoke in such a way to gain Kamsa’s trust.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Having been answered by Vasudeva and Devakī in such a way to gain his faith (viśuddham), because he was pleased with their words and they agreed with his philosophy, he took permission and entered his house. Another version has akapaṭam (without duplicity) instead of viśuddham.

Personal Guidance
When we speak from viśuddha-bhāva — purified by bhakti — even a hostile heart is disarmed; truth in sattva neutralizes rajas and tamas around us.
Practical Applications
  • Before your next difficult conversation, sit silently and remember Kṛṣṇa for two minutes so your words leave the mouth already viśuddha, not reactive.
  • Notice when you are about to argue from fear or anger — pause, drop the agenda, and speak only what is true and beneficial, the way Devakī and Vasudeva did to Kaṁsa.
  • Refuse to confuse appeasing someone with surrendering to them; today, soften your tone with a difficult person while keeping your shelter firmly in Kṛṣṇa, not in their approval.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my life am I, like Kaṁsa, accepting a softened, purified address from those I have wronged and treating their forgiveness as permission to simply 'enter my home' and resume old patterns — without any inner change?
  • When someone speaks to me in viśuddha-vāk (purified speech), do I receive it as a call to deeper sādhana, or do I extract only the relief of being appeased and walk away comforted?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • When a friend or classmate has wronged you and you finally have to speak to them — a betrayal in a friend group, a partner who cheated, a teammate who took credit — practice viśuddha speech: say the true thing without the sting, and watch what it costs your ego (and what it saves your sleep).
  • On social media, the temptation is to reply to a hostile comment with rajas-laden cleverness. Today, when provoked online, draft the reply, save it, and post nothing for 24 hours. Notice how much of your urge to 'win' is ahaṅkāra, not truth.
  • In one tense conversation this week — with a parent about your career, a professor about a grade, a friend about a falling-out — speak as Devakī and Vasudeva did: from sattva, with self-control, refusing to either grovel or attack. Journal afterward on which mode (rajas, tamas, or sattva) you defaulted to under pressure.
householder
  • In the next family decision involving a difficult relative — in-laws, an estranged sibling, a manipulative cousin around money or property — coordinate with your spouse beforehand so you speak 'devakī-vasudevābhyām,' as one voice, not two splintered reactions. The unity of viśuddha speech is half its power.
  • At work, when a boss or client behaves like a small Kaṁsa, resist both the tamas of silent endurance and the rajas of sharp pushback. Practice the third path: appeased-but-not-compromised speech. Note specifically which fruits (job security, status, the satisfaction of a comeback) you are quietly attached to.
  • In raising children, model viśuddha speech in front of them when speaking about people who have hurt the family — an absent parent, a difficult grandparent, a former friend. They are absorbing your tone more than your words; what you transmit becomes their default in their own future Kaṁsa-encounters.
senior
  • Old wounds — a sibling who took the inheritance, a colleague who derailed your career, a friend who betrayed you decades ago — are still candidates for viśuddha speech, even if only inwardly or in a long-overdue letter. Before the body fails, clear the speech-debts; do not carry rajas-residue into the final years meant for bhakti.
  • When mentoring younger family members or students about people who have wronged them, do not validate their grievance with your own old grievances. Show them the Devakī-Vasudeva mode: truth without venom. This is legacy more durable than any property you leave.
  • In facing your own mortality, recognize that every person who has been a 'Kaṁsa' in your life is, like you, a soul on the way somewhere. Loosen the attachment to being vindicated in this lifetime; offer the unfinished accounts to Kṛṣṇa, and let the discriminative wisdom of the dhīra carry you the rest of the way home.
Common Pitfalls
  • Mistaking the appeasement of an adversary for actual transformation — Kaṁsa was pacified, not purified; students often treat momentary peace with a difficult person as evidence that the underlying ahaṅkāra has dissolved.
  • Romanticizing Devakī and Vasudeva's purity into a generic 'be kind to your enemies' lesson, losing the specifically Vaiṣṇava point: their viśuddha speech flowed from their unwavering shelter in Kṛṣṇa, not from natural temperament or moral effort alone.
  • Reading 'anujñāto 'viśad gṛham' (he entered his home with their permission) as a happy resolution, when the narrative arc shows Kaṁsa returning to the same demonic environment that will soon reactivate his envy — a warning against trusting reform that is not anchored in surrender to Kṛṣṇa.
Discussion Prompts
  • Devakī and Vasudeva spoke to Kaṁsa — their son's murderer — with such purity (viśuddham) that he left appeased. Share with your partner: was this real compassion for Kaṁsa's soul, or a tactical accommodation of a tyrant? Where does each of you draw the line, and why might you disagree?
  • Kaṁsa is 'pleased' and walks home — yet within hours he will resume his demonic plotting. Discuss in your group: does this verse vindicate the parents' shaucha-laden speech, or does it expose the limits of speaking purely to someone whose ahaṅkāra is intact? Push each other on whether 'success' is measured by the speaker's purity or the listener's transformation.
  • The verse uses the dual 'devakī-vasudevābhyām' — they speak as one. Talk together about a relationship in your life (spouse, parent, sibling, co-worker) where you have to face a difficult third party. Where do you and your partner-in-life actually speak with one voice in viśuddham, and where do you splinter under rajas/tamas? Be specific; resist the urge to make your partnership sound holier than it is.
Role Play

Set up a triangle. One student plays a 'Kaṁsa' figure — a powerful relative or boss who has genuinely harmed the family (a brother who cheated the parents in a property dispute, say, or a manager who pushed a colleague out). Two students play a married couple, like Devakī-Vasudeva, who must speak to this person at a family function. The Kaṁsa-figure is testing them, half-apologetic, half-arrogant. The couple's task: speak in viśuddham — neither flattering nor flaming — until the Kaṁsa-figure feels genuinely 'pleased' enough to leave the encounter without escalation. The rest of the class watches for: where does the couple slip into rajas (sharp retort) or tamas (cowardly silence)? Where do they actually achieve sattva-grounded speech? Run it twice with different couples, then debrief on what shaucha of speech actually costs.

Objections & Responses
  • This sounds like spiritualized appeasement. Devakī and Vasudeva are flattering a tyrant who killed their babies, and the text praises them for it. Isn't this exactly the kind of religious passivity that lets evil flourish?
    Look closer at the word viśuddham — purely, without admixture. Their speech is not flattery, which is rajas dressed as sweetness; it is sattva-grounded truth that does not provoke unnecessarily. They are not pretending Kaṁsa is good; they are refusing to let his demonic nature contaminate theirs. A Gauḍīya teacher would point out that real compassion here is for Kaṁsa's soul — every moment he is not killing is a moment closer to the liberation Kṛṣṇa Himself will eventually grant him. Their atma-nigraha is not passivity; it is the discipline of one who knows Kṛṣṇa is the actual doer, and that protecting their own bhakti is the first duty. Resistance to evil and viśuddha speech are not opposed — Arjuna will fight, and rightly, but only after surrender. This is the inner work that must precede any outer action.
  • Kaṁsa is 'pleased' and goes home — but we know he resumes his evil shortly. So the parents' purity changed nothing. What's the practical value of speaking purely if the wicked person doesn't actually transform?
    This objection assumes the value of pure speech lies in the listener's reform. The Gauḍīya line says otherwise: shaucha and atma-nigraha are first ours to keep, regardless of effect. Devakī and Vasudeva preserved their own consciousness intact for Kṛṣṇa's appearance — and Kṛṣṇa appears in a heart undisturbed by hatred, not in one consumed by revenge fantasies, however justified. Second, the verse shows that even temporary appeasement of a Kaṁsa buys time — time in which Kṛṣṇa's pastimes unfold. We do not control when or how the wicked transform; that is in Kṛṣṇa's hands. Our duty is to remain instruments fit for His use. A heart polluted by even righteous rage is a less hospitable place for bhakti than a heart that spoke truth purely and let the result rest with Him. This is karma-yoga in dialogue: right speech, no attachment to its fruit.
Analogy

A guest who entered a brother's home as a thief leaves it, for one night at least, as a guest. The truth, spoken without grasping by Devakī and Vasudeva, has softened the air; Kaṁsa walks home through it. But truth not anchored in bhakti is a temporary peace — the same Kaṁsa, by morning, will be calling his ministers.

Stories
Aśvatthāmā released by the Pāṇḍavas

After his offense at Uttarā's womb, Aśvatthāmā was released by the Pāṇḍavas — his head shaved, the jewel on his crest cut off — and sent into the forest. He left the camp, but he never repented; he wanders, the tradition says, to this day, neither dead nor at peace.

Kaṁsa entering his home here is in the same mood. The release is real, the conversation with Devakī and Vasudeva was real — but he carries no purified heart out of the room. The Gauḍīya tradition contrasts him with those who actually leave such a meeting transformed (Vidura after Maitreya, the Naimiṣāraṇya sages after Sūta). The same exit door opens onto two different paths depending on what is in the heart.
