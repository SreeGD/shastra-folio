Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.22

In the bodily conception of life, one remains in darkness, without self-realization, thinking, “I am being killed” or “I have killed my enemies.” As long as a foolish person thus considers the self to be the killer or the killed, he continues to be responsible for material obligations, and consequently he suffers the reactions of happiness and distress.
Purport

By the grace of the Lord, Kaṁsa felt sincere regret for having unnecessarily persecuted such Vaiṣṇavas as Devakī and Vasudeva, and thus he came to the transcendental stage of knowledge. “Because I am situated on the platform of knowledge,” Kaṁsa said, “understanding that I am not at all the killer of your sons, I have no responsibility for their death. As long as I thought that I would be killed by your son, I was in ignorance, but now I am free from this ignorance, which was due to a bodily conception of life.” As stated in Bhagavad-gītā (18.17) : yasya nāhaṅkṛto bhāvo buddhir yasya na lipyate hatvāpi sa imāḻ lokān na hanti na nibadhyate “One who is not motivated by false ego, whose intelligence is not entangled, though he kills men in this world, is not the slayer. Nor is he bound by his actions.” According to this axiomatic truth, Kaṁsa pleaded that he was not responsible for having killed the sons of Devakī and Vasudeva. “Please try to excuse me for such false, external activities,” he said, “and be pacified with this same knowledge.”

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
In the bodily conception of life, one remains in darkness, without self-realization, thinking, "I am being killed" or "I have killed my enemies." As long as a foolish person thus considers the self to be the killer of the killed, he continues to be responsible for material obligations, and consequently he suffers the reactions of happiness and distress.
Commentary

Kamsa from the point of view of the jnani, attempts to show that he is not the killer of her sons. "Those who do not see the soul, but only the body (a sva drk) are ignorant. Since I do not identify with the body, I incur no sin nor get any bondage of karma for killing the children."

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

The cause of all problems is that the ignorant person does not understand about ātmā. Śrīdhara Svāmī explains that as long as one identifies with the body, the person will think in terms oppressor and oppressed. Or the verse can be a response to the question “If you know this, then why do you fear death and kill all my children?” He answers “I have not realized this. I see in terms of bodies.” Though I have scriptural knowledge (svadṛk) I am fearful. Therefore I thought, “I will kill the children.” I am actually ignorant, devoid of realization of ātmā.

Personal Guidance
While trapped in the bodily 'I am killer / I am killed,' you remain bound to karma; step out of that ahaṅkāra and the chain breaks.
Practical Applications
  • Notice each time today you say 'I did this' or 'they did this to me,' and silently re-frame it as Kṛṣṇa's arrangement working through bodies.
  • Refuse to nurse the grudge of one specific 'wrong done to me' — offer it to Kṛṣṇa this morning and stop replaying it as injury to your self.
  • Before any consequential act today, pause and ask, 'Am I doing this as the soul serving Kṛṣṇa, or as the body claiming to be doer?' — and proceed only in the first mood.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my life am I still narrating myself as 'the one who did this' or 'the one to whom this was done' — and how is that ahaṅkāra-driven story keeping me bound to bādhya-bādhakatā (the cycle of obligation and reaction)?
  • Kaṁsa's regret arose only after he saw past the bodily conception — what unresolved guilt or grievance am I carrying that would dissolve the moment I stopped identifying the soul with the body involved?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • When you bomb an exam or get cut from a team, watch the two voices: 'I am a failure' and 'they ruined me.' Both are the ajña speaking from bodily conception. Name them out loud, then return to the next concrete action — one problem set, one honest conversation with the coach.
  • In group chats and social media, notice how fast you slip into 'I was attacked' or 'I destroyed them' over a comment. That is bādhya-bādhakatā in miniature. Practice this week: before replying to anything that stings, wait ten minutes and ask, 'Is the soul actually being touched here, or only the image I am defending?'
  • When peers pressure you into something and it goes wrong, resist the easy exit of 'it wasn't really me, they made me do it' — that is Kaṁsa's move. Own the action fully, then take it to your iṣṭa-deva in your evening sādhana and offer it. Responsibility plus surrender, not one without the other.
householder
  • In family conflict — with spouse, parents, in-laws — track the two phrases 'you are killing me' and 'I have to set them straight.' Each time one arises, pause and remember: the ātman of the person across from you is eternal, untouched. Speak to that soul, not to the threat your ahaṅkāra is constructing.
  • At work, when a project fails or a colleague is let go on your watch, refuse both extremes: the false humility of 'I destroyed their career' and the false detachment of 'it wasn't my doing.' Take precise kartavya — what specifically did you decide, what will you repair — and offer the rest to Kṛṣṇa in your morning offering.
  • With your children, especially when discipline is hard, do not weaponize 'I had to do it for your own good' (Kaṁsa's logic) and do not collapse into 'I have ruined them.' Sit with your spouse weekly and review one parenting decision from the week through this verse: where was ahaṅkāra driving, and where was genuine sevā to the soul entrusted to you?
senior
  • As you watch contemporaries fall ill and pass, notice the grip of 'they were taken from me' and 'I survived them.' Both keep you in bādhya-bādhakatā with the dead. Replace them with daily remembrance: this soul is eternal, our relationship is eternal in Kṛṣṇa, and my work now is to prepare my own passage.
  • Review the regrets you still carry — people you feel you wronged, decisions that harmed others. Do not numb them with 'I was not really the doer,' which is Kaṁsa's escape. Where possible, make direct amends this year; where not, perform specific sevā and japa in their name, and entrust the rest to the Lord.
  • In mentoring younger devotees or family members, refuse the elder's temptation to say 'I made you who you are' or 'you have abandoned me.' Both are the bodily conception speaking. Offer guidance freely, claim no fruit, and let your loosening grip itself become the teaching they remember.
Common Pitfalls
  • Misreading 'I am not the killer' as moral license — using non-doership as a sophisticated excuse to act recklessly or escape accountability, when the verse instead dismantles the false ego that produces such acts in the first place.
  • Applying the teaching only to grand acts of harm while leaving daily ahaṅkāra untouched — congratulating oneself on philosophical detachment from violence while still inwardly claiming 'I succeeded,' 'I was wronged,' 'I fixed this.'
  • Sliding into impersonalist abstraction — treating 'I am not the doer' as merging into a featureless Self, instead of holding it as Kṛṣṇa does in BG 18.17: free from false ego because intelligence is fixed in Him, not because the personal self has been erased.
Discussion Prompts
  • Kaṁsa uses this verse to argue that since he was acting from the bodily conception, he should not be held responsible for killing Devakī's sons. Where in this reasoning is he speaking genuine knowledge, and where might he be using transcendental language to evade kartavya? Disagree openly with your partner before you settle.
  • Take a recent moment in your own life when you said, 'It wasn't really me — I was angry / tired / under pressure.' Was that the dhīra speaking from beyond ahaṅkāra, or the ajña hiding behind a borrowed philosophy? How would you tell the difference without flattering yourself?
  • The verse says the one who thinks 'I am being killed' or 'I have killed' remains bound by bādhya-bādhakatā — the mutual obligation of killer and killed. If a soldier, a surgeon, or a parent disciplining a child takes this verse seriously, what changes in their action, and what must NOT change? Push back on each other's answers.
Role Play

Two students play a scene set the morning after a serious car accident. Student A was driving and survived; the passenger, a close friend, did not. Student A has just read SB 10.4.22 and tells Student B (the deceased friend's older sibling), 'I was not really the killer — the soul cannot be slain, and I was acting without false ego.' Student B is grieving and furious. The teacher pauses the scene every two minutes and asks the class: where is Student A misusing the verse like Kaṁsa, and what would it sound like for Student A to actually stand on this knowledge — accepting full kartavya for the action while not identifying the ātman as the doer? Swap roles and replay.

Objections & Responses
  • This verse sounds like a spiritual loophole for violence. If 'I am not the killer,' then any murderer, abuser, or war criminal can quote śāstra and walk away. Isn't this exactly the kind of teaching that gives religion a bad name?
    Notice who is speaking the verse — Kaṁsa, the man who has just murdered six infants. Vyāsa puts these words in the mouth of a tyrant precisely so we feel the danger of the misuse. The verse does not say the killer escapes reaction; it says the one still in bodily conception (tad-abhimāny ajño) remains bound by bādhya-bādhakatā. Real freedom from karma comes only when ahaṅkāra itself has dissolved through bhakti and surrender to Kṛṣṇa — not when one cleverly redescribes one's crime. The Gauḍīya teacher tests the claim by looking at the life: is there tapas, vairāgya, sevā, sincere regret? Kaṁsa briefly shows regret here, but soon returns to demoniac scheming — proving he never actually stood on the platform he quoted. The verse exposes the loophole; it does not authorize it.
  • Modern psychology says taking responsibility — 'I did this, I own it' — is the foundation of mental health and moral growth. This verse seems to teach the opposite: dis-identify, say it wasn't really you. Isn't that spiritual bypassing?
    The verse distinguishes two layers, and modern psychology usually collapses them. At the level of kartavya — duty, accountability, apaiśunam, making amends — the Gauḍīya tradition is fiercely demanding: you owe restitution, you face the consequences, you do not slander or shift blame. At the level of ātma-jñāna, you also see that the eternal soul is not the body and is not ultimately the doer; Kṛṣṇa is the sanctioner. Holding both is not bypassing — it is what allows you to take full responsibility without being crushed by it, and to act righteously without inflating the false ego of the doer. Bypassing is when you use layer two to escape layer one, as Kaṁsa attempts. The dhīra uses layer two to fuel layer one: because I am not this body, I can afford to do the hard, honest work of repair.
