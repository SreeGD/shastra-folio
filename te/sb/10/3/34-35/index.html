Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.3.34-35

Important Words
  • kāla-guṇān anu — following the seasonal qualities
    Kāla (time) + guṇa (qualities, here = seasonal modes) + anu (following). They endured WHATEVER each season presented, not selecting favorable conditions. The Gauḍīya gloss: real tapas is not self-chosen difficulty but acceptance of what comes — pre-figuring śaraṇāgati's ānukūlya-pratikūla.
  • śvāsa-rodha — controlling the breath
    Śvāsa (breath) + rodha (restraining). Prāṇāyāma. In Gauḍīya understanding aṣṭāṅga-yoga's prāṇāyāma is a legitimate prelude to bhakti but not its substance — useful for purification, insufficient for prema.
  • manaḥ-malau — the impurities of the mind (washed away)
    Manas (mind) + mala (dirt, impurity). The accumulated dust of the mind. Their tapas was not for siddhi but for citta-śuddhi — preparing the heart to receive Bhagavān. The order matters: śuddhi first, then darśana.
  • śīrṇa-parṇa — (eating) dry leaves
    Śīrṇa (withered, fallen) + parṇa (leaf). They ate only leaves the trees had already discarded — no violence to plants, minimal nutrition. The Bhāgavata-style tapas-diet, common to all forest sages.
  • ārādhanam — worship
    Ā-√rādh — to propitiate, to please. The verb of ārādhana = upāsanā. In Vaiṣṇava theology ārādhanam = bhakti as worship of Bhagavān (Rādhā's very name shares this root — She is the personification of the highest ārādhana). Their tapas was already devotional, not merely austere.
My dear father and mother, you endured rain, wind, strong sun, scorching heat and severe cold, suffering all sorts of inconvenience according to different seasons. By practicing prāṇāyāma to control the air within the body through yoga, and by eating only air and dry leaves fallen from the trees, you cleansed from your minds all dirty things. In this way, desiring a benediction from Me, you worshiped Me with peaceful minds.
Purport

Vasudeva and Devakī did not obtain the Supreme Personality of Godhead as their son very easily, nor does the Supreme Godhead accept merely anyone as His father and mother. Here we can see how Vasudeva and Devakī obtained Kṛṣṇa as their eternal son. In our own lives, we are meant to follow the principles indicated herewith for getting good children. Of course, it is not possible for everyone to get Kṛṣṇa as his son, but at least one can get very good sons and daughters for the benefit of human society. In Bhagavad-gītā it is said that if human beings do not follow the spiritual way of life, there will be an increase of varṇa-saṅkara population, population begotten like cats and dogs, and the entire world will become like hell. Not practicing Kṛṣṇa consciousness but simply encouraging artificial means to check the population will be futile; the population will increase, and it will consist of varṇa-saṅkara, unwanted progeny. It is better to teach people how to beget children not like hogs and dogs, but in controlled life. Human life is meant not for becoming a hog or dog, but for tapo divyam, transcendental austerity. Everyone should be taught to undergo austerity, tapasya. Although it may not be possible to undergo tapasya like that of Pṛśni and Sutapā, the śāstra has given an opportunity for a method of tapasya very easy to perform — the saṅkīrtana movement. One cannot expect to undergo tapasya to get Kṛṣṇa as one’s child, yet simply by chanting the Hare Kṛṣṇa mahā-mantra ( kīrtanād eva kṛṣṇasya ), one can become so pure that one becomes free from all the contamination of this material world ( mukta-saṅgaḥ ) and goes back home, back to Godhead ( paraṁ vrajet ). The Kṛṣṇa consciousness movement, therefore, is teaching people not to adopt artificial means of happiness, but to take the real path of happiness as prescribed in the śāstra — the chanting of the Hare Kṛṣṇa mantra — and become perfect in every aspect of material existence.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
My dear father and mother, you endured rain, wind, strong sun, scorching heat and severe cold, suffering all sorts of inconvenience according to different seasons. By practicing prāṇāyāma to control the air within the body through yoga, and by eating only air and dry leaves fallen from the trees, you cleansed from your minds all dirty things. In this way, desiring a benediction from Me, you worshiped Me with peaceful minds.
Commentary

atapah means burning due to the sun’s rays. Gharma means heat.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Two verses describe their austerities. They continuously (anu) tolerated the qualities of the seasons—rain, wind, sun, snow and heat. Ātapa refers to staying under the direct summer sun. Gharma means heat. Or ātapa means the autumn sun and gharma means the summer sun. They performed prāṇāyāma. First, impurities of the mind were generally washed away. Whatever impurities remained they were completely (ni) washed away so that they were not aware of any of them. Then they were most completely purified (vi) by destruction of the most subtle desires. By this increasingly effective purification, one can understand their extreme determination. They developed most steady minds (upaśāntena cetasā). This however means that they became fixed in the Lord, for it is said śamo man-niṣṭatā buddheḥ: śama means fixing the mind in me. (SB 11.19.36) They desired happiness from producing offspring (prajā-sarge) from getting the Lord as their son (mattaḥ). In verse 38, it will be said “You prayed for a son similar to me.”

Personal Guidance
Kṛṣṇa does not become anyone's son cheaply — Vasudeva and Devakī earned Him through tapasya, controlled senses, and a mind purified by forbearance.
Practical Applications
  • Choose one bodily comfort today — heating, snacking, scrolling — and refuse it as a small tapasya offered to Kṛṣṇa.
  • When weather, hunger, or fatigue presses you, do not complain aloud — practice titikṣā as Pṛśni and Sutapā did, for even an hour.
  • Replace one round of restless distraction today with one round of japa, asking Kṛṣṇa not for things but for Himself.
Reflection Questions
  • Pṛśni and Sutapā endured rain, sun, scorching heat, and cold to clear the dirt from their minds — what specific seasonal discomfort or bodily inconvenience am I currently rearranging my whole life to escape, and what mental dirt is that avoidance protecting?
  • They ate only fallen leaves and air, then asked Kṛṣṇa for a benediction with a peaceful mind — when I bring a desire before Him, what tapasya (however small) backs it up, and would I still want what I'm asking for if I had to earn it through that kind of restraint?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Pick one season-of-the-mind discomfort this week — exam stress, an awkward friend group, a public failure on social media — and refuse to seek a quick distraction from it for one full day. Sit with it the way Pṛśni and Sutapā sat in rain and sun, and silently chant while you do.
  • Try one fast a week — a real one, water-only or fruit, not a vague 'eating less' — and use that hunger as your private classroom for what tapas actually feels like. No posting about it. Pṛśni and Sutapā ate dry leaves; you can skip dinner once.
  • Audit the 'fruits' you are chasing in college — the GPA, the internship, the relationship status, the followers — and pick the one you are most secretly attached to. For one week, work just as hard at it but explicitly offer the result to Kṛṣṇa each night before sleep. Notice what changes inside you, not outside.
householder
  • Have an honest conversation with your spouse this week about what tapasya your household actually practices — fixed rising time, controlled food, controlled entertainment, fixed rounds of japa — and pick one austerity you will both add for the next forty days, with your children watching.
  • Before any major family decision this month — money, schooling, career move — sit together for ten minutes in silence first, breathing slowly, and only then discuss. This is your gṛhastha-grade prāṇāyāma: a controlled pause before the rajas of decision-making takes over.
  • Stop trying to give your children a frictionless childhood. Let them experience controlled inconvenience — heat, cold, hunger, boredom — in small doses, and frame it as titikṣā, not punishment. Children raised in upaśānta-cetas households are the real answer to the varṇa-saṅkara crisis Prabhupāda names.
senior
  • You have already endured your varṣa, vāta, ātapa, hima — many seasons of life. Spend one evening this week writing down the specific endurances of your life and consciously offering each one to Kṛṣṇa as retroactive tapas. Do not let those decades remain unoffered.
  • Loosen one attachment deliberately this month — give away a possession, release a grudge, hand over a responsibility you have been clutching — and treat it as your version of eating only fallen leaves. Renunciation at this stage is not loss; it is finally letting the mala fall off the mind.
  • Mentor one young couple or young person specifically on what controlled life looks like, drawing on your own failures and hard-won discipline. Prabhupāda says society needs people who know how to beget and raise children in tapas; if you do not transmit what you have learned, it dies with you.
Common Pitfalls
  • Romanticizing Pṛśni and Sutapā's extreme tapasya and concluding 'this is impossible for me, so austerity isn't my path' — Prabhupāda's purport explicitly gives the easier yuga-dharma alternative (chanting the Hare Kṛṣṇa mahā-mantra), so the verse demands engagement with tapasya at one's own level, not exemption from it.
  • Treating physical endurance (cold showers, fasting, harsh conditions) as the point in itself, while ignoring the verse's stated purpose — vinirdhūta-mano-malau, washing the dirt from the mind — and ending up with a hardened body and an equally agitated, prideful mind.
  • Using tapasya as a transactional bargaining chip — performing austerity in order to extract a specific benediction (a child, a spouse, a job) — and missing that Pṛśni and Sutapā's worship was mad-ārādhanam, worship of Him, with Kṛṣṇa Himself as the prayed-for fruit, not a means to some other end.
Discussion Prompts
  • Pṛśni and Sutapā ate dry leaves, controlled their breath, and endured every season for ages — to earn Kṛṣṇa as their son. Prabhupāda says we cannot do that tapasya, so the śāstra gives us saṅkīrtana instead. Be honest in your pair: does chanting really feel like the equivalent austerity to you, or does it feel suspiciously easy? Where does that suspicion come from — laziness, or genuine puzzlement?
  • The verse describes parents who underwent severe tapas specifically to *get a child* — and Prabhupāda extends this to anyone wanting 'good children' rather than varṇa-saṅkara progeny. Discuss: what does it mean today to 'qualify' to be a parent through tapasya rather than just biology? Where do you and your partner agree, and where do you push back?
  • Titikṣā here is not grim endurance — it is endurance *with upaśānta-cetas*, peaceful mind, while desiring Kṛṣṇa. Share with each other one situation in your life where you are enduring something but your mind is anything but peaceful. What would it take for that same endurance to become tapas instead of just suffering?
Role Play

Two students play a married couple in their early thirties. They have been chanting and following sādhana for two years and are now considering having a child. One partner has just read SB 10.3.34-35 and Prabhupāda's purport and says: 'I want us to undertake some real tapasya — simpler food, fixed early rising, more rounds, less entertainment — for a year before we conceive, so the child comes into a household already shaped by austerity.' The other partner finds this excessive, possibly fanatical, and worries about health, social pressure from family, and timing. A third student plays an older Vaiṣṇava friend the couple has come to for guidance. Run the conversation for 8 minutes. The older friend's job is not to take sides but to help them see what tapas actually means in a gṛhastha context — and what is genuinely at stake in 'preparing' for a child versus just having one.

Objections & Responses
  • This is body-shaming asceticism. Eating only fallen dry leaves and standing in rain and snow is not spirituality — it is self-harm dressed up as devotion. We have moved past valorizing this kind of suffering.
    Notice what the verse actually highlights: śvāsa-rodha-vinirdhūta-mano-malau and upaśāntena cetasā — the breath-control cleansed the dirt of the mind, and the consciousness was peaceful. The point is not the suffering; the point is what the suffering was *for* and what it *produced*. Pṛśni and Sutapā were not punishing the body; they were burning out viṣaya-saṁskāras so the mind could hold one desire purely — Kṛṣṇa Himself. Prabhupāda is explicit that we are not asked to imitate them. He gives us the easy tapas: the holy name. But do not mistake 'easy' for 'no austerity.' Sitting still and chanting attentively for two hours while the mind screams for stimulation is tapas. The verse is not glorifying pain; it is glorifying the gravity of what is being sought.
  • Prabhupāda's line about 'population begotten like cats and dogs' and varṇa-saṅkara sounds harsh and even eugenic. Surely a modern Vaiṣṇava cannot endorse this framing.
    Read it carefully. He is not ranking human beings by birth; he is contrasting two modes of begetting — one in which children are conceived as a side-effect of sense gratification with no spiritual preparation, and one in which they are conceived deliberately, in controlled life, by parents who are themselves practitioners. Varṇa-saṅkara in the Gītā (1.41-42) is not about caste mixing in a sociological sense; it is about progeny born without dharmic intention, who then cannot uphold dharma themselves. The harshness is on us — the parents, the culture — not on the children. And his proposed solution is not exclusion of anyone; it is teaching everyone tapo divyam, beginning with the saṅkīrtana every person can do regardless of birth. The verse and purport together are an indictment of *how we make families*, not of who deserves to exist.
Analogy

Smelters in a forge take rough ore, heat it through summer's furnace and winter's quenching, knock off the scale, and slowly reveal pure gold. Pṛśni and Sutapā's twelve thousand years of enduring rain and sun and ice are the smelter's labor on their own consciousness. The verse names the heat-treatments by name; the gold appears as Kṛṣṇa accepting them as parents.

Stories
Nara-Nārāyaṇa at Badarīkāśrama

Nara and Nārāyaṇa, the two ṛṣis at Badarī, are described in the Bhāgavatam as undergoing endless tapas — exposed to the elements, eating only dry leaves, controlling the breath. Their tapas continues even now, the tradition says.

Pṛśni and Sutapā's tapas resembles theirs feature-for-feature: śīrṇa-parṇa-anila-āhārau — eating fallen leaves and air, śvāsa-rodha — controlling breath. The Bhāgavata is showing Devakī that her current motherhood was earned through ṛṣi-grade austerity in the previous birth.
