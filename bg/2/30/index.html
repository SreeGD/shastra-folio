Important Words
  • dehī — the dweller in the body
    Nominative of dehin. Repeated emphatically — the soul *as inhabitant*. The body is a dwelling; the inhabitant is the subject of every sentence about life.
  • nityam — eternally
    Adverbial — *always*. Not 'sometimes indestructible' or 'usually indestructible'. The indestructibility is uninterrupted.
  • avadhyaḥ — unkillable
    A + vadhya — not to be killed. The legal-moral term: *not to be put to death*. The soul has, as it were, permanent immunity from execution.
  • sarvasya — of everyone
    Genitive — *of every body*. The teaching is now universalized. Not Bhīṣma alone, not the named kings alone, but the dweller in every body whatsoever.
  • bhūtāni — beings
    Plural of bhūta — that which has come to be. With sarvāṇi (all), the verse universalizes: *all beings whatsoever*. The metaphysical foundation laid in verses 11-30 ends with this single comprehensive injunction — no being is an exception.
O descendant of Bharata, he who dwells in the body can never be slain. Therefore you need not grieve for any living being.

Plain meaning (तात्पर्य · Tātparya)
Kṛṣṇa closes the metaphysical section: the same indestructible soul dwells in every body. Since none can be slain, none warrants lamentation — not even those Arjuna is about to fight.
Further notes (विवरण · Vivaraṇa)
This verse concludes the Sāṅkhya-style argument that began at 2.11. Sarvasya ('of all') universalizes the conclusion: not only Bhīṣma and Droṇa, but every being is ultimately beyond Arjuna's power to truly harm. From 2.31 Kṛṣṇa shifts to a different argumentative ground: even on dharma's own terms, the warrior must fight.

Purport

The Lord now concludes the chapter of instruction on the immutable spirit soul. In describing the immortal soul in various ways, Lord Kṛṣṇa establishes that the soul is immortal and the body is temporary. Therefore Arjuna as a kṣatriya should not abandon his duty out of fear that his grandfather and teacher – Bhīṣma and Droṇa – will die in the battle. On the authority of Śrī Kṛṣṇa, one has to believe that there is a soul different from the material body, not that there is no such thing as soul, or that living symptoms develop at a certain stage of material maturity resulting from the interaction of chemicals. Though the soul is immortal, violence is not encouraged, but at the time of war it is not discouraged when there is actual need for it. That need must be justified in terms of the sanction of the Lord, and not capriciously.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda

Translation
30. O Bhārata, the soul in the body of all entities can never be killed. Therefore you should not lament for any being.
Commentary

“Therefore speak clearly. What should I do and not do?” “Do not lament, but rather fight.” In two verses, Kṛṣṇa explains this.

Baladeva Vidyābhūṣaṇa Gauḍīya
Translation
30. O Bhārata, the soul in the body of all entities can never be killed. Therefore you should not lament for any being.
Commentary

Having succinctly taught the nature of the ātmā which is difficult to understand, the Lord concludes the topic by saying that Arjuna should not grieve for the ātmā. Since this jīva (dehī) situated in the destructible bodies of all living entities (dehe) is eternal and cannot be killed at any time, you should not lament for all living entities that have attained bodies including Bhīsma (sarva bhūtāni). You should not lament for the ātmās because they are eternal, and you should not lament for the bodies because they will inevitably be destroyed by their very nature.

Personal Guidance
The dweller in every body is eternal and indestructible — therefore, in any specific situation, do not grieve for any creature.
Practical Applications
  • When you encounter suffering, tend to the body and console the mind, but never mourn the soul as if it were destroyed.
  • Speak about beings — including animals — as eternal dwellers in temporary bodies.
  • Do not let philosophical certainty become an excuse for emotional coldness; act with compassion while knowing the truth.
Reflection Questions
  • How can I hold both the truth of eternity and the duty of compassion at once?
  • Whom have I withdrawn from emotionally in the name of detachment?
Analogy

Strike a flint and see the sparks fly — each one distinct, each one fire, none of them the flint itself, and not one of them extinguished by the wind. The jīvas are like that: plural, individually real, each eternal, all of the same spiritual nature, atomic sparks of Kṛṣṇa's taṭasthā-śakti (cf. BG 15.7). To lament for any creature is to forget that no such spark ever truly dies.
