Important Words
  • guṇān etān atītya — transcending these qualities
    Ati-i — to go across, to step beyond. The chapter's central verb. Not to manage the modes, not to perfect one of them, but to ati-i — to cross over. Note the plural: all three, not one or two.
  • deha-samudbhavān — produced of the body
    Sam-ud-bhava — emerging from. The verse insists the modes arise from embodiment, not from the soul. Once that origin is grasped, transcending them stops feeling like betraying oneself. They were never one's own.
  • vimuktaḥ — fully freed
    Vi-muc — to release thoroughly. The vi- prefix gives the verb its completeness: not freed from one thing but from the four named (birth, death, old age, distress). Released across the board.
  • amṛtam aśnute — he enjoys nectar
    Amṛta — non-death, immortal nectar. Aśnute — he eats, he tastes. Note the present tense: even in this life. The verse promises tasting amṛta now, not only post-mortem. The chapter's mode-vigilance has a positive payoff and the payoff is immediate.
When the embodied being is able to transcend these three modes associated with the material body, he can become free from birth, death, old age and their distresses and can enjoy nectar even in this life.

Plain meaning (तात्पर्य · Tātparya)
Crossing beyond all three modes — yes, even sattva — the embodied jīva slips out from under birth, death, and aging, and tastes amṛta here itself. Liberation is not posthumous policy; it is a present, edible nectar for the guṇātīta.
Further notes (विवरण · Vivaraṇa)
Climaxes the diagnostic arc and triggers Arjuna's question in 14.21. 'Atītya' (having transcended) underscores that all three guṇas must be left behind, including sattva — a key advaita-bhakti convergence point. 'Amṛtam aśnute' (present tense) emphasizes jīvanmukti, freedom while embodied. Cross-ref BG 2.15 ('amṛtatvāya kalpate'), 5.21 ('akṣayaṁ sukham aśnute'), and Kaṭha 1.3.15.

Purport

How one can stay in the transcendental position, even in this body, in full Kṛṣṇa consciousness, is explained in this verse. The Sanskrit word dehī means “embodied.” Although one is within this material body, by his advancement in spiritual knowledge he can be free from the influence of the modes of nature. He can enjoy the happiness of spiritual life even in this body because, after leaving this body, he is certainly going to the spiritual sky. But even in this body he can enjoy spiritual happiness. In other words, devotional service in Kṛṣṇa consciousness is the sign of liberation from material entanglement, and this will be explained in the Eighteenth Chapter. When one is freed from the influence of the modes of material nature, he enters into devotional service.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda

Baladeva Vidyābhūṣaṇa Gauḍīya
Translation
20. Surpassing the three guṇas which give rise to the body, free from the suffering of birth, death and old age, one attains eternal life.
Commentary

The Lord clarifies the meaning of His previous statement about attaining mad bhāvam. Though the ātmā is situated in the body, by the power of distinguishing jīva from the guṇas, he overcomes the three guṇas which give rise to the body (deha samudbhavān) and, freed from birth, death, old age and suffering, experiences the ātmā (amṛtam). Thus mad bhāvam of the previous verse can mean “beyond saṁsāra” in the sense of being deathless (amṛtam), or “becoming qualified for My pure bhakti.” The verse brahma-bhūtaḥ prasannātmā explains this: having attained the state of brahman, being a pure soul, he does not lament in loss of what he has or desire for he does not have, and looks upon all beings as equal; he then attains prema bhakti. (BG 18.54)

Personal Guidance
Transcending the three modes which bind the body, the soul, free from birth, death, old age, and misery — attains the Supreme.
Practical Applications
  • Aim for transcendence, not just balance among the modes.
  • Cultivate the path that leads beyond.
  • Treat full liberation as the actual goal.
Reflection Questions
  • Have I been satisfied with mode-balance instead of transcendence?
  • What does full transcendence call for?
Analogy

A bird that has lived in three nested cages all its life — sattvic, rajasic, tamasic — and one day finds the doors of all three open at once. To step out of all three is transcendence. Stepping merely from the tamasic cage into the sattvic one is not.

Stories
Śukadeva from birth

Born already realized, Śukadeva walked out of his father Vyāsa's hermitage as a naked sixteen-year-old, undisturbed by hunger, weather, mockery, or honor. He had not transcended one mode by means of another; he had stepped out of the modes altogether at the start.

When he arrived at King Parīkṣit's gathering on the Ganges, the assembly rose because the body in front of them was no longer a body in the ordinary sense — it was guṇa-atīta, the verse made flesh. The amṛta the verse promises was the only thing he ate.
