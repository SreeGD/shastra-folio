O Kṛṣṇa .
Important Words
  • lobha — by greed
    *Lobha* — covetousness, greed. One of the six classical inner enemies (*ṣaḍ-ripu*: kāma, krodha, lobha, moha, mada, mātsarya). The Gītā in 16.21 names three gates to hell: *kāma, krodha, lobha*. Arjuna correctly diagnoses the Kauravas. He does not yet see the *moha* (delusion) similarly afflicting himself.
  • upahata — overpowered
    *Upa-han* — to strike down, to be afflicted. Same root *han* (to strike, to kill) that is at the centre of this chapter — *hantum* (to kill), *hatvā* (by killing), *hanyuḥ* (might kill). Vyāsa is hammering the *han*-root: the Kauravas have been *han*-ed by greed; Arjuna is being asked to *han* them in turn. The Gītā will untangle who is really being killed by whom.
  • cetasaḥ — their hearts (consciousness)
    *Cetas* — consciousness, mind-heart. The faculty that *knows*. Arjuna says the Kauravas' *cetas* has been damaged. The Gītā will use this same root *cit-* throughout — *citta* (mind-stuff), *cetana* (consciousness). The whole text is medicine for the *cetas*.
  • kula - kṣaya — destruction of the family
    *Kula* (family, clan, dynasty) + *kṣaya* (destruction, wasting away). Same root *kṣi-* as in *kṣatra*. Note the ironic etymology: the *kṣatra* class itself takes its name from a root that means *destruction*. The kṣatriya's dharma is destruction of the unrighteous; perverted, it becomes *kula-kṣaya*.
  • doṣam — fault
    *Doṣa* — fault, defect, blemish. Same word the Gītā uses repeatedly for *moral defect*. Arjuna sees the *doṣa* clearly. The Gītā does not say his analysis is wrong; it says his prescription is wrong. Seeing the *doṣa* in others is far easier than seeing the *doṣa* of *refusing to act* in oneself.
  • prapaśyadbhiḥ — by those who can see
    Instrumental plural participle of *pra-paś* — to see clearly, to perceive fully. *Pra-* intensifies. "By those *fully seeing*." Arjuna claims clear sight. The Gītā will gently demonstrate that what he calls *clear seeing* is partial: he sees the *doṣa* of action but not the *doṣa* of inaction.
O Janārdana, although these men, their hearts overtaken by greed, see no fault in killing one’s family or quarreling with friends, why should we, who can see the crime in destroying a family, engage in these acts of sin?
Purport

A kṣatriya is not supposed to refuse to battle or gamble when he is so invited by some rival party. Under such an obligation, Arjuna could not refuse to fight, because he had been challenged by the party of Duryodhana. In this connection, Arjuna considered that the other party might be blind to the effects of such a challenge. Arjuna, however, could see the evil consequences and could not accept the challenge. Obligation is actually binding when the effect is good, but when the effect is otherwise, then no one can be bound. Considering all these pros and cons, Arjuna decided not to fight.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Bhūrijana Dāsa Gauḍīya
Translation
O Janardana, although these men, their hearts overtaken by greed, see no fault in killing one's family or quarreling with friends, why should we, who can see the crime in destroying a family, engage in these acts of sin? (cn/DS) To be king of the world is a job for 'mahabhagavats' not for ordinary devotees. The Pandavas had such a high karma to be born in such a powerful family able to govern all the world. Many times Krsna addressed Arjuna 'O prtha!", remarking his high lineage. "This is your family heritage, how can you not fight?" This was the mood of the ksatriyas, they were proud of their families. They would uphold their family tradition. And if this family tradition was destroyed, then practically, because all the rest of the population was dependent on the kings, the rest of the population would be in trouble. And the sacrifices that the elders of the family would perform for the benefit of the forefathers and the future generations which practically the future of the world, these would stop. Therefore Arjuna is saying that too much bad is going to come.

Śrī Rāmānujācārya Classical
Translation
1.26 - 1.47 Arjuna said - Sanjaya said Sanjaya continued: The high-minded Arjuna, extremely kind, deeply friendly, and supremely righteous, having brothers like himself, though repeatedly deceived by the treacherous attempts of your people like burning in the lac-house etc., and therefore fit to be killed by him with the help of the Supreme Person, nevertheless said, 'I will not fight.' He felt weak, overcome as he was by his love and extreme compassion for his relatives. He was also filled with fear, not knowing what was righteous and what unrighteous. His mind was tortured by grief, because of the thought of future separation from his relations. So he threw away his bow and arrow and sat on the chariot as if to fast to death.
Śrī Abhinavagupta Classical
Translation
1.35 1.44 Nihatya etc. upto anususruma. Sin alone is the agent in the act of slaying these desperadoes. Therefore here the idea is this : These ememies of ours have been slain, i.e., have been take possession of, by sin. Sin would come to us also after slaying them. Sin in this context is the disregard, on account of greed etc., to the injurious conseences like the ruination of the family and the like. That is why Arjuna makes a specific mention of the [ruin of the] family etc., and of its duties in the passage 'How by slaying my own kinsmen etc'. The act of slaying, undertaken with an individualizing idea about its result, and with a particularizing idea about the person to be slain, is a great sin. Personal Guidance
Kṛṣṇa-consciousness is not blunted by others' ignorance; though they cannot see the harm in their actions, the devotee sees and acts in dharma anyway.
Practical Applications
  • Stop using others' lack of awareness as an excuse for your own.
  • When you see clearly, act on what you see — don't wait for the unaware to validate it.
  • Pray for those who cannot yet see; do not let their blindness pull you back into yours.
Reflection Questions
  • Where am I waiting for collective consensus to do what is already individually clear?
  • How do I respond when those I love cannot see what dharma requires?
Analogy

Arjuna's argument is the eternal one of the conscientious objector: *they cannot see, but we can — so we, who see, must refrain*. The reasoning sounds noble. It is also exactly the reasoning a deserter uses. The Gītā will not deny the visibility; it will deny the conclusion.
