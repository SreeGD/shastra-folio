Important Words
  • duḥkham — trouble, distress
    The whole motive of rājasa renunciation in one word. The work is given up *because* it causes pain — not because the soul has matured beyond it.
  • bhayāt — out of fear
    Ablative. The renouncer here is identified not by what he gives up but by what he is afraid of. Fear, not vairāgya, drives the action.
  • kleśa — trouble, distress
    Specifically bodily trouble (kāya-kleśa in the compound). The dread of kleśa is what powers rājasa renunciation — comfort, not wisdom, is its motive.
  • rājasam — in the mode of passion
    The middle category. Rājasa-tyāga is more dignified than tāmasa but still bound to the body and its preferences.
  • phalam — fruit, result
    An exquisite irony. He renounced fruits and yet does not even gain the fruit *of renouncing* (tyāga-phalam). Tāmasa and rājasa both miss the real harvest of giving up.
  • labhet — gains
    Optative. With the verse's negation, Kṛṣṇa firmly states that the elevation tyāga is meant to confer will not arrive when its motive is bodily fear.
Anyone who gives up prescribed duties as troublesome or out of fear of bodily discomfort is said to have renounced in the mode of passion. Such action never leads to the elevation of renunciation.

Plain meaning (तात्पर्य · Tātparya)
Quitting an action merely because it hurts the body is renunciation in the mode of passion. Such cowardly drop-out yields nothing of the real fruit of renunciation — the inner freedom that tyāga is meant to deliver.
Further notes (विवरण · Vivaraṇa)
Rājasa-tyāga is calibrated to comfort, not to truth. The motive is 'kāya-kleśa-bhaya' — fear of physical effort. The verse warns against confusing aversion with detachment: the rājasa renunciate looks like a yogī but has only swapped one craving (for ease) for another. Cross-ref to 3.4-6 on the futility of forced inaction.

Purport

One who is in Kṛṣṇa consciousness should not give up earning money out of fear that he is performing fruitive activities. If by working one can engage his money in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, or if by rising early in the morning one can advance his transcendental Kṛṣṇa consciousness, one should not desist out of fear or because such activities are considered troublesome. Such renunciation is in the mode of passion. The result of passionate work is always miserable. If a person renounces work in that spirit, he never gets the result of renunciation.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
8. One who gives up the obligatory actions out of fear of bodily pain, thinking it is an unnecessary inconvenience for the body, performs tyāga in the mode of rajas, and does not attain the intended result of that tyāga.
Commentary

Even though one knows that performance of nitya-karma is necessary and to do it is praiseworthy and to neglect to do it is a sinful, if one rejects the action thinking it is useless trouble to the body, it is known as rajasic tyāga. One will not attain the desired result of tyāga, knowledge, by doing so.

Baladeva Vidyābhūṣaṇa Gauḍīya
Translation
8. One who gives up the obligatory actions out of fear of bodily pain, thinking it is an unnecessary inconvenience for the body, and performs tyāga in the mode of rajas, does not attain the intended result of that tyāga.
Commentary

Even knowing that performing prescribed actions with no desire is a cause of liberation, if the desirer of liberation gives up those actions out of fear of bodily pain (kāya kleśa bhāyāt), such as having difficulties to obtain the materials for sacrifice or disliking the discomfort of a morning bath, his renunciation is in the mode of passion, since such experience of discontent is the quality of the mode of passion. Having given up the actions, that person will not attain steadiness in jṣāna (tyāga phalam), which is the result of performance of the action.

Bhūrijana Dāsa Gauḍīya
Commentary

Someone may take sannyasa because he is having so many business troubles, the family his giving him so much trouble, and his work is so difficult, he finds no happiness in the world... one may take sannyasa and think 'what is the use of working hard to get bread, if I go to Vrndavana I can get so many capatis to eat and I don't have to work at all and I save myself so much trouble.' In that way if someone takes sannyasa and dresses in the sannyasa way ‑ this is in the mode of passion, not what Krsna recommends. A person in this consciousness knows that he should do his duty in detachment but he rejects doing the duty because it is too hard, too difficult. And yet, he still wants to get some spiritual benefit, 'I will be a sadhu, I will be a sannyasi, but I don't work hard, it is too difficult.'

Śrī Rāmānujācārya Classical
Translation
18.8 Although actions constitute the indirect menas for release, yet they produce mental depression, since they can be done only by collecting materials involving painful effort and since they cause bodily strain on account of their reiring strenuous exertion. If, on account of such fear, one decides that the practice of knowledge alone should be tried for perfection in Yoga, and abandons actions like the great sacrifices applicable to one's station in life, he practises renunciation rooted in Rajas. Since that is not the meaning of the Sastras, one cannot win the fruit of renunciation in the form of the rise of knowledge. So it will be shown further one: 'That reason by which one erroneously knows, O Arjuna, is Rajasika' (18.31). In fact, actions do not directly cause purity of the mind but indirectly by winning the grace of God.

Personal Guidance
Renunciation done out of fear of bodily trouble — rajasic; one does not gain the result of renunciation.
Practical Applications
  • Recognize fear-based renunciation as rajasic, not pure.
  • Address the fear; don't bypass it through renunciation.
  • Refine the motive.
Reflection Questions
  • Where is my renunciation fear-based?
  • What addresses the underlying fear?
Analogy

A pilgrim quits the long walk because his feet hurt and the sun is hot. He sits in the shade and calls it renunciation. The shade is real, the rest is real — but the destination is no closer. He has spared his body and lost his pilgrimage.
