Important Words
  • śraddhayā — with faith
    Instrumental of śraddhā. The chapter's recurring word; here qualified by parayā (transcendental). Not generic faith but faith aimed at the Supreme. The same śraddhā Kṛṣṇa diagnosed in 17.2 reappears here in its sattvic apex.
  • parayā — transcendental
    Instrumental of parā — highest, beyond. Qualifies śraddhayā. The verse's modal marker: faith *parā* — directed beyond the material — is what makes the threefold tapas sāttvika. Lower-directed faith would yield the same actions in a lower mode.
  • taptam — executed, heated
    Past participle of √tap — to heat, to perform austerity. The same root as tapas itself. Note the etymology: tapas is fundamentally 'heating' — the disciplined application of inner heat to one's life.
  • tri - vidham — of three kinds
    Here tri-vidha refers not to the three guṇas but to the three previous verses — body, speech, mind. Sāttvika-tapas requires all three together. The same phrase carrying a different referent — read context carefully.
  • aphala - ākāṅkṣibhiḥ — who are without desires for fruits
    Reappearance of the phrase from verse 11. The same condition that made yajña sāttvika now makes tapas sāttvika. The phrase is the chapter's recurring north star: fruit-desirelessness is the universal mark of sattva in any activity.
  • yuktaiḥ — engaged, yoked
    Past participle of √yuj — to yoke, to join. The same root as yoga. The tapasvī is yoked — not merely disciplined but united with the higher purpose. The discipline is in service of the yoke, not the yoke in service of the discipline.
This threefold austerity, performed with transcendental faith by men not expecting material benefits but engaged only for the sake of the Supreme, is called austerity in goodness.

Plain meaning (तात्पर्य · Tātparya)
When the threefold austerity of body, speech, and mind (14-16) is fueled by the highest faith and freed from desire for results, it qualifies as sāttvika. Two qualifications convert mere discipline into spiritual tapas: parā śraddhā and aphala-ākāṅkṣā.
Further notes (विवरण · Vivaraṇa)
Parayā śraddhayā echoes 6.47 (śraddhāvān bhajate yo mām) and 4.39 (śraddhāvāl labhate jñānam). Yuktaiḥ marks the practitioner as already standing in yoga. The verse summarizes the sāttvika pole of tapas and sets up the contrast with rājasa (18) and tāmasa (19) tapas to follow — the same threefold scheme as for yajña (11-13).

Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
17. When these three types of austerity are undertaken with full faith, and without longing for results, they are said to be sattvic.
Commentary

The three types of austerity are those of body, words, and mind (tat tri-vidham).

Baladeva Vidyābhūṣaṇa Gauḍīya
Translation
17. When these three types of austerity are undertaken with full faith, without longing for results, with concentrated mind, they are said to be sāttvika.
Commentary

The austerities according to the three modes are now described in three verses. Austerity of three types (body, words and mind) performed (taptam) by men without desire for results, with concentrated consciousness (yuktaiḥ), with full faith, is said to be in the mode of goodness.

Personal Guidance
These three austerities, performed by the disciplined — without expectation of reward, with faith — are sattvic.
Practical Applications
  • Perform austerities of body, speech, mind together — not in isolation.
  • Pair them with faith and no-reward expectation.
  • Audit the alignment.
Reflection Questions
  • Where have I been austere in one realm but not the others?
  • What integration is needed?
Analogy

Body, speech, and mind disciplined together are like three strings of one vīṇā tuned to the same note. Tune two and leave the third loose and the music will not come — it will only be louder dissonance. Sāttvika-tapas is the moment all three strings ring as one tone, with no expectation of an audience.

Stories
Raghunātha dāsa Gosvāmī

Born to one of the wealthiest families in Bengal, Raghunātha dāsa abandoned everything to live under Caitanya Mahāprabhu's guidance, eventually as a renunciant in Vraja. His tapas is famous for integrating all three austerities: body (a single piece of cloth, eating only what could be begged or salvaged, ultimately reducing intake to a few drops of buttermilk), speech (constant chanting and recitation, never idle talk), and mind (continuous meditation on the līlā of Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa).

Bhakti-ratnākara and the writings of Kṛṣṇadāsa Kavirāja record that all three were undertaken aphala-ākāṅkṣibhiḥ — with no expectation of any reward but service itself. He is the canonical sāttvika-tapasvī of the Gauḍīya line.
