Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.2.22

स एष जीवन् खलु सम्परेतोवर्तेत योऽत्यन्तनृशंसितेन । देहे मृते तं मनुजा: शपन्तिगन्ता तमोऽन्धं तनुमानिनो ध्रुवम् ॥ २२ ॥

A person who is very cruel is regarded as dead even while living, for while he is living or after his death, everyone condemns him. And after the death of a person in the bodily concept of life, he is undoubtedly transferred to the hell known as Andhatama.
Purport

Kaṁsa considered that if he killed his sister, while living he would be condemned by everyone, and after death he would go to the darkest region of hellish life because of his cruelty. It is said that a cruel person like a butcher is advised not to live and not to die. While living, a cruel person creates a hellish condition for his next birth, and therefore he should not live; but he is also advised not to die, because after death he must go to the darkest region of hell. Thus in either circumstance he is condemned. Kaṁsa, therefore, having good sense about the science of the soul’s transmigration, deliberately refrained from killing Devakī. In this verse the words gantā tamo ’ndhaṁ tanu-mānino dhruvam are very important and require extensive understanding. Śrīla Jīva Gosvāmī, in his Vaiṣṇava-toṣaṇī-ṭīkā, says, tatra tanu-māninaḥ pāpina iti dehātma-buddhyaiva pāpābhiniveśo bhavati: “One who lives in the bodily concept, thinking, ‘I am this body,’ involves himself, by the very nature of this conception, in a life of sinful activities. Anyone living in such a conception is to be considered a candidate for hell.” adānta-gobhir viśatāṁ tamisraṁ punaḥ punaś carvita-carvaṇānām ( Bhāg. 7.5.30 ) One who is in a bodily concept of life has no control over sense gratification. Such a person can do anything sinful to eat, drink, be merry and enjoy a life of sense gratification, not knowing of the soul’s transmigration from one body to another. Such a person does whatever he likes, whatever he imagines, and therefore, being subject to the laws of nature, he suffers miserably again and again in different material bodies. yāvat kriyās tāvad idaṁ mano vai karmātmakaṁ yena śarīra-bandhaḥ (Bhāg. 5.5.5 ) In the bodily concept of life, a person is karmānubandha, or conditioned by karma, and as long as the mind is absorbed in karma, one must accept a material body. Śarīra-bandha, bondage to the material body, is a source of misery ( kleśa-da ). na sādhu manye yata ātmano ’yam asann api kleśada āsa dehaḥ ( Bhāg. 5.5.4 ) Although the body is temporary, it always gives one trouble in many ways, but human civilization is now unfortunately based on tanu-mānī, the bodily concept of life, by which one thinks, “I belong to this nation,” “I belong to this group,” “I belong to that group,” and so on. Each of us has his own ideas, and we are becoming increasingly involved, individually, socially, communally and nationally, in the complexities of karmānubandha, sinful activities. For the maintenance of the body, men are killing so many other bodies and becoming implicated in karmānubandha. Therefore Śrīla Jīva Gosvāmī says that tanu-mānī, those in the bodily concept of life, are pāpī, sinful persons. For such sinful persons, the ultimate destination is the darkest region of hellish life ( gantā tamo ’ndham ). In particular, a person who wants to maintain his body by killing animals is most sinful and cannot understand the value of spiritual life. In Bhagavad-gītā (16.19-20) the Lord says: tān ahaṁ dviṣataḥ krūrān saṁsāreṣu narādhamān kṣipāmy ajasram aśubhān āsurīṣv eva yoniṣu āsurīṁ yonim āpannā mūḍhā janmani janmani mām aprāpyaiva kaunteya tato yānty adhamāṁ gatim “Those who are envious and mischievous, who are the lowest among men, are cast by Me into the ocean of material existence, into various demoniac species of life. Attaining repeated birth among the species of demoniac life, such persons can never approach Me. Gradually they sink down to the most abominable type of existence.” A human being is meant to understand the value of human life, which is a boon obtained after many, many births. Therefore one must free oneself from tanu-mānī, the bodily concept of life, and realize the Supreme Personality of Godhead.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
A person who is very cruel is regarded as dead even while living, for while he is living or after his death, everyone condemns him. And after the death of a person in the bodily concept of life, he is undoubtedly transferred to the hell known as Andhatama.
Commentary

"If I kill the child to save my life, I will spend the rest of my life in misfortune." Thus he speaks this verse. "Those who perform cruel acts are dead within a living body. After committing cruel acts and then dying, people will curse me, but even while living they will curse me. I will fear even while alive. They will shout in taunting voices,’You should go to hell for this.’ Those who maintain their bodies by committing violence on others (tanu maninah) will certainly go to hell."

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

“Well, it is still better that you live a little longer.” He answers with this verse. If one commits such sin, one appears to be living but is actually dead, for I will be rejected just as people avoid a corpse by such a violent act (atyanta-nṛśaṁistena). The word khalu indicates conjecture. All men will condemn this act. Śrīdhara Svāmī explains that tanu-māninaḥ means sinful people, for by giving regard to their bodies (tanu-māninaḥ), they become sinful. Men condemn the sinful people while dead, or even while living (dehe amṛte).

Concepts
Jiva
Personal Guidance
Cruelty rooted in tanu-mānī — the bodily concept of life — is a living death; rise above 'I am this body' and take shelter of Kṛṣṇa.
Practical Applications
  • Catch yourself today the moment you say 'I am this nation/group/body' and silently correct it to 'I am the soul, servant of Kṛṣṇa.'
  • Refuse one act of harshness — a cutting word, an impatient dismissal — and recognize it as the seed of cruelty Kaṁsa feared in himself.
  • Examine one daily habit of bodily maintenance (food, comfort, status) and ask whether it is causing harm to other living beings, then adjust it accordingly.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my life am I a tanu-mānī — identifying so completely with this body, family, community, or nation that I am quietly accumulating the very karma that leads to Andhatama?
  • Whose suffering have I been willing to cause — through my diet, my speech, my work, my indifference — because I told myself the body that suffers 'doesn't really count'?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Audit your group chats and social media from the last week — where have you participated in cruelty you would deny in person (mocking, pile-ons, exclusion, leaked screenshots)? Apologize to one person today, in private, with no demand for response.
  • Notice the tanu-mānī identities you are using to gain status on campus — 'I'm pre-med,' 'I'm in this fraternity,' 'I'm the smart one in my friend group.' Pick the one you defend most fiercely and spend a week deliberately not leading with it; observe what comes up.
  • Try one full day of vegetarian eating offered to Kṛṣṇa before consumption, and at the end of the day write down honestly what shifted — in your body, your mood, and your sense of who you actually are underneath the appetites you assumed were 'you.'
householder
  • Examine the cruelties built into your household economy — what you buy, who makes it, what you eat, who suffers for it. Pick one supply chain (food, clothing, or one regularly purchased item) and change it this month toward less violence; explain the change to your children in plain words.
  • Before your next significant family decision (school, move, career, money), name aloud to your spouse which choice is sva-dharma and which is tanu-mānī ambition wearing sva-dharma's clothes. Let your spouse push back. Decide only after that conversation.
  • Establish one daily ritual where the whole family offers food to Kṛṣṇa before eating. The point is not the ritual's perfection but that your children grow up knowing the body is maintained by Kṛṣṇa's mercy, not by their parents' striving — this is the only inheritance that survives your death.
senior
  • List the people you have been quietly cruel to over a long life — through neglect, withheld words, status games, family politics. Before you die, write or speak to as many as you still can. Do not negotiate the apology; offer it and let it land where it lands.
  • Identify the tanu-mānī attachments that intensify with age — pride in lineage, fixation on the body's decline, hoarding for grandchildren, defending your reputation. Pick one and begin deliberately loosening it: give the asset away, drop the grievance, stop the narrative mid-sentence when you catch it starting again.
  • You are now closer to the next body than to this one's beginning. Mentor at least one younger person — not in your career skills but in what you wish you had known about the soul, the body, and Kṛṣṇa. Speak from your failures, not your successes; the dhīra teaches by what he no longer needs to defend.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading this verse as being about 'cruel people out there' (Kaṁsa, butchers, tyrants) and missing that Prabhupāda points the finger at anyone living in the bodily concept — including the respectable, well-spoken practitioner who eats and lives at the cost of other bodies.
  • Twisting 'the soul is eternal, the body is temporary' into a license for callousness — concluding that since others are not their bodies, their bodily suffering at our hands is no real harm. Real karuṇā for the soul never requires cruelty to the body that houses it.
  • Treating the warning about Andhatama as mythological scenery to be skipped over, and so failing to feel the actual gravity Kaṁsa felt — a gravity strong enough that even a demon, on hearing it, stayed his hand. If a demon's discrimination outpaces ours, the teaching has not yet landed.
Discussion Prompts
  • Kaṁsa restrained himself from killing Devakī not out of compassion but out of fear of hellish reactions. Share with your partner: name one cruelty you have refrained from this month, and honestly examine — was your restraint sva-dharma, fear of consequences, or social image management? Does the motive matter if the outward action is the same?
  • Śrīla Jīva Gosvāmī equates tanu-mānī (those in the bodily concept) with pāpī (sinful). That means our 'I belong to this nation / community / family' identifications are themselves implicated in karmānubandha. In your group, name one identity you would defend with violence — verbal or otherwise — and let the others push back: is that defense Kṛṣṇa-conscious, or is it tanu-mānī dressed up as duty?
  • Prabhupāda points out that for the maintenance of one body, men kill many other bodies. Discuss: where in your own consumption (food, fashion, technology, entertainment) are you participating in cruelty you have rendered invisible? Disagree openly — is the householder's complicity unavoidable, or are we hiding behind 'practicality'?
Role Play

Two students take roles. Student A is a successful corporate manager who has just been asked by leadership to lay off twelve people right before the holidays to hit a quarterly number; doing it will secure A's promotion, refusing will likely end A's career at the firm. Student B is A's elder Vaiṣṇava friend who has come for evening tea. A wants validation: 'It's just business, I'm not the one who decided, and if I don't do it someone else will.' B's task is not to give easy answers but to walk A through SB 10.2.22 — the difference between Kaṁsa's calculated restraint and real karuṇā, the trap of tanu-mānī ('it's just bodies, just headcount'), and what sva-dharma actually requires here. The role-play ends not with a decision but with A naming, out loud, what the bodily concept has been hiding from them. Swap roles and run again.

Objections & Responses
  • This whole verse is fear-based morality — 'be good or you'll go to Andhatama hell.' That's exactly the kind of religion modern people have rightly outgrown. Shouldn't we behave well because it's right, not because we're scared of post-mortem punishment?
    The verse is not the foundation of Vaiṣṇava ethics; it is a diagnostic Kaṁsa himself uses, and Prabhupāda preserves it because it is psychologically honest. Most of us are not yet at the stage of pure prema where 'because it is right' is a sufficient motive — if we were, we would already be acting that way. Śāstra meets us where we are: fear of hell is a lower rung, sva-dharma is a middle rung, and bhakti-yoga out of love for Kṛṣṇa is the top rung. The Gauḍīya teacher does not despise the lower rung; he uses it to get the foot off the floor. Notice also what the verse actually targets: not isolated bad acts but the tanu-mānī worldview that produces them. The remedy is not better behavior management — it is the dissolution of the bodily concept through bhakti. Fear is the alarm; surrender to Kṛṣṇa is the medicine.
  • Calling people who eat meat 'most sinful' and destined for the darkest hell is exactly the kind of dogmatic, judgmental religion that alienates everyone. My uncle is the kindest man I know and he eats chicken every day. Are you telling me he is going to Andhatama?
    Two things are being conflated here — personal worth and karmic mechanics. Vaiṣṇavas do not assess your uncle's soul; that is Kṛṣṇa's department, and your uncle, like everyone, is part-and-parcel of Him and infinitely dear. What śāstra describes is a law, not an insult: where the bodily concept reigns, the maintenance of one body costs many others, and that cost is real, whether or not the kind uncle ever sees it. Kindness within a sphere (to family, friends, even pets) coexisting with violence to those outside the sphere is precisely the karuṇā of māyā that the Gītā exposes — Arjuna's compassion for his kinsmen was also 'kindness,' and Kṛṣṇa called it weakness of heart. Real karuṇā extends to every living being because every living being is a soul. So the question is not 'is my uncle a bad person' but 'what is my uncle's relationship to the law of karmānubandha, and what would it mean to love him enough to gently offer him prasādam?'
