Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.1.41

स्वप्ने यथा पश्यति देहमीद‍ृशंमनोरथेनाभिनिविष्टचेतन: । द‍ृष्टश्रुताभ्यां मनसानुचिन्तयन्प्रपद्यते तत् किमपि ह्यपस्मृति: ॥ ४१ ॥

Important Words
  • svapne — in a dream
    Locative of svapna, 'dream.' The Bhāgavata's favored analogy for ordinary consciousness. In Gauḍīya: jāgrat-svapna-suṣupti are all conditioned states; turīya is the bhāva-state of pure devotion. The dream-analogy is doctrinally precise, not poetic.
  • abhiniviṣṭa — is fully absorbed
    Abhi (toward) + ni-viṣṭa (entered). The same word is used in BG 13.18-style descriptions. The verse identifies the operative mechanism: it is abhiniveśa (deep absorption) that authors the next embodiment. Bhakti's task is to make Kṛṣṇa the object of abhiniveśa.
  • dṛṣṭa-śrutābhyām — by what has been seen and heard
    Dṛṣṭa (seen) + śruta (heard). Both feed the mind. The Gauḍīya commitment to controlled hearing (śravaṇa-saṁyama) starts here: what you let in through eye and ear becomes the material from which your next body is built.
  • prapadyate — surrenders to
    Pra + pad, 'falls toward, surrenders to.' Same root as prapatti. The verse uses prapadyate of the conditioned soul surrendering to its dream — bhakti uses the same word for surrendering to Bhagavān (BG 18.66, mām ekaṁ śaraṇaṁ vraja). Same verb; opposite direction.
  • apasmṛtiḥ — experiencing forgetfulness of the present body
    Apa (away) + smṛti (memory). Forgetfulness. Kṛṣṇa says (BG 15.15) mattaḥ smṛtir jñānam apohanaṁ ca — from Him come both memory and forgetfulness. The Bhāgavata reminds: smṛti and apasmṛti are Bhagavān's own administration; the devotee asks for the right one.
Having experienced a situation by seeing or hearing about it, one contemplates and speculates about that situation, and thus one surrenders to it, not considering his present body. Similarly, by mental adjustments one dreams at night of living under different circumstances, in different bodies, and forgets his actual position. Under this same process, one gives up his present body and accepts another [tathā dehāntara-prāptiḥ].
Purport

Transmigration of the soul is very clearly explained in this verse. One sometimes forgets his present body and thinks of his childhood body, a body of the past, and of how one was playing, jumping, talking and so on. When the material body is no longer workable, it becomes dust: “For dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return.” But when the body again mixes with the five material elements — earth, water, fire, air and ether — the mind continues to work. The mind is the subtle substance in which the body is created, as we actually experience in our dreams and also when we are awake in contemplation. One must understand that the process of mental speculation develops a new type of body that does not actually exist. If one can understand the nature of the mind ( manorathena ) and its thinking, feeling and willing, one can very easily understand how from the mind different types of bodies develop. The Kṛṣṇa consciousness movement, therefore, offers a process of transcendental activities wherein the mind is fully absorbed in affairs pertaining to Kṛṣṇa. The presence of the soul is perceived by consciousness, and one must purify his consciousness from material to spiritual, or, in other words, to Kṛṣṇa consciousness. That which is spiritual is eternal, and that which is material is temporary. Without Kṛṣṇa consciousness, one’s consciousness is always absorbed in temporary things. For everyone, therefore, Kṛṣṇa recommends in Bhagavad-gītā (9.34) , man-manā bhava mad-bhakto mad-yājī māṁ namaskuru. One should always be absorbed in thought of Kṛṣṇa, one should become His devotee, one should always engage in His service and worship Him as the supreme great, and one should always offer Him obeisances. In the material world one is always a servant of a greater person, and in the spiritual world our constitutional position is to serve the Supreme, the greatest, paraṁ brahma. This is the instruction of Śrī Caitanya Mahāprabhu. Jīvera ‘svarūpa’ haya-kṛṣṇera ‘nitya-dāsa’ (Cc. Madhya 20.108). To act in Kṛṣṇa consciousness is the perfection of life and the highest perfection of yoga. As Lord Kṛṣṇa says in Bhagavad-gītā (6.47) : yoginām api sarveṣāṁ mad-gatenāntarātmanā śraddhāvān bhajate yo māṁ sa me yuktatamo mataḥ “Of all yogīs, he who always abides in Me with great faith, worshiping Me in transcendental loving service, is most intimately united with Me in yoga and is the highest of all.” The condition of the mind, which flickers between saṅkalpa and vikalpa, accepting something and rejecting it, is very important in transferring the soul to another material body at the time of death. yaṁ yaṁ vāpi smaran bhāvaṁ tyajaty ante kalevaram taṁ tam evaiti kaunteya sadā tad-bhāva-bhāvitaḥ “Whatever state of being one remembers when he quits his body, that state he will attain without fail.” ( Bg. 8.6 ) Therefore one must train the mind in the system of bhakti-yoga, as did Mahārāja Ambarīṣa, who kept himself always in Kṛṣṇa consciousness. Sa vai manaḥ kṛṣṇa-padāravindayoḥ . One must fix the mind at the lotus feet of Kṛṣṇa twenty-four hours a day. If the mind is fixed upon Kṛṣṇa’s lotus feet, the activities of the other senses will be engaged in Kṛṣṇa’s service. Hṛṣīkeṇa hṛṣīkeśa-sevanaṁ bhaktir ucyate: to serve Hṛṣīkeśa, the master of the senses, with purified senses is called bhakti. Those who constantly engage in devotional service are situated in a transcendental state, above the material modes of nature. As Kṛṣṇa says in Bhagavad-gītā (14.26) : māṁ ca yo ’vyabhicāreṇa bhakti-yogena sevate sa guṇān samatītyaitān brahma-bhūyāya kalpate “One who engages in full devotional service, who does not fall down in any circumstance, at once transcends the modes of material nature and thus comes to the level of Brahman.” One must learn the secret of success from the Vedic literatures, especially when the cream of Vedic knowledge is presented by Bhagavad-gītā as it is. Because the mind is ultimately controlled by the Supreme Personality of Godhead, Kṛṣṇa, the word apasmṛtiḥ is significant. Forgetfulness of one’s own identity is called apasmṛtiḥ. This apasmṛtiḥ can be controlled by the Supreme Lord, for the Lord says, mattaḥ smṛtir jñānam apohanaṁ ca: “From Me come remembrance, knowledge and forgetfulness.” Instead of allowing one to forget one’s real position, Kṛṣṇa can revive one’s original identity at the time of one’s death, even though the mind may be flickering. Although the mind may work imperfectly at the time of death, Kṛṣṇa gives a devotee shelter at His lotus feet. Therefore when a devotee gives up his body, the mind does not take him to another material body ( tyaktvā dehaṁ punar janma naiti mām eti ); rather, Kṛṣṇa takes the devotee to that place where He is engaged in His pastimes ( mām eti ), as we have already discussed in previous verses. One’s consciousness, therefore, must always be absorbed in Kṛṣṇa, and then one’s life will be successful. Otherwise the mind will carry the soul to another material body. The soul will be placed in the semen of a father and discharged into the womb of a mother. The semen and ovum create a particular type of body according to the form of the father and mother, and when the body is mature, the soul emerges in that body and begins a new life. This is the process of transmigration of the soul from one body to another ( tathā dehāntara-prāptiḥ ). Unfortunately, those who are less intelligent think that when the body disappears, everything is finished. The entire world is being misled by such fools and rascals. But as stated in Bhagavad-gītā (2.20) , na hanyate hanyamāne śarīre. The soul does not die when the body is destroyed. Rather, the soul takes on another body.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Having experienced a situation by seeing or hearing about it, one contemplates and speculates about that situation, and thus one surrenders to it, not considering his present body. Similarly, by mental adjustments one dreams at night of living under different circumstances, in different bodies, and forgets his actual position. Under this same process, one gives up his present body and accepts another [tathā dehāntara-prāptiḥ [Bg. 2.13]].
Commentary

Vasudeva now shows and example from everyday life. Just as in a dream one sees a body similar to the one in waking state (idrsam), so a person whose consciousness is deeply absorbed in someone through seeing (such as a king) or through hearing (for example, hearing about Indra) one sees oneself as the king or Indra, so that one can enjoy similar to the king or Indra, and one forgets ones original body.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

According to Śrīdhara Svāmī an example is given to understand by personal experience. Hi indicates that one forgets even in the waking state. What is seen or heard applies to both dreaming and waking states. One can thus understand the difference between the ātmā and the body. In dreams and in fantasy by giving up identity with the body and becoming absorbed in different body, one can realize one’s difference from one’s body. Because of the control of karma, on attaining the new body one gives up the old body. This of course confirms that attaining another body is unavoidable

Personal Guidance
Just as the mind builds dream-bodies from what it dwells on, at death it carries the soul into a new body shaped by its strongest absorption — so absorb it in Kṛṣṇa now.
Practical Applications
  • Notice three times today when your mind is dragging you into a fantasy 'body' — a future role, a past glory, a grievance — and re-anchor it at Kṛṣṇa's lotus feet by chanting His name.
  • Before sleep tonight, deliberately offer the day's seeing and hearing to Kṛṣṇa, so the mind rehearses Him rather than viṣaya as you cross into the dream state.
  • Refuse to feed the inputs (scrolling, gossip, idle entertainment) that you would not want to be your last thought at death — cut one such input starting today.
Reflection Questions
  • Which 'dream-body' am I currently inhabiting in waking life — what mental rehearsal of a future self, role, or circumstance have I surrendered to, forgetting the body and duty actually in front of me?
  • If at this moment I had to give up this body, what bhāva would my mind carry me toward — and does my daily sādhana actually train it toward Kṛṣṇa's lotus feet, or toward something else I haven't admitted?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Audit one week of your scrolling and series-watching. The verse says we 'surrender' to whatever we repeatedly see and hear (dṛṣṭa-śrutābhyāṁ manasānucintayan) — the influencer's body you keep watching, the relationship you keep imagining, the version of yourself you keep rehearsing in your head. Name out loud the one fantasy-body you have been building, and cut the input feeding it for seven days.
  • Before an exam or a hard conversation, notice how much of the night before is spent in manoratha — replaying imagined scenarios, fantasy victories, fantasy disasters. Replace ten minutes of that loop with chanting Hare Kṛṣṇa or reading one verse with translation. You are not killing ambition; you are refusing to let the mind cast you in a body that does not exist yet.
  • When a friend group or a romantic interest starts to define who you are — 'I am the kind of person who...' — recognize this as an ahaṅkāra-body the mind is stitching together. Speak with one elder devotee or mentor this month who can mirror back to you the eternal soul beneath the college-self you are constructing.
householder
  • Notice the bodies you are mentally rehearsing for your children — the doctor-child, the IIT-child, the obedient-child. The verse warns that what we contemplate, we surrender to. Sit with your spouse this week and ask: which of these projected futures is for the child's soul, and which is a manoratha of our own ahaṅkāra wearing a parental costume?
  • At the end of each workday, before opening the next screen, pause for two minutes and offer the day's actions to Kṛṣṇa (man-manā bhava mad-bhakto). The householder's danger is not laziness — it is that every working hour silently builds the body of a 'successful professional' the mind intends to inhabit forever. Interrupt that construction daily.
  • Examine one major financial or career decision pending right now. Ask honestly: am I choosing this because dharma and family welfare require it, or because I have been contemplating a lifestyle-body (the house, the title, the vacation) and am now surrendering to that imagined form? Bring this to a senior Vaiṣṇava before you decide.
senior
  • The vivid memories of childhood and middle-age that now visit unbidden are not nostalgia — Prabhupāda explicitly says the mind continues to work even as bodies dissolve, and these are the very mechanism that will shape the next birth. Each time a memory-body rises, audibly say 'Kṛṣṇa' and turn the mind toward His lotus feet. Practice this now, while the mind is still trainable, so it is automatic at the final hour.
  • Identify one attachment — a property, a grudge, a role you held — that you are still mentally inhabiting decades after it ended. This is a phantom body the mind refuses to cremate. Take one concrete act this month to formally release it: write the will, make the call, give the thing away. Loosen the grip before the grip becomes the next womb.
  • Mentor at least one younger devotee or family member specifically on the verse's teaching of dehāntara-prāpti — not as doctrine, but as your own observed experience of bodies passed through. The senior years are the unique window when this teaching can be transmitted with the authority of someone who has actually watched bodies come and go. Do not let that window close in silence.
Common Pitfalls
  • Treating transmigration as an abstract metaphysical doctrine to debate, rather than as the urgent reason to fix the mind on Kṛṣṇa today — turning the verse into theory while the mind keeps building its next material body unchecked.
  • Assuming that because Kṛṣṇa can revive a devotee's smṛti at the moment of death, one can postpone serious sādhana now — ignoring that the apasmṛtiḥ-controlling mercy comes to those who have actually trained the mind in bhakti-yoga like Mahārāja Ambarīṣa, not to those who merely intend to.
  • Confusing vivid manorathena fantasy life — planning, replaying, speculating — with genuine contemplation, and so mistaking a mind absorbed in viṣaya for a mind being purified, when in fact each rehearsed scenario is shaping the next body.
Discussion Prompts
  • Prabhupāda says the mind 'develops a new type of body that does not actually exist' through manorathena. Share with your partner one fantasy-body you currently inhabit in your daydreams (the future-you who has lost the weight, gotten the promotion, won the argument). Then disagree: is rehearsing this future a harmless motivation, or is it the very mechanism of dehāntara-prāpti this verse warns against? Push each other — don't settle for 'it depends.'
  • The verse equates dreaming, contemplating what we've seen-and-heard, and transmigration as one continuous process. In your group, argue both sides: (a) modern media — the films we binge, the feeds we scroll — is a serious soteriological emergency because we are literally building our next body in viṣaya; (b) this is overstated; bhakti-yoga can absorb these inputs without harm. Where does each of you actually stand, not just theoretically?
  • Apasmṛtiḥ — forgetfulness of one's real position — is what Kṛṣṇa Himself can grant or withdraw. Yet Prabhupāda also insists we must train the mind via sādhana like Mahārāja Ambarīṣa. Discuss the apparent tension: if Kṛṣṇa controls smṛti and apohanam, why exert ourselves at all? Where does each of you over-rely on one side — passive 'Kṛṣṇa will handle it' or anxious 'I must fix my mind by sheer effort'?
Role Play

Two students pair up. Student A is a hospice volunteer sitting with a dying 70-year-old man who whispers, 'I keep seeing my childhood home, my mother calling me in for dinner — am I going crazy?' Student B is the dying man. Student A must respond from this verse — without quoting Sanskrit at the bedside — helping him understand that the mind's vivid return to a past body is not madness but the very mechanism by which the soul will soon transit to a next body, and gently turning his attention toward Kṛṣṇa as the only shelter that breaks the cycle. After 5 minutes, swap. Then debrief: where did you slip into generic 'it's okay, let go' platitudes versus actually transmitting the verse's teaching on dehāntara-prāpti and apasmṛtiḥ?

Objections & Responses
  • This is just pre-scientific cosmology dressed up as psychology. Dreams are neurological — REM sleep, memory consolidation. Equating them with 'transmigration' is a category error. Why should I take the leap from 'the mind imagines bodies in dreams' to 'therefore the soul transmigrates to a new body at death'?
    The verse is not arguing from dreams to transmigration as a deductive proof — it is offering a direct experiential analogy you can verify in your own consciousness right now. You already know, without a microscope, that something in you persists through last night's dream-body, through your eight-year-old body, through the body you had before this conversation. That continuity is not neurology; neurology is what the continuity observes. Prabhupāda's point is sharper: the same subtle mechanism (manorathena — mental rehearsal of what is seen and heard) that fabricates a dream-body tonight is fabricating, by accumulation, the gross body you will wear next. Dismiss that and you have not refuted the verse; you have only declined to look. The Gauḍīya invitation is not 'believe this'; it is 'observe your own mind for one week and notice whether it is, in fact, building bodies it intends to inhabit.'
  • If Kṛṣṇa controls remembrance and forgetfulness Himself (mattaḥ smṛtir jñānam apohanaṁ ca), and He gives the devotee shelter even when the mind is flickering at death, then the whole urgency around training the mind collapses. I'll just chant a bit, call myself a devotee, and trust Him to bail me out at the end. Why the heavy sādhana?
    This is the exact misreading Prabhupāda anticipates by citing Mahārāja Ambarīṣa in the same purport. Kṛṣṇa's rescue at the moment of death is not a courtesy extended to anyone who claimed the label; it is extended to those whose mind has been actually placed at His lotus feet — sa vai manaḥ kṛṣṇa-padāravindayoḥ — through years of hṛṣīkeṇa hṛṣīkeśa-sevanam. The mind at death does not suddenly turn toward Kṛṣṇa if it spent fifty years turning toward viṣaya; it goes where it has been trained to go (yaṁ yaṁ vāpi smaran bhāvaṁ). Surrender is not a verbal contract that bypasses the mind's grooves — it is the substance that wears those grooves into the shape of bhakti. The 'shelter' you are counting on is real, and it is reserved for the one who has actually taken shelter, daily, in small acts, long before the final breath.
Analogy

Vasudeva explains transmigration by the analogy of a dream: in sleep, by mental adjustment, one sees oneself in different bodies and forgets the body lying on the bed. Death is a longer version of the same mechanism — the mind, absorbed by its dominant thought, projects the next body and forgets the present one. The verse teaches that the next body is being authored, right now, by what the mind is rehearsing tonight.

Stories
Bharata Mahārāja and the deer

Emperor Bharata, after renouncing his kingdom, was performing severe austerities by the river when he rescued an orphaned deer. His affection for the fawn grew until, at the moment of death, his mind was fixed on the deer rather than on Bhagavān (SB 5.7–8).

He was reborn as a deer in his next life — but because of his prior practice, he retained memory of his fall and resolved not to be caught again by saṅga. In his third life as Jaḍa-Bharata he refused to speak or engage, lest any new attachment author another wrong rebirth.

The verse here teaches the principle Bharata's life demonstrates: at the moment of death, the mind's last absorbing thought becomes the next body. Vasudeva is using this principle to reason with Kaṁsa, but the verse remains as the personal warning to every hearer.
