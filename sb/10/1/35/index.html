Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.1.35

Kaṁsa was a condemned personality in the Bhoja dynasty because he was envious and sinful. Therefore, upon hearing this omen from the sky, he caught hold of his sister’s hair with his left hand and took up his sword with his right hand to sever her head from her body.
Purport

Kaṁsa was driving the chariot and controlling the reins with his left hand, but as soon as he heard the omen that his sister’s eighth child would kill him, he gave up the reins, caught hold of his sister’s hair, and with his right hand took up a sword to kill her. Before, he had been so affectionate that he was acting as his sister’s chariot driver, but as soon as he heard that his self-interest or his life was at risk, he forgot all affection for her and immediately became a great enemy. This is the nature of demons. No one should trust a demon, despite any amount of affection. Aside from this, a king, a politician or a woman cannot be trusted, since they can do anything abominable for their personal interest. Cāṇakya Paṇḍita therefore says, viśvāso naiva kartavyaḥ strīṣu rāja-kuleṣu ca.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Kaṁsa was a condemned personality in the Bhoja dynasty because he was envious and sinful. Therefore, upon hearing this omen from the sky, he caught hold of his sister's hair with his left hand and took up his sword with his right hand to sever her head from her body.
Commentary

Kamsa, who contaminates his family with sin (kula pamsana), prepared to kill her using a knife. His left hand which out of affection was holding the reins of the horses to drive his sister, suddenly held her hair in order to kill her. And throwing away the whip from his right hand, he took up his sword. The affection of material people is flickering, transforming to hate, without regard for religious principles, and most shameful even in the eyes of the common man, so that the person can even murder.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

As soon as the voice spoke (iti), without considering the meaning, Kaṁsa acted, because he was without good mentality (pāpaḥ). Because he was the personification of evil, he did not consider whether his act was sinful, since from birth he was the contaminator of his dynasty, what to speak of contamination of his own self. Therefore immediately he attempted to kill her. Ādikarmaṇi kattari tkaḥ: the past participle form can denote the agent when it expresses the beginning of an action. (Pāṇini 3.4.71) He began to kill her (saḥ hantum arabdhaḥ). He did not try to do it secretly by poison, but by a sword, not covering it with cloth, and without considering that touching her was a condemned act. He grabbed her by the hair.

Personal Guidance
When self-interest is threatened, the demoniac drops all affection and turns on even kin — guard your heart against this asuric reflex.
Practical Applications
  • Notice the moment today when a perceived threat to your interest tempts you to drop affection for someone close — pause and refuse the asuric reflex.
  • Identify one relationship where you have been a 'chariot driver' only while it served you, and offer one act of service that costs you something.
  • Refuse to extend full trust where Cāṇakya warns against it — keep discernment alive even with those who have shown you affection.
Reflection Questions
  • When my own self-interest feels threatened, whose well-being is the first to drop from my hand — like Kaṁsa dropping the reins to seize his sister's hair — and what does that reveal about the affection I claimed to have?
  • Where in my life am I treating affection as a calculation that holds only as long as the other person poses no risk to me, and what would it mean to root my care for them in the soul rather than in my own safety?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Look at your group chat or closest friend circle: who became cold, exclusionary, or weaponized shared secrets the moment a romantic interest, internship spot, or college admission threatened their position? Name it honestly to yourself — not to gossip, but to stop being naive about who is actually safe.
  • Before posting anything personal — a struggle, a family situation, a setback — ask whether you have confused 'audience' with 'sangha.' Followers and even close friends will, under their own rajas, repurpose what you share the moment it serves them. Practice tyāga: keep what is sacred off the feed.
  • When a teacher, coach, or senior student suddenly turns on you after months of warmth, do not waste the next week asking 'what did I do wrong?' Do svādhyāya of this verse instead: ask what shifted in their self-interest. The answer will usually be obvious, and it will free you from false self-blame.
householder
  • In every major family financial decision — inheritance, a sibling asking for a loan, a business partnership with a relative — write down beforehand what each party actually stands to gain or lose. Affection is real, but nyāya requires you to see the structural pressures honestly before you sign anything.
  • With your children, do not raise them on the fairy tale that 'family always has your back.' Teach them, with concrete examples from the Bhāgavatam (Kaṁsa with Devakī, Hiraṇyakaśipu with Prahlāda), that one's guṇa governs one's behavior under pressure — and that their job is to develop the discrimination to recognize it, including in themselves.
  • When a colleague, client, or business partner begins behaving in ways that contradict their stated affection for you, do not extend the benefit of the doubt for a third or fourth round. Real compassion — for them and for your dependents — sometimes means clear separation. Practice the vairāgya of letting a relationship die honestly rather than rotting it with forced sentimentality.
senior
  • Review the major betrayals and sudden turns you have experienced across your life — by relatives, business associates, even by people you mentored. Instead of carrying residual bitterness, do svādhyāya: in each case, where did the other person's self-interest pivot, and what blindness in you missed the warning? Offer the lesson, not the wound, to those younger than you.
  • As you write your will, distribute possessions, or decide who receives what — do not let sentiment about who 'should' love you whom override your nyāya about who actually will steward what you leave. Tyāga at this stage means letting go of the fantasy of being universally cherished after death.
  • When mentoring younger devotees or family members, do not protect them from the hard teaching of this verse with cheerful platitudes about human goodness. Tell them plainly what you have seen — that affection collapses under acute self-interest in anyone not anchored in bhakti — and let that hard-won discrimination be part of the legacy you transmit.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading this verse only as a condemnation of Kaṁsa 'out there' and never as a mirror for the smaller, quieter ways I withdraw care the moment my comfort or status is at stake.
  • Misusing Cāṇakya Paṇḍita's caution (viśvāso naiva kartavyaḥ strīṣu rāja-kuleṣu ca) as a license for cynicism or contempt toward whole categories of people, rather than as sober discrimination grounded in protecting one's sādhana.
  • Assuming demonic nature is exotic and far away, and so failing to notice the same ahaṅkāra-driven reflex in oneself — where 'my life,' 'my plan,' 'my position' instantly overrides every bond the moment it is threatened.
Discussion Prompts
  • Kaṁsa shifts from affectionate chariot-driver to executioner the instant his self-interest is threatened. Share with your partner a moment when you watched yourself (or someone close to you) flip on a person you 'loved' the moment your security felt at risk — what was the actual trigger, and what did that reveal about the quality of the affection?
  • Prabhupāda quotes Cāṇakya: 'a king, a politician, or a woman cannot be trusted' — and adds that no demon can be trusted regardless of affection. Argue both sides in your group: is this a culturally bound aphorism we should quietly retire, or a hard nyāya about how rajas/tamas distort judgment that we soften at our peril? Try to actually disagree, not negotiate a comfortable middle.
  • Devakī had every external reason to trust her own brother — he was driving her chariot on her wedding day. The verse tells us that sva-dharma-blindness to a person's guṇa is itself a kind of violence we permit. Where in your own life are you currently 'letting Kaṁsa drive the chariot' because acknowledging his nature would cost you too much socially or emotionally?
Role Play

Two students. Student A is a young professional whose business partner of five years (Student B) has just learned that A is being courted by a larger firm that does not want B included in the deal. The partnership was warm — vacations together, families intermingled. B, sensing the threat to his livelihood, is already moving to lock A out of shared accounts and contracts 'just to protect himself.' A has come to a senior devotee for counsel. Run the scene three times: (1) A naively insists 'he would never hurt me — we're like brothers'; (2) the senior devotee walks A through the Kaṁsa-Devakī dynamic, distinguishing real compassion (protecting the soul's progress, including B's) from material compassion (preserving the comfortable fiction of the friendship); (3) A practices what nyāya — righteous, clear-eyed action — actually looks like without descending into vairāgya as a mask for revenge. Debrief: where did each of you feel the pull to flatten the situation into either sentimentality or cynicism?

Objections & Responses
  • Prabhupāda's purport — 'a king, a politician, or a woman cannot be trusted' — is plainly misogynistic and reflects medieval Indian social bias, not eternal truth. Why should I take spiritual instruction from a text that lumps half of humanity in with tyrants?
    Take the verse on its own terms before reaching for the cultural critique. Cāṇakya's aphorism is a nyāya about structural position, not essence: those whose role places them under intense rajas-and-tamas pressure — sovereign power, political ambition, or (in the classical setting) economic and social dependence that forces survival-calculation — will, under threat, act from that pressure rather than from affection. The verse is not saying women are wicked; it is saying that wherever ahaṅkāra is yoked to acute self-preservation, affection is a thin veneer. Test it empirically in your own life: have you not seen exactly this pattern in powerful executives, in politicians, and yes, in people of any gender whose security suddenly feels threatened? The Gauḍīya teacher's job is not to defend every word as sociology but to ask: do I trust anyone — including myself — past the point where their self-interest and my welfare diverge? That is the verse's question, and it cuts in every direction.
  • This whole framing of 'demons cannot be trusted' is paranoid and tribal. Real spiritual maturity is to see Krishna in everyone — to extend trust and love universally, not to start sorting people into asuras and devas.
    Seeing Krishna in everyone is the perception of a paramahaṁsa — and even Kṛṣṇa Himself, in BG 16.1 onward, spends an entire chapter teaching Arjuna to discriminate daivī sampad from āsurī sampad. Discrimination is not the opposite of love; it is the precondition for real love. To 'trust' a person whose guṇa makes them act like Kaṁsa is not compassion — it is participation in the harm they will inevitably do, to you and to others. Real bhakti loves the soul of every being while remaining unsentimental about the conditioned nature currently driving the body. A doctor does not 'trust' a tumor because she sees Krishna in the patient. Svadhyāya of this verse trains exactly that double vision: see the soul, name the guṇa, act with nyāya. Anything less is not universal love — it is spiritual cowardice dressed up as it.
