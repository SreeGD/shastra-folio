Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.1.40

Important Words
  • padā ekena — on one foot
    Padā (instrumental of pada, foot) + ekena (with one). The walking analogy: the foot on the ground holds the body while the other lifts. Karma-grip on the present body holds until the next body has been gripped. The verse forbids the fantasy of a footless moment.
  • tṛṇa-jalaukā — a worm on a vegetable
    Tṛṇa (grass, leaf) + jalaukā (leech, worm; here a leaf-worm or aphid). The image is borrowed from older Upaniṣadic sources (cf. Bṛhad-āraṇyaka 4.4.3). Bhāgavata uses it for one specific purpose: to show that transmigration is leaf-to-leaf, not leaf-to-sky-to-leaf.
  • dehī — the living entity
    Same dehī as in verse 39. The verse continues developing the same subject: it is dehī who walks, dehī who transmigrates, dehī who is the constant through changes. Bhakti is dehī's only available resistance to the chain.
  • karma-gatim — the reactions of fruitive activities
    Karma + gati (motion, destination). Together: 'the karma-trajectory.' The dehī undergoes karma-gati — he is moved by karma. The grammar refuses agency: he goes through karma's motion, not the other way around.
Just as a person traveling on the road rests one foot on the ground and then lifts the other, or as a worm on a vegetable transfers itself to one leaf and then gives up the previous one, the conditioned soul takes shelter of another body and then gives up the one he had before.
Purport

This is the process of the soul’s transmigration from one body to another. At the time of death, according to his mental condition, the living being is carried by the subtle body, consisting of mind, intelligence and ego, to another gross body. When higher authorities have decided what kind of gross body the living entity will have, he is forced to enter such a body, and thus he automatically gives up his previous body. Dull-minded persons who do not have the intelligence to understand this process of transmigration take for granted that when the gross body is finished, one’s life is finished forever. Such persons have no brains with which to understand the process of transmigration. At the present moment there is great opposition to the Hare Kṛṣṇa movement, which is being called a “brainwashing” movement. But actually the so-called scientists, philosophers and other leaders in the Western countries have no brains at all. The Hare Kṛṣṇa movement is trying to elevate such foolish persons by enlightening their intelligence so that they will take advantage of the human body. Unfortunately, because of gross ignorance, they regard the Hare Kṛṣṇa movement as a brainwashing movement. They do not know that without God consciousness one is forced to continue transmigrating from one body to another. Because of their devilish brains, they will next be forced to accept an abominable life and practically never be able to liberate themselves from the conditional life of material existence. How this transmigration of the soul takes place is very clearly explained in this verse.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Just as a person traveling on the road rests one foot on the ground and then lifts the other, or as a worm on a vegetable transfers itself to one leaf and then gives up the previous one, the conditioned soul takes shelter of another body and then gives up the one he had before.
Commentary

In this verse Vasudeva gives examples to show how one gives up the present body after attaining the new body ( in subtle form, via karma). A man puts his forward foot on the ground firmly and then withdraws his back leg from the ground and puts it forward, not putting both feet forward at the same time. However in this example the feet are fixing themselves and withdrawing themselves from the same ground. As this may be unsatisfying to the mind, he gives another example. A caterpillar, after taking support of one blade of grass in front of him, gives up the previous blade of grass.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Dehī means the possessor of the body. Instead of jalauka the variation jalūka is sometimes seen.

Personal Guidance
Death is just the soul stepping from one body to the next — like a foot lifting or a worm crossing leaves; only Kṛṣṇa-consciousness ends the walk.
Practical Applications
  • Notice when you flinch at the thought of death today, and remind yourself the body is the leaf, not the soul crossing it.
  • Speak to one person caught in bodily fear — illness, aging, loss — and gently turn the conversation toward the eternal soul and shelter in Kṛṣṇa.
  • Refuse to spend this human birth like the 'brainless' — chant Hare Kṛṣṇa attentively for a fixed number of rounds today, treating sādhana as the one act that ends transmigration.
Reflection Questions
  • Like the worm that will not release one leaf until its foot is firmly on the next, which body-based identity (role, relationship, possession) am I refusing to release because I have not yet placed my weight on Kṛṣṇa?
  • If 'higher authorities' decide my next body based on my mental condition at death, what is the actual content of my mind in its unguarded moments today — and what trajectory is it quietly setting?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • You have already 'died' as the child in your old school photos — that body and its friendships are gone, and you are still here. Sit with one such photo for two minutes tonight and notice who is doing the noticing; this is the same continuity the verse points to past the final transition.
  • Your phone trains your mental condition every hour. The verse says the mind at the moment of death decides the next body — extrapolate: the mind at the moment of every sleep decides tomorrow's mood, focus, and desires. For one week, put down the phone 30 minutes before sleep and chant or read instead. Watch what shifts.
  • Peer pressure works because you secretly believe this body and this friend group are you. Pick one situation this week — a party, a group chat, a trend — where you have been performing to belong, and skip it deliberately. Notice the fear that comes up; that fear is the worm's grip on the old leaf.
householder
  • The mortgage, the school admissions, the promotion — these are leaves the worm is currently standing on. Name out loud, with your spouse this week, which leaf you are most terrified to leave. Then together decide one concrete way to loosen that grip — a financial cushion, a will, a frank talk with the kids — so fear is not steering the household.
  • Your children are watching what mental condition you cultivate at the end of each day. If you collapse into news, scrolling, or grievance every night, you are training their saṁskāras as much as your own. Institute one family practice — a tulasī offering, a verse read aloud, ārati — that becomes the last input of the day for everyone in the house.
  • When a parent or in-law is aging or dying, distinguish real compassion from material compassion. Material compassion arranges comfort for the body; real compassion arranges Kṛṣṇa's name in the room. Do both, but do not skip the second because the first is socially easier.
senior
  • You are now closer to the foot-lift than the foot-plant. Spend 15 minutes this week writing down what mental condition you want to be in at the moment of death — concretely, what name on the lips, what image before the eyes — and then audit your daily routine against that target. Adjust one thing.
  • Legacy understood materially is another leaf — the firm, the house, the reputation. Legacy understood as a Vaiṣṇava is what you transmit of Kṛṣṇa to grandchildren, students, and neighbors. Identify one younger person who has no one giving them this verse, and begin meeting with them monthly. Do not wait for them to ask.
  • Loosen one possession this month — not symbolically, actually. Give away a piece of property, a collection, an heirloom, while you can still witness its right placement. Each release is rehearsal for the final release the verse describes, and each one weakens the worm's reflex to clutch the leaf it is about to leave.
Common Pitfalls
  • Treating transmigration as an abstract metaphysical doctrine to debate or defend, rather than as the immediate logic that should reshape today's choices about how the mind is being trained.
  • Using the 'soul is eternal' teaching as emotional anesthesia — bypassing real grief, responsibility, or the urgency of sādhana, instead of letting the verse sharpen vairāgya toward this body and seriousness toward the next.
  • Assuming that intellectual assent to transmigration is the same as being prepared for it — forgetting Prabhupāda's point that the subtle body carries you according to your mental condition, not according to your stated philosophy.
Discussion Prompts
  • Prabhupāda calls modern scientists and philosophers 'brainless' for denying transmigration. Is that polemic still useful in your circle today, or does it close more doors than it opens? Where in your own life have you been intellectually polite about this verse's claim, and what has that politeness cost you?
  • The verse uses two images: a walker shifting weight foot to foot, and a worm crossing leaf to leaf. One feels dignified, the other slightly humiliating. Which image lands harder for you, and why? Share a moment in the last year when you were clearly the worm.
  • If 'higher authorities' decide the next body based on the mental condition at death, then sādhana is essentially rehearsal for one moment. Do you actually live as if that is true? Where would your daily schedule have to break for that conviction to be visible to someone watching you?
Role Play

Two students pair up. Student A is a hospice nurse who has just sat with a dying patient — a kind, secular man who told her, 'When the lights go out, they go out. That's it.' Student B is a Gauḍīya devotee friend she calls that night, shaken. Student A must voice the patient's view honestly and her own sympathy with it; Student B must respond using SB 10.1.40 — the foot-and-leaf images, the role of the subtle body, the mental condition at death — without lecturing, without quoting verse numbers, and without offering vague comfort. After 6 minutes, swap roles. Then debrief: where did the devotee slip into platitude? Where did the verse actually land?

Objections & Responses
  • There is zero empirical evidence for transmigration. Calling scientists 'brainless' for refusing to believe an unfalsifiable claim is not a response — it is a tantrum. Why should I take this verse seriously rather than as pre-modern speculation?
    Fair — the polemical edge in the purport is a feature of Prabhupāda's preaching style, not the argument itself. The argument is this: every one of us has already watched the body of a five-year-old, a fifteen-year-old, and a thirty-year-old 'die' inside this single lifetime, while the one who remembers them remains. The verse simply extends that observed continuity past one more transition. The empirical question — 'is there a witness behind these changing bodies?' — is one you can examine in your own experience right now. Śāstra describes the mechanism (subtle body, mental condition at death); your job is not to accept it on faith but to test the prior claim of a continuous self, on which the rest stands. Surrender to Kṛṣṇa is what gives this examination weight; without Him it stays an interesting hypothesis.
  • If 'higher authorities' decide my next body and I am 'forced' into it, this is just karmic determinism. Where is my agency? It sounds like I am being processed by a cosmic bureaucracy.
    The verse does describe a forced transfer — but the force is the momentum of your own ahaṅkāra and saṁskāras, not an external bureaucracy. The 'higher authorities' administer; they do not author. What they administer is the mental condition you yourself cultivated. That is precisely where agency lives — in this human body, right now, in what you feed the mind. The Gauḍīya answer is sharper still: bhakti to Kṛṣṇa cuts the karmic chain that the administrators would otherwise have to enforce. You are not negotiating with the bureaucracy; you are stepping out of its jurisdiction by taking shelter of Him personally. Sarva-dharmān parityajya is the exit, not a loophole.
Analogy

Just as a person walking rests one foot before lifting the other, or as a worm on a leaf transfers itself to the next leaf before relinquishing the first, the conditioned soul accepts another body before giving up the present one. The Bhāgavata's choice of image — the walker, the worm — refuses the romantic interval; transmigration is not a journey through space but the leaf-to-leaf grip of attachment.
