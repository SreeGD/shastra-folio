Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.1.55

श्रीशुक उवाच स्वसुर्वधान्निववृते कंसस्तद्वाक्यसारवित् । वसुदेवोऽपि तं प्रीत: प्रशस्य प्राविशद् गृहम् ॥ ५५ ॥

Important Words
  • svasuḥ vadhāt nivavṛte — stopped for the time being from the act of killing his sister
    Svasṛ (sister, gen.) + vadha (killing, abl.) + nivṛtta (turned back, past of ni-vṛt). The same root as nivṛtta-tarṣa (verse 4) — turning back. Kaṁsa turns back, however momentarily, from killing. Even an asura's nivṛtti is dharmically registered.
  • sāra-vit — knowing to be perfectly correct
    Sāra (essence, truth) + vit (knowing). Kaṁsa knows the essence of Vasudeva's argument. He is not stupid; he is khala. The Bhāgavata distinguishes: asuras are often jñāta-vit (one who knows the truth) and still anuṣṭhāna-virata (refraining from acting on it). Knowledge without obedience is the asuric signature.
  • prītaḥ — being satisfied
    Past participle of prī, 'to please.' Vasudeva is prīta — momentarily pleased. The Bhāgavata grants devotees the right to feel relief when relief is real, even temporary. Bhakti does not require constant existential gravity; it permits the small joys that life supplies.
  • praśasya — pacifying more
    Pra + śaṁs (to praise). Gerund. Vasudeva praises Kaṁsa further on the way out — locking in the bargain. The strategic verbalization of the same word that named Parīkṣit's request (śaṁsa, verse 2) — same root, opposite use: praise as bait.
Śrīla Śukadeva Gosvāmī continued: Kaṁsa agreed to the logical arguments of Vasudeva, and, having full faith in Vasudeva’s words, he refrained from killing his sister. Vasudeva, being pleased with Kaṁsa, pacified him further and entered his own house.
Purport

Although Kaṁsa was a sinful demon, he believed that Vasudeva would never deviate from his word. The character of a pure devotee like Vasudeva is such that even so great a demon as Kaṁsa firmly believed in his words and was satisfied. Yasyāsti bhaktir bhagavaty akiñcanā sarvair guṇais tatra samāsate surāḥ ( Bhāg. 5.18.12 ). All good attributes are present in a devotee, so much so that even Kaṁsa believed in Vasudeva’s words without a doubt.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Śrīla Śukadeva Gosvāmī continued: Kaṁsa agreed to the logical arguments of Vasudeva, and, having full faith in Vasudeva's words, he refrained from killing his sister. Vasudeva, being pleased with Kaṁsa, pacified him further and entered his own house.
Commentary

Kamsa could understand that Vasudeva did not speak a lie. Vasudeva praised Kamsa by saying that his good character would be spread through the universe.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Kaṁsa refrained from killing Devakī, a friend. The word suhṛd is an alternative to svasuḥ (sister). Kaṁsa thought that, by refraining from killing her, she would be friendly to him. Moreover it is suggested by this that on not killing her, he also refrained from killing many of her friends since it was possible that many would be killed if Kaṁsa had to fight with Devaka and others. Śrīdhara takes the reading svasuḥ. Vasudeva’s words were logical. Sāra means “strong.” This means his words were logical. Or his words had to be followed. Vasudeva was pleased with Kaṁsa, in so far as Kaṁsa refrained from killing Devakī. This shows his greatness. He praised Kaṁsa saying, “You are most merciful and intelligent.” He entered his house in a grand manner (pra), with music and fanfare to increase Kaṁsa’s trust.

Personal Guidance
A pure devotee's word carries such weight that even a demon like Kaṁsa trusts it — speak so truthfully that your word alone disarms hostility.
Practical Applications
  • Today, refuse to soften, exaggerate, or hedge a single promise — let your word match reality so completely that even an adversary could rest on it.
  • Identify one person you regard as 'difficult' or 'demonic' and speak to them with the same plain truthfulness you would offer a devotee.
  • Before leaving a tense conversation, like Vasudeva entering his house, pacify the other person with a sincere word rather than walking away in silent victory.
Reflection Questions
  • Whose trust have I quietly squandered by treating my word as negotiable, and what would it cost me today to speak with the unwavering integrity that even a Kaṁsa could rely on?
  • Where am I tempted to use clever argument (like Vasudeva's tad-vākya-sāra) to get my way, versus letting my words rest on the bedrock of devotional truthfulness that needs no manipulation?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Pick the one person in your friend group or class whom you most want to impress, and notice how your speech shifts around them — softer truths, edited opinions. This week, say one true thing to them you would otherwise have swallowed, framed with vinaya, not aggression. That is the Vasudeva move.
  • On social media this week, do not post a single thing you would not be willing to defend in a face-to-face conversation with someone who disagrees. Vasudeva's words worked on Kaṁsa because they were sāra — substance, not performance. Train your speech offline first.
  • Identify one 'Kaṁsa' in your life right now — a professor, coach, roommate, or family member who is volatile and powerful over you. Instead of avoiding or flattering them, prepare three honest, calm sentences you can use the next time they escalate. Practice them aloud. Daksya is preparation, not improvisation.
householder
  • In the next hard conversation with your spouse about money, kids, or in-laws, do what Vasudeva did: drop the rhetorical wins, speak only what you actually believe, and trust that a settled tone will do more than a clever argument. Track whether the conversation lands differently.
  • Your children watch whether your word holds when it costs you something. This week, keep one promise to them that has become inconvenient — a small one — without renegotiating it. That single act builds the kind of credibility Kaṁsa recognized in Vasudeva.
  • At work, identify one situation where you have been managing a difficult stakeholder through flattery or avoidance. Replace it with one calm, truthful sentence delivered with anahankarah — no ego, no edge. Offer the result to Krishna and notice how much less the outcome owns you.
senior
  • Your grown children and grandchildren will remember the steadiness of your speech long after they forget your advice. In every family interaction this month, prioritize samata over being right. The legacy is the bhāva, not the verdict.
  • Vasudeva, even in mortal danger to his wife, did not lose his sthitaprajna. Use the approach of mortality the same way: each time fear of death, illness, or loss arises, meet it with one slow breath and one truthful word — to yourself or to Krishna. This is the rehearsal for the final passage.
  • Mentor someone younger this season not by giving them answers but by showing them what unflustered speech under pressure looks like. Tell them the story of one time you spoke truthfully when it cost you, and one time you did not — and what you learned. Vinaya in an elder is more instructive than any lecture.
Common Pitfalls
  • Mistaking Vasudeva's diplomatic skill (dakṣya) for mere strategic cunning — and concluding that a devotee may lie or manipulate so long as the cause is good. The verse points the opposite way: Kaṁsa believed him precisely because Vasudeva never deviated from his word.
  • Reading 'pacifying Kaṁsa' as appeasement of evil or moral compromise, and using this to justify flattering toxic people in our own lives. Vasudeva's vinaya was rooted in protecting Devakī and serving Kṛṣṇa's plan, not in cowardice or people-pleasing.
  • Assuming that because 'all good qualities reside in a devotee,' those qualities will appear automatically without sādhana — and neglecting the daily cultivation of truthfulness, humility, and equanimity that made Vasudeva's character trustworthy in the first place.
Discussion Prompts
  • Vasudeva makes a binding promise to a known murderer to save his wife's life. Share with your partner: was this a compromise of dharma, or its highest expression? Where is the line between skillful speech (daksya) and the cowardice of telling a powerful person what they want to hear?
  • Kaṁsa, a demon, trusted Vasudeva absolutely because of Vasudeva's character. Discuss: name one person in your life whose word you would accept without verification, and one person whose word you would not. What specifically built or destroyed that trust — and what does that say about your own credibility to others?
  • Prabhupāda says all good qualities (sarvair guṇaiḥ) automatically reside in a devotee. Push back on this honestly in your group: have you actually seen this in devotees you know? Where does the claim seem to break down, and is the failure in the principle or in the practitioner?
Role Play

Two students play this scene. Student A is a senior employee who has just discovered that a volatile, vindictive boss (Student B) is about to fire a junior colleague over a misunderstanding — a firing that would destroy the colleague's visa status and family. Student A has a rare moment alone with the boss. They must, like Vasudeva, use only truthful words (no lies, no flattery) but arranged with such samata and vinaya that the boss actually hears them and stands down. Student B is instructed to start hostile and only soften if Student A speaks from genuine equanimity, not fear. Run it twice; swap roles. Debrief: where did fear leak into the speech? Where did real sthitaprajna show up?

Objections & Responses
  • Vasudeva basically negotiated with a tyrant and handed over his future children to be killed. This isn't wisdom — it's appeasement, the same logic that lets abusers keep abusing. A modern reader would call this enabling, not bhakti.
    The verse is not prescribing appeasement as a general policy; it is showing what a surrendered devotee does when force is impossible and Krishna's plan is not yet visible. Vasudeva does not flatter Kaṁsa, does not lie, does not pretend Kaṁsa is good — he buys time with truthful argument while remaining inwardly fixed on the Lord. The very children he 'hands over' become the instruments through which Kaṁsa is destroyed and Krishna appears. Real ishvara-pranidhana is not passivity; it is acting rightly in this moment while trusting the larger arc. Confusing this with enabling collapses the difference between strategic non-violence under constraint and moral cowardice.
  • The purport claims that 'all good attributes' are present in a devotee. That's empirically false — we all know devotees who lie, manipulate, or behave badly. Isn't this just sectarian self-congratulation dressed up as scripture?
    The verse Prabhupāda cites (Bhāg. 5.18.12) speaks of one who has akiñcanā bhakti — undivided, possessionless devotion to Bhagavān. That is a precise spiritual condition, not a club membership. Someone who wears the dress, chants the mantras, and still lies has not yet reached that state; the verse is not falsified, the diagnosis is. The Gauḍīya teachers are quite frank that most of us are sādhakas, not siddhas. The standard the verse sets is not a flattering description of us — it is a mirror that shows us how far our bhakti still has to mature. Treating it as tribal boasting misses that it is actually the most uncomfortable verse in the room.
Analogy

Kaṁsa, persuaded by Vasudeva's logic, sheathes the sword and lets Devakī go — for now. Vasudeva, pleased by even this temporary mercy, pacifies him further and enters his own house. The verse closes the chariot-scene with a tender domestic image: a husband walks his wife home. The Bhāgavata does not narrate Devakī's tears or Vasudeva's exhale; it lets the simple gṛham — 'house' — carry the weight of what was almost not theirs.
