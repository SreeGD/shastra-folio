Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.1.38

मृत्युर्जन्मवतां वीर देहेन सह जायते । अद्य वाब्दशतान्ते वा मृत्युर्वै प्राणिनां ध्रुव: ॥ ३८ ॥

Important Words
  • mṛtyuḥ — death
    From mṛ, 'to die.' The Bhāgavata uses mṛtyu as a personal force — Kṛṣṇa Himself is mṛtyu in BG 10.34 (mṛtyuḥ sarva-haraś cāham). Vasudeva names the inevitable not as misfortune but as Bhagavān's own form.
  • janma-vatām — of the living entities who have taken birth
    Janman (birth) + -vat (possessing). For the janma-possessing, mṛtyu is paired. The compound makes the logical link Vasudeva is exploiting: avoid birth, and you avoid death — but once birth is taken, no avoidance is possible.
  • dehena saha — along with the body
    Dehena (instrumental of deha, body) + saha (with). Death rides into the world simultaneously with the body. The verse rules out the asuric fantasy that the body is durable and death is an external enemy.
  • dhruvaḥ — is assured
    Dhruva — fixed, certain. The same word that names Dhruva Mahārāja, who attained the dhruva-loka by realizing that nothing in this world is dhruva. The verse closes with the one thing that is dhruva: mṛtyu. Everything else moves; death stands still.
O great hero, one who takes birth is sure to die, for death is born with the body. One may die today or after hundreds of years, but death is sure for every living entity.
Purport

Vasudeva wanted to impress upon Kaṁsa that although Kaṁsa feared dying and therefore wanted to kill even a woman, he could not avoid death. Death is sure. Why then should Kaṁsa do something that would be detrimental to his reputation and that of his family? As confirmed in Bhagavad-gītā (2.27) : jātasya hi dhruvo mṛtyur dhruvaṁ janma mṛtasya ca tasmād aparihārye ’rthe na tvaṁ śocitum arhasi “For one who has taken his birth, death is certain; and for one who is dead, birth is certain. Therefore, in the unavoidable discharge of your duty, you should not lament.” One should not fear death. Rather, one should prepare oneself for the next birth. One should utilize one’s time in this human form to end the process of birth and death. It is not that to save oneself from death one should entangle oneself in sinful activities. This is not good.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
O great hero, one who takes birth is sure to die, for death is born with the body. One may die today or after hundreds of years, but death is sure for every living entity.
Commentary

After saying this, with no change of heart in Kamsa, Vasudeva tried to advise him using logic. "O king! If you are about to commit murder because of fear of death, listen to me. Death is unavoidable." With this intention, he then speaks this verse. Factually there is no birth or death for the soul. Only those who take birth ( janmavatam) by accepting a material body have death. "Sa" here means it is well known. "Ha" indicates that the fact is definite. When will death take place? Today or a hundred years later. Two "vas’ are used to indicate that being unable to determine at all the time of death, it could happen anytime between now and a hundred years. He presents proof by saying "mrtyur vai praninam dhruvah." This recalls the verse in Gita: jatasya hi dhruvam mrtyur dhruvam janma mrtasya ca... "O brave one! A courageous warrior like you should not be afraid of death."

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

He uses logic in six verses to convince Kaṁsa not to kill Devakī. First he explains that death is unavoidable. It is said jātasya hi dhurvo mṛtyur dhruvaṁ janma mṛtasya ca: for one who is born death is certain and for one dies, birth is certain. (BG 2.27) He addresses him as a hero to encourage him but indirectly indicates that he is not a hero at all. Or he indicates that Kaṁsa is skillful in battle but not in discerning the truth. How is death born with the body? The effect (death) should be included in the cause. The word vā is repeated to show that dying today or after a hundred years are both prominent possibilities. The word vai indicates that this is a well known fact.

Personal Guidance
Death is born with the body — sure for all; do not commit sin to flee it, but use this human breath to end birth and death by surrendering to Kṛṣṇa.
Practical Applications
  • Refuse any compromise today — a lie, a cruelty, a shortcut — that you would justify by 'self-preservation,' since no sin can buy you one extra breath.
  • Spend ten unhurried minutes contemplating that the body you are protecting was already dying the day it was born, and let that loosen one specific fear gripping you now.
  • Use one concrete slot of today — a chant, a reading, a service — explicitly as preparation for the next birth rather than the comfort of this one.
Reflection Questions
  • Vasudeva tells Kaṁsa that death is born with the body — where in my own life am I burning energy trying to outrun a death that was sewn into me at birth, instead of using that same energy to prepare for what comes after?
  • If my span could end today or in a hundred years, what is the one sinful entanglement I am still tolerating because I am quietly betting on the 'hundred years'?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • When you catch yourself curating your social media to 'preserve' an image that will not survive your next semester, let alone your next body, post less and chant more — even ten minutes of japa before you open the app.
  • Notice the moment in friend-group dynamics when you are tempted to lie, exclude someone, or cheat on an assignment to protect your standing. Name it as Kaṁsa-logic: harming another to extend a self that is already on a clock. Refuse it once this week and tell a sādhu-saṅga friend you did.
  • Pick one elder in your family and have a real conversation about death — theirs, yours, the family's. Not morbid, just honest. This is the dhīra training you will not get from any course.
householder
  • Sit with your spouse this weekend and write down which family decisions — career moves, schools, real estate, health spending — are quietly driven by the assumption that you can outrun death or aging. Re-decide one of them on the basis of 'today or after a hundred years, it is sure.'
  • Refuse the household compromise that crosses into pāpa for security: the inflated invoice, the tax dodge, the harsh word to a subordinate to protect your position. Teach your children, by visible example, that the family does not buy extra years of comfort with sinful reactions.
  • Build a daily family practice — even fifteen minutes of kīrtana, reading, and prasāda — that explicitly frames this human form as the window to end the cycle. Your children should know, from your schedule, that this is the actual point of the household.
senior
  • Each morning, name one attachment — a possession, a role, a grievance, an identity — that you will loosen this week, because death is born with the body and you do not want to be carrying it across the threshold.
  • Identify two younger people — a grandchild, a junior at work, a neighbor — and begin a deliberate mentoring relationship oriented not to worldly success but to the soul. Offer them what Vasudeva offered Kaṁsa: a sober reminder, with affection, that this body has an expiry.
  • Audit your medical, financial, and end-of-life decisions for any Kaṁsa-residue — choices made out of fear that quietly entangle you or your family in sinful reactions. Replace them with arrangements rooted in abhaya: chanting, hearing, association of devotees, and trust in Kṛṣṇa for the next birth.
Common Pitfalls
  • Turning the certainty of death into morbid fatalism or passivity ('why bother?') instead of the urgency Prabhupāda points to — using this human form to end the cycle of birth and death.
  • Using 'death is sure for everyone' as cold consolation when comforting the bereaved, rather than as a personal call to vairāgya and sādhana for oneself.
  • Concluding, like Kaṁsa, that since death is inevitable one may as well commit sinful acts to postpone or control it — the very inversion the verse warns against.
Discussion Prompts
  • Vasudeva tells Kaṁsa that death is born with the body — yet Kaṁsa is still willing to commit murder to postpone his own. Where in your own life are you 'killing' something or someone (a relationship, a reputation, a truth) just to delay an outcome that is in fact unavoidable? Push back on each other — do not let your partner give a tidy answer.
  • Prabhupāda says, 'It is not that to save oneself from death one should entangle oneself in sinful activities.' Discuss a concrete modern situation — medical, financial, professional — where people routinely take on pāpa to extend the body. Is there ever a Vaiṣṇava case for accepting such reactions? Disagree openly.
  • If death is dhruvaḥ — certain — then what actually changes in your daily schedule starting tomorrow? Each person name one thing they would stop doing and one thing they would start. Then challenge each other: is this real preparation for the next birth, or just spiritualized self-improvement?
Role Play

Two students pair up. Student A plays a 55-year-old uncle who has just received a serious cancer diagnosis and is frantically pursuing every experimental treatment, willing to drain the family savings and even bend a few rules to get priority access. Student B plays the niece/nephew who has been studying the Bhāgavatam and wants to share Vasudeva's argument to Kaṁsa — that death comes with the body, today or after a hundred years, and that sinful entanglement to postpone it is not good. Student B must speak as a fellow practitioner, not lecture; must honor the uncle's fear (abhaya is offered, not demanded); and must offer a concrete next step beyond 'accept death.' After 7 minutes, switch roles. Then debrief: where did the conversation become preachy? Where did real compassion (for the soul) get confused with material compassion (for the body)?

Objections & Responses
  • This sounds like fatalism dressed up in Sanskrit. 'Death is sure anyway, so do not fight it' — that logic would shut down medicine, public health, and every protective instinct a parent has. Vasudeva himself is arguing this to save his wife's life, which is a contradiction.
    Vasudeva is not arguing 'do not fight death.' He is arguing 'do not commit pāpa in order to postpone death.' Those are different. A Vaiṣṇava accepts medicine, takes reasonable care of the body as Kṛṣṇa's instrument, and protects dependents — this is rakṣā, protective responsibility. What he refuses is the Kaṁsa-logic: harming others, lying, exploiting, hoarding, in the desperate hope of buying a few more years. The contradiction you sense is actually the Gītā's whole pivot — real compassion serves the soul's trajectory across births, not just the body's comfort in this one. Vasudeva is in fact protecting Devakī through truth and reasoning, not through violence.
  • The 'prepare for the next birth' framing assumes reincarnation, which I have no evidence for. Without that premise the whole verse collapses into a generic memento mori — useful, but nothing distinctively Vaiṣṇava.
    Fair — the verse does rest on janma, mṛtyu, punar-janma as a real structure, not a metaphor. A Gauḍīya teacher would not try to prove rebirth in a debate; we would invite you to test the working hypothesis. Live for one month as if this body were a temporary posting and the next destination depended on today's saṁskāras — chant, hear, serve, and watch what happens to your fear of death. The distinctively Vaiṣṇava element is not just 'you will die' but 'Kṛṣṇa is personally waiting, and bhakti is the one sādhana that actually transfers across the gap.' That is a claim you can examine experientially, not just intellectually.
Analogy

Vasudeva pivots from dharma-appeal to existential argument: 'Great hero, one who is born must die. Death is born with the body.' The verse is a knife the devotee turns inward: even murdering your sister will not buy you a single day. Mortality is built into birth like the seed in the fruit. Asuric striving to outrun death is exactly the striving the Bhāgavata diagnoses as bondage.
