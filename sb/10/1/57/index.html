Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.1.57

Vasudeva was very much disturbed by fear of becoming a liar by breaking his promise. Thus with great pain he delivered his first-born son, named Kīrtimān, into the hands of Kaṁsa.
Purport

In the Vedic system, as soon as a child is born, especially a male child, the father calls for learned brāhmaṇas, and according to the description of the child’s horoscope the child is immediately given a name. This ceremony is called nāma-karaṇa. There are ten different saṁskāras, or reformatory methods, adopted in the system of varṇāśrama-dharma, and the name-giving ceremony is one of them. Although Vasudeva’s first son was to be delivered into the hands of Kaṁsa, the nāma-karaṇa ceremony was performed, and thus the child was named Kīrtimān. Such names are given immediately after birth.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Vasudeva was very much disturbed by fear of becoming a liar by breaking his promise. Thus with great pain he delivered his first-born son, named Kīrtimān, into the hands of Kaṁsa.
Commentary

It was the custom to name the child on the day he was born. Thus on that day the first child was named Kirtiman.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Six sons of Marīci in their third birth became the first six sons of Vasudeva. As sons of Marīci they had two names each such as Smara and Kīrtimān as Marīci’s first son. This is Śrīdhara Svāmī’s explanation (on the assumption that they would to not be given names by Vasudeva since they were immediately killed). Another explanation is that when they were brought back to Devakī from Sutala they were given the names starting with Kīrtimān and sent to Vaikuṇṭha by Kṛṣṇa. This should be the understanding of their names mentioned in SB 9.24.54.

Personal Guidance
Keep your word even when it costs you everything dear — Vasudeva's anguished honesty shows truthfulness as the ground on which Kṛṣṇa's plan unfolds.
Practical Applications
  • Honor the small promise you made this week that no one would notice you breaking, even if keeping it costs you something.
  • Notice the moment you are tempted to soften a hard truth into a convenient lie, and speak the truth instead.
  • Perform today's painful duty fully — like Vasudeva's nāma-karaṇa for Kīrtimān — refusing to skip the proper form just because the outcome looks bleak.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my own life am I, like Vasudeva, holding to a promise whose literal fulfillment seems to harm what I most love — and what does keeping my word in the face of that pain ask of me today?
  • When I act under crushing duress, do I still perform the small dharmic acts of dignity (the nāma-karaṇa moments) — or do I let the weight of the situation become my excuse to skip the saṁskāras that honor the soul I am dealing with?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • You made a casual commitment — to a group project, a friend's plan, a club role — and now keeping it is genuinely costing you sleep, grades, or peace. Before you ghost or renegotiate, sit for ten minutes with Vasudeva's kṛcchreṇa: feel the pain of keeping the word, then decide. Often the pain is the price of becoming someone whose word means something.
  • Watch for the moment in a group chat or social feed when you are about to soften, hide, or recast a promise you made — 'I never really said I'd…' Catch the lie in your mouth before it leaves. Anṛta — falsehood — is what Vasudeva was 'ativihvalaḥ' about avoiding; train the same allergy in yourself now, while the stakes are still small.
  • Pick one daily saṁskāra-sized act — chanting a fixed number of names, a pre-meal offering, a morning praṇāma to your parents — and do it on the days you least feel like it: exam morning, after a fight, hungover from a bad night. Vasudeva did the nāma-karaṇa on the worst day of his life. That is when the practice is actually being built.
householder
  • Identify one promise you made to your spouse, child, or aging parent that has quietly become inconvenient — a financial commitment, a time commitment, a boundary around work. Do not renegotiate it this week. Sit in the kṛcchreṇa, the pain of keeping it, and notice what in you wants to slip out. The household runs on satya more than on income.
  • When a family decision goes badly — a job loss, a child's diagnosis, a parent's decline — resist the urge to abandon your family rituals 'until things settle.' That is exactly when the lamp gets lit, the prasāda gets offered, the festival gets observed. Vasudeva named his doomed son with full ceremony; do not let your household's saṁskāras become fair-weather practices.
  • Practice rakṣā as Vasudeva did — protective responsibility that is willing to look weak in the short term to preserve what matters in the long term. This week, refuse one heroic, satisfying confrontation (with a boss, an in-law, a school administrator) in favor of the slower, less photogenic protection of your family's actual long-term good.
senior
  • You have surrendered children, careers, parents, perhaps a spouse — willingly or otherwise — into hands you did not trust. Sit with Vasudeva today, not as a story but as a peer. What have you handed over with kṛcchreṇa? Where is the unhealed ativihvalatā still lodged in your body? Offer it, by name, to Kṛṣṇa in your evening sit.
  • Mentor one younger person this month specifically on satya — keeping the spoken word — by telling them, without moralizing, one story of a promise you kept at real cost and one you broke that you still feel. Your generation's lived authority on this is what they cannot get from a podcast.
  • Begin loosening a saṁskāra-rich legacy on purpose: teach a grandchild or younger relative one observance you have done for decades — the way you offer the lamp, the verses you chant, the naming or birthday rite — so it outlives you. Like Vasudeva, your job at this stage is not to clutch the child but to send the practice forward in good order.
Common Pitfalls
  • Romanticizing Vasudeva's anguish as mere sentimentality, missing that his pain is born of an unbreakable commitment to satya (truthfulness) — not of attachment to the child's body.
  • Concluding that 'protecting one's word' justifies handing the innocent over to harm in any modern situation; Vasudeva's act is set within Kṛṣṇa's specific arrangement, not a general license to abandon raksha (protective responsibility).
  • Treating outward saṁskāras like the nāma-karaṇa as optional formalities under stress, when in fact they are precisely the disciplines that keep us anchored in varṇāśrama-dharma when emotion would have us shortcut them.
Discussion Prompts
  • Vasudeva keeps a promise made to a demon — handing over his own newborn. Share with your partner: when has 'keeping your word' actually cost someone you love? Was the promise still worth keeping, or were you hiding behind 'integrity' to avoid a harder choice?
  • The verse says Vasudeva was 'ativihvalaḥ' — overwhelmed — yet he still acted. Disagree productively in your group: is it more spiritually mature to act through grief without numbing it, or to first cultivate the samatā that wouldn't feel grief so acutely? Where does each view break down?
  • Even though the child Kīrtimān was about to be killed, Vasudeva performed the nāma-karaṇa saṁskāra. Discuss: in your own life, what saṁskāras (formal observances — meals offered, prayers before sleep, festivals) have you dropped because the situation 'didn't seem to warrant them'? What did that quiet skipping cost you?
Role Play

Two students. Student A is a hospital chaplain visiting a Vaiṣṇava family whose newborn has just been diagnosed with a terminal condition; doctors say weeks at most. The grandfather wants to cancel the nāma-karaṇa and 'not get attached.' Student B plays the grandfather. Student A must, in five minutes, draw on Vasudeva's example at SB 10.1.57 to argue why the saṁskāra should still be performed — fully, with brāhmaṇas, with the horoscope, with the name — precisely because the child will not live long. Then swap roles. Debrief: where did 'spiritual bypass' show up disguised as detachment?

Objections & Responses
  • Vasudeva is morally a coward. A real father would have died fighting Kaṁsa rather than hand over his child. Dressing this up as 'keeping a promise' is rationalization — the verse is celebrating cowardice.
    Read carefully — the verse does not celebrate the act, it describes Vasudeva's kṛcchreṇa, his agony, and calls him ativihvalaḥ, overwhelmed. He is not poised; he is shattered. What is being honored is satya-pālana, fidelity to the spoken word, which in the Vedic frame is not a private virtue but the load-bearing wall of dharmic society. Vasudeva also knows what a fighting reaction would cost — Devakī, the unborn children, the wider kingdom. He is not refusing to fight from fear; he is refusing the satisfaction of one heroic gesture in favor of the longer, harder protection (rakṣā) that requires him to stay alive and stay near Devakī. Kṛṣṇa Himself later vindicates this restraint by appearing as the eighth child. The skeptic mistakes raw force for courage; Vasudeva shows that vinaya and dākṣya — humility and skill in impossible circumstances — are themselves a form of valor.
  • Performing the name-giving ceremony for a child you're about to surrender to be killed is grotesque — a piece of religious theater that just deepens everyone's pain. Surely real spirituality would skip the ritual and just grieve.
    This is the modern instinct — that ritual is decoration we add when life is going well, and that 'authentic' suffering means stripping it away. The Gauḍīya line teaches the opposite. Saṁskāras are not performances for the comfortable; they are precisely the scaffolding that holds a person upright when life has become unbearable. By naming Kīrtimān, Vasudeva refuses to let Kaṁsa reduce his son to a thing-to-be-disposed-of. The child enters and leaves this body as a named soul, a person, with his birth marked by brāhmaṇas and Vedic mantra. That is not theater — that is the assertion that the soul is real and the relationship is real, even when the body's tenure is hours. Try this yourself: the next time something in your life is collapsing, do not skip your sandhyā, your offering of food, your rounds. That is when they were designed for.
