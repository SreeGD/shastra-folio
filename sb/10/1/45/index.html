Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.1.45

एषा तवानुजा बाला कृपणा पुत्रिकोपमा । हन्तुं नार्हसि कल्याणीमिमां त्वं दीनवत्सल: ॥ ४५ ॥

Important Words
  • anujā — younger sister
    Anu (after) + ja (born), 'born after.' The relational term — she came after you, you ought to protect her. Sister-protection is one of the oldest brother-duties in śāstra. Vasudeva names the duty Kaṁsa is breaking by naming the relation.
  • kṛpaṇā — completely dependent on you
    Kṛpaṇa = poor, helpless. Devakī, in her wedding chariot at her brother's hands, is structurally dependent. The verse uses the word to summon kṛpā (compassion) from Kaṁsa — Sanskrit's etymological link: the kṛpaṇa deserves the kṛpā-vṛtti.
  • putrikā-upamā — exactly like your own daughter
    Putrikā (daughter) + upamā (similar to). A younger sister, in the dharmic logic, occupies the position of a daughter to her elder brother — to be protected, given in marriage, defended. Vasudeva calls Kaṁsa into the father-role to dislodge him from the killer-role.
  • dīna-vatsalaḥ — very compassionate to the poor and innocent
    Dīna (poor, helpless) + vatsala (affectionate). The epithet — usually applied to Bhagavān — is now applied (hopefully? ironically?) to Kaṁsa. Vasudeva clothes Kaṁsa in the Lord's own epithet, asking him to wear it. The next verse will show whether the clothing fits.
As your younger sister, this poor girl Devakī is like your own daughter and deserves to be affectionately maintained. You are merciful, and therefore you should not kill her. Indeed, she deserves your affection.
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
As your younger sister, this poor girl Devakī is like your own daughter and deserves to be affectionately maintained. You are merciful, and therefore you should not kill her. Indeed, she deserves your affection.
Commentary

Vasudeva again began praising Kamsa, according to the saying "By praise the ferocious becomes tamed." She is deserving your mercy because she is just like your daughter. The sentence can also mean " she is petrified like a doll out of fear." You should not kill her because you are compassionate to the suffering (dina vatsala). The real meaning of dina vatsala however is " one who accepts even a calf from the most poor person as a tax."

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

For the most cruel, one should use the type of sāma known as praise. This newly married woman is miserable (kṛpaṇā), crying in fear or she is low by her nature. She is like your daughter, worthy of your affection. Or she is like a doll, unconscious, not knowing good from bad. She is innocent, without fault (kalyaṇīm). The different words used to describe Devakī progress to the most important--kalyaṇīm. Dīnavalsalaḥ (affectionate to the miserable) has a hidden meaning. You are the type of person who gives (lā) an almost-dead calf (dīna-vatsa) to brāhmaṇas, or who takes a calf from a destitute person. You should not kill her. Of course people like you would do this, but it is to be condemned.

Personal Guidance
True dayā means protecting the helpless soul placed in your trust — Vasudeva appeals to Kaṁsa's own dharma as elder brother and protector.
Practical Applications
  • Identify one person whose welfare has been entrusted to you today, and act decisively to shield them rather than rationalize harm.
  • Notice when fear or self-interest tempts you to harden against someone weaker, and consciously invoke dīna-vatsalatā — tenderness toward the vulnerable.
  • Speak to a younger sibling, child, or junior in your circle as a protector would, asking what burden you can lift from them this week.
Reflection Questions
  • When someone close to me is in danger or distress, do I appeal to the aggressor's better nature as Vasudeva did with Kaṁsa — or do I retreat into helplessness because the situation feels too volatile to engage?
  • Whose 'younger sister' have I forgotten to protect — what vulnerable person in my orbit have I stopped advocating for because defending them feels socially or personally costly?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • When a friend is about to be cancelled in your group chat or piled on at school, be the Vasudeva in the room — don't lecture the group on ethics, name out loud the kindness you have actually seen the angry person show before, and make it harder for them to act against that self-image.
  • Notice the difference between defending someone because it will make you look good on social media (material compassion, performance) and quietly intervening in a DM where no one will ever see (real daya). Do one of the second kind this week.
  • When you are the angry one — about a roommate, a parent, an ex — pause before sending the message and ask: am I about to harm someone Kṛṣṇa has placed under my protection right now? Vasudeva's plea works on Kaṁsa for one moment; let someone's plea, real or imagined, work on you.
householder
  • In family decisions where a vulnerable person is in the room — an aging parent, a struggling sibling, a child being discussed as a 'problem' — speak the way Vasudeva speaks: name the relational bonds and the better nature of the harshest voice in the room, before arguments and logistics take over.
  • Distinguish, this week, one act of real daya toward your spouse or children (serving their soul — patience, honest correction, time given without phone) from one act of material indulgence dressed as love (buying-them-off, avoiding hard conversations to keep the peace). Do more of the first.
  • When you must negotiate with someone who holds power over your family — a boss, a landlord, a school administrator, a difficult in-law — remember that Vasudeva did not threaten and did not grovel. He appealed to the person's own self-respect. Try this once instead of the usual tactics, and watch what shifts.
senior
  • You have spent a lifetime accumulating the moral authority Vasudeva is using here — the right, earned by years of restraint, to appeal to a younger person's better nature without sounding preachy. Use it deliberately on one person this month: a grandchild, a junior colleague, an estranged relative. Don't waste it on advice; spend it on naming a virtue you have seen in them.
  • As bodily strength fades, the kṣatriya-temptation to control through force fades with it — and the Vasudeva-path opens: protecting through speech, prayer, and the staying of another's hand. Identify one situation in your family where your only remaining power is this kind, and use it consciously rather than mourning the other kind.
  • Mortality sharpens the question of what compassion is really for. Spend less of your remaining energy on smoothing others' bodily discomforts (material compassion) and more on what their soul will need when you are gone — a spiritual book given, a verse taught to a grandchild, a wrong forgiven aloud. That is the daya Vasudeva models, scaled to your stage.
Common Pitfalls
  • Mistaking Vasudeva's gentle, kinship-based appeal for mere flattery or appeasement — and so dismissing the verse as weak diplomacy rather than recognizing it as skillful, dharmic protection of the innocent under impossible pressure.
  • Reading dīna-vatsala (mercy to the fallen) as a sentimental quality belonging only to saints, instead of a daya we are obligated to cultivate and exercise toward those weaker than us in our own households today.
  • Confusing material compassion (saving Devakī's body) with the verse's deeper teaching — and so applying it only as a case for non-violence in general, while missing that real protection of others requires courage, tact, and willingness to address the aggressor directly.
Discussion Prompts
  • Vasudeva appeals to Kaṁsa's affection for his sister rather than to dharma or fear of consequence. Was this a wise strategic move, or did it reveal a kṣatriya husband bargaining with sentiment when stronger ground was available? Defend the side you find less obvious.
  • Vasudeva calls Kaṁsa dīna-vatsalaḥ — 'merciful to the fallen' — when Kaṁsa is at that very moment about to murder a defenseless woman. Is this flattery, hopeful naming, or genuine recognition of a buried quality? Can you think of a time you named a virtue in someone precisely to call it forth — and did it work?
  • Real daya serves the soul; material compassion only soothes the body. Vasudeva's plea here is to spare Devakī's body. Where does this verse sit on that line — and where in your own life are you confusing the two kinds of compassion?
Role Play

Two students. Student A is a manager who has just learned that a long-time team member (Student B's character) is about to be fired tomorrow morning by a senior executive (a third student, or the teacher) for a mistake the executive is taking personally. Student A has a five-minute window with the executive before the decision is final. Student A must plead the case the way Vasudeva pleads here — not by arguing rights, not by threatening, not by invoking policy, but by appealing to the executive's own better nature and to the bonds that already exist (years of service, the team-member's family, the executive's own past mercies). After three minutes, switch: the 'executive' must respond honestly — what landed, what felt manipulative, what would actually have moved them. Then debrief: when is this kind of appeal a Vaiṣṇava act of daya, and when is it merely sentiment dressed up as virtue?

Objections & Responses
  • Vasudeva is a kṣatriya. Why is he begging instead of fighting? Surrendering his wife to a tyrant — even rhetorically — looks like cowardice, not dharma. A real warrior would have died defending her at the chariot.
    Look more carefully at what's happening. Vasudeva has already weighed the field: Kaṁsa is armed, surrounded by guards, and a dead Vasudeva saves no one — not Devakī, and certainly not the eighth child Kṛṣṇa has yet to send. His apparent softness is in fact abhaya, fearlessness operating with intelligence. The kṣatriya-dharma is to protect, not to perform protection. Later he will give up child after child to honor his word, which takes a steadier courage than swinging a sword once. The Gauḍīya reading: Vasudeva is already a devotee, and the devotee never confuses the gesture of bravery with the substance of it. He is buying time for the Lord's plan, not bargaining away his honor.
  • This whole appeal trades on Devakī being 'like a daughter,' 'a poor girl,' deserving of affection. Isn't that exactly the patriarchal framing modern readers reject — a woman valued through her relational role, pleaded for as helpless rather than respected as an equal?
    The verse is doing something more pointed than modern ears first hear. Vasudeva is not describing Devakī's worth — he knows perfectly well she is the future mother of the Lord. He is naming the relationships Kaṁsa himself claims to honor, holding up the mirror of Kaṁsa's own self-image as 'merciful to the fallen' so that to kill her, Kaṁsa would have to kill that self-image first. This is upāya — skillful means — not a metaphysical statement about women. The Bhāgavatam elsewhere is unambiguous that the soul is neither male nor female, and that Devakī's true glory is spiritual, not relational. What the verse actually models is how to speak to a person trapped in ahaṅkāra: meet them inside their own story and appeal to the better self they have buried there. That is daya in action, not patriarchy.
Analogy

Vasudeva makes his most tender appeal: 'This poor girl, your younger sister, is like your own daughter. You are merciful — do not kill her.' He addresses Kaṁsa as if Kaṁsa's better nature were the operative one. The verse models hope under impossibility: Vasudeva names the merciful man Kaṁsa is supposed to be, knowing he is not — but offering him, in the naming, one last chance to become him.
