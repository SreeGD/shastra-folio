Chapter 76 · Class prep

BG 76.1

Śukadeva Gosvāmī said: Now please hear, O King, another wondrous deed performed by Lord Kṛṣṇa, who appeared in His humanlike body to enjoy transcendental pastimes. Hear how He killed the master of Saubha.

Essence. Śukadeva invited Parīkṣit to hear another wondrous deed of Kṛṣṇa — the killing of Saubha's master. The Bhāgavata keeps offering one pastime after another; the cure for spiritual sluggishness is more hearing, not less.

BG 76.2

Śālva was a friend of Śiśupāla’s. When he attended the wedding of Rukmiṇī, the Yadu warriors defeated him in battle, along with Jarāsandha and the other kings.

Essence. Śālva, Śiśupāla's friend, had been defeated at Rukmiṇī's wedding. Defeat by the Lord and His associates does not always produce surrender; it sometimes produces obsession.

BG 76.3

Śālva swore in the presence of all the kings: “I will rid the earth of Yādavas. Just see my prowess!”

Essence. Śālva vowed publicly to rid the earth of the Yadus. Vows made out of wounded pride lock us into action that pride cannot back down from.

BG 76.4

Having thus made his vow, the foolish King proceeded to worship Lord Paśupati [Śiva] as his deity by eating a handful of dust each day, and nothing more.

Essence. Śālva worshiped Śiva by eating only a handful of dust daily. Severe austerity can be undertaken for malicious ends; the rigor itself does not sanctify the intention.

BG 76.5

The great Lord Umāpati is known as “he who is quickly pleased,” yet only at the end of a year did he gratify Śālva, who had approached him for shelter, by offering him a choice of benedictions.

Essence. Lord Śiva, normally quickly satisfied, took a full year — knowing the boon to Kṛṣṇa's enemy would be fruitless. Even apparent delays from the divine often reflect kind reluctance, not indifference.

BG 76.6

Śālva chose a vehicle that could be destroyed by neither demigods, demons, humans, Gandharvas, Uragas nor Rākṣasas, that could travel anywhere he wished to go, and that would terrify the Vṛṣṇis.

Essence. Śālva asked for a vehicle indestructible to demigods, demons, humans, Gandharvas, Uragas, or Rākṣasas — that could go anywhere and terrify the Vṛṣṇis. The wish list of revenge is precise and exhaustive; it leaves the wisher exposed only to the Lord.

BG 76.7

Lord Śiva said, “So be it.” On his order, Maya Dānava, who conquers his enemies’ cities, constructed a flying iron city named Saubha and presented it to Śālva.

Essence. Maya Dānava built the flying iron city Saubha as Śiva's instrument. The boons of the demigods are real; they can construct astonishing things — but the question is who is using them.

BG 76.8

This unassailable vehicle was filled with darkness and could go anywhere. Upon obtaining it, Śālva went to Dvārakā, remembering the Vṛṣṇis’ enmity toward him.

Essence. Filled with darkness, Saubha could go anywhere. Śālva flew it to Dvārakā remembering the Vṛṣṇis' enmity. Holding grievance leads us straight to the doorstep of those we resent.

BG 76.9-11

Śālva besieged the city with a large army, O best of the Bharatas, decimating the outlying parks and gardens, the mansions along with their observatories, towering gateways and surrounding walls, and also the public recreational areas. From his excellent airship he threw down a torrent of weapons, including stones, tree trunks, thunderbolts, snakes and hailstones. A fierce whirlwind arose and blanketed all directions with dust.

Essence. Śālva besieged Dvārakā, destroying its parks, mansions, walls — hurling stones, trees, thunderbolts, snakes. Resentment unleashed externally is indiscriminate; it harms the beautiful as it tries to reach its target.

BG 76.12

Thus terribly tormented by the airship Saubha, Lord Kṛṣṇa’s city had no peace, O King, just like the earth when it was attacked by the three aerial cities of the demons.

Essence. Kṛṣṇa's city had no peace, just as the earth once endured the three demoniac aerial cities. Even sacred spaces are tested by malice; their sanctity is not magical immunity.

BG 76.13

Seeing His subjects so harassed, the glorious and heroic Lord Pradyumna told them, “Do not fear,” and mounted His chariot.

Essence. Pradyumna told his subjects, 'Do not fear,' and mounted his chariot. Leadership in crisis says the simple sentence first and then acts. Reassurance without action is empty; action without reassurance is cold.

BG 76.14-15

The chief commanders of the chariot warriors — Sātyaki, Cārudeṣṇa, Sāmba, Akrūra and his younger brothers, along with Hārdikya, Bhānuvinda, Gada, Śuka and Sāraṇa — went out of the city with many other eminent bowmen, all girded in armor and protected by contingents of soldiers riding on chariots, elephants and horses, and also by companies of infantry.

Essence. Sātyaki, Cārudeṣṇa, Sāmba, Akrūra, Gada, Śuka, Sāraṇa and many others rode out with chariot, elephant, horse, and infantry forces. Leadership rallies competent allies; we do not face Śālva alone.

BG 76.16

A tumultuous, hair-raising battle then commenced between Śālva’s forces and the Yadus. It equaled the great battles between the demons and demigods.

Essence. A tumultuous hair-raising battle began, equal to ancient demigod-demon wars. Some struggles are genuinely epic; we don't have to apologize for the scale of our fight.

BG 76.17

With His divine weapons Pradyumna instantly destroyed all of Śālva’s magic illusions, in the same way that the warm rays of the sun dissipate the darkness of night.

Essence. Pradyumna destroyed Śālva's magic illusions like the sun dissipates night. Divine weapons cut through illusions; the heart that holds the Lord's name can pierce thicker fogs than we usually expect.

BG 76.18-19

Lord Pradyumna’s arrows all had gold shafts, iron heads and perfectly smooth joints. With twenty-five of them He struck down Śālva’s commander-in-chief [Dyumān], and with one hundred He struck Śālva himself. Then He pierced Śālva’s officers with one arrow each, his chariot drivers with ten arrows each, and his horses and other carriers with three arrows each.

Essence. Pradyumna's golden-shafted, iron-headed arrows struck Śālva's commander, then Śālva himself, then officers, drivers, and horses in calibrated counts. Skilled response is not random; it discriminates and lands precisely.

BG 76.20

When they saw the glorious Pradyumna perform that amazing and mighty feat, all the soldiers on both sides praised Him.

Essence. Soldiers on both sides praised Pradyumna's feat. Genuine excellence is acknowledged even by adversaries; we do not have to seek their applause, but it often comes anyway.

BG 76.21

At one moment the magic airship built by Maya Dānava appeared in many identical forms, and the next moment it was again only one. Sometimes it was visible, and sometimes not. Thus Śālva’s opponents could never be sure where it was.

Essence. The Saubha airship multiplied, vanished, reappeared in many forms — confusing all observers. Illusion's chief power is making us uncertain where it actually is; bhakti steadies us against that flicker.

BG 76.22

From one moment to the next the Saubha airship appeared on the earth, in the sky, on a mountain peak or in the water. Like a whirling, flaming baton, it never remained in any one place.

Essence. From moment to moment, Saubha appeared on earth, sky, mountain peak, water — a whirling flaming baton. Some opponents will never settle in one place; we have to learn to fight a moving target.

BG 76.23

Wherever Śālva would appear with his Saubha ship and his army, there the Yadu commanders would shoot their arrows.

Essence. Wherever Śālva appeared, the Yadu commanders shot their arrows. Steady, attentive response — wherever the enemy shows up, the answer goes there. Vigilance is the discipline.

BG 76.24

Śālva became bewildered upon seeing his army and aerial city thus harassed by his enemy’s arrows, which struck like fire and the sun and were as intolerable as snake venom.

Essence. Śālva was bewildered as the Yadu arrows struck like fire, the sun, and snake venom — burning, omnidirectional, lethal at one touch. Pure devotional response is more potent than pride realizes.

BG 76.25

Because the heroes of the Vṛṣṇi clan were eager for victory in this world and the next, they did not abandon their assigned posts on the battlefield, even though the downpour of weapons hurled by Śālva’s commanders tormented them.

Essence. The Vṛṣṇi heroes never left their posts, eager for victory in this world and the next. The kṣatriya doctrine — either die at one's post or win — is also bhakti's: stay where the Lord placed you.

BG 76.26

Śālva’s minister Dyumān, previously wounded by Śrī Pradyumna, now ran up to Him and, roaring loudly, struck Him with his club of black steel.

Essence. Dyumān, previously wounded, returned and struck Pradyumna with a black steel club. Opponents we have hurt often return more fiercely; not every battle is won in one round.

BG 76.27

Pradyumna’s driver, the son of Dāruka, thought that his valiant master’s chest had been shattered by the club. Knowing well his religious duty, he removed Pradyumna from the battlefield.

Essence. Pradyumna's charioteer, thinking his master was injured, removed him from the battlefield — out of intense love. Even mistaken protection by a loving servant has its dharmic logic.

BG 76.28

Quickly regaining consciousness, Lord Kṛṣṇa’s son Pradyumna said to His charioteer, “O driver, this is abominable — for Me to have been removed from the battlefield!

Essence. Regaining consciousness, Pradyumna said, 'This is abominable — for Me to have been removed from the battlefield!' Honor's first response on waking is to ask: where am I, and why?

BG 76.29

“Except for Me, no one born in the Yadu dynasty has ever been known to abandon the battlefield. My reputation has now been stained by a driver who thinks like a eunuch.

Essence. Pradyumna said no Yadu had ever fled the battlefield, and his reputation had been stained. Family honor is real to those who carry it; we cannot lightly dismiss the names we represent.

BG 76.30

“What will I say to My fathers, Rāma and Keśava, when I return to Them after having simply fled the battle? What can I tell Them that will befit My honor?

Essence. Pradyumna worried what He would say to His 'fathers,' Balarāma and Kṛṣṇa, when He returned. Accountability to elders we love restrains us in ways no rule can; love is the most precise discipline.

BG 76.31

“Certainly My sisters-in-law will laugh at Me and say, ‘O hero, tell us how in the world Your enemies turned You into such a coward in battle.’”

Essence. Pradyumna imagined His sisters-in-law mocking Him for cowardice. Sometimes our worst fears about how we'll be seen reveal what we actually value about ourselves.

BG 76.32

The driver replied: O long-lived one, I have done this knowing full well my prescribed duty. O my Lord, the chariot driver must protect the master of the chariot when he is in danger, and the master must also protect his driver.

Essence. The driver replied: 'I have done this knowing my duty — the driver protects the master, and the master protects the driver.' Mutual protection is the structure of any good partnership.

BG 76.33

With this rule in mind, I removed You from the battlefield, since You had been struck unconscious by Your enemy’s club and I thought You were seriously injured.

Essence. The driver explained: I removed You because You were struck unconscious and seemed seriously injured. Decisions made out of love sometimes overrule honor in the moment; both have their place.
