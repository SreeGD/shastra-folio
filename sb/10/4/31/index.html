Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.31

एवं चेत्तर्हि भोजेन्द्र पुरग्रामव्रजादिषु । अनिर्दशान् निर्दशांश्च हनिष्यामोऽद्य वै शिशून् ॥ ३१ ॥

Important Words
  • bhoja-indra — O Indra of the Bhojas (Kaṁsa)
    Vocative — 'O lord of the Bhojas'. The ministers flatter their king with royal titles even as they invite him into mass murder. The asuric counsel always wears a flattering opening.
  • pura-grāma-vraja-ādiṣu — in towns, villages, cattle-pastures, and so on
    Compound locative plural. Pura (city), grāma (village), vraja (cattle pasture) — the very list contains the name Vraja, where Kṛṣṇa now is. The asuric net is cast wide, and the very word for Kṛṣṇa's hidden home appears in the catalogue of intended slaughter.
  • anirdaśān — those less than ten days old
    Compound — 'not-ten-day-ed'. Newborns under ten days. The atrocity is precise; the ministers specify infants. The verse forces the reader to feel the moral weight of asuric expertise — its specificity is its evil.
  • haniṣyāmaḥ — we shall kill
    Future plural — 'we shall slay'. Plural agency: the ministers volunteer their own hands. The Bhāgavatam shows asuric coalition: many hands eager to do what one demon's mouth proposes.
  • śiśūn — infants
    Accusative plural — 'infants, children'. Same word as in v.5 (śiśavaḥ); the slaughter that began in Devakī's prison is now to be extended to every village. The verse rhymes its own atrocity outward.
If this is so, O King of the Bhoja dynasty, beginning today we shall kill all the children born in all the villages, towns and pasturing grounds within the past ten days or slightly more.
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
If this is so, O King of the Bhoja dynasty, beginning today we shall kill all the children born in all the villages, towns and pasturing grounds within the past ten days or slightly more.
Commentary

Anirdasan means children who were not ten days old. Nirdasan refers to those over ten days old. "We will kill all children today, who are either younger or older than ten days old."

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Addressing him as the king of kings (rājendra) they slight Indra. Another version has bhojendra with the same intention. We will kill the children certainly (vai), right now (adya).

Śrīdhara Svāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

You have defeated all kings (rājendra). They will help us. Another version has bhojendra with the same intention. Bhoja also means, “You enjoy sense objects without worry.” Just stay here. We will kill the children certainly (vai), right now (adya).

Personal Guidance
When fear of Kṛṣṇa's plan rules the heart, even 'reasonable' counsel turns murderous — material logic without bhakti always demands innocents.
Practical Applications
  • Notice today where fear of losing control is pushing you toward a 'practical' decision that quietly harms someone vulnerable.
  • Refuse to lend your intelligence to a plan whose logic depends on treating another soul as expendable, however efficient it sounds.
  • When advisors or your own mind escalate from worry to harm, pause and ask whether Kṛṣṇa's protection — not your preemptive action — is the real shelter.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my own life have I, like Kaṁsa's ministers, decided that the safest course is to eliminate every possibility of a threat — and what innocence am I willing to crush to feel secure?
  • When I sense that Kṛṣṇa's plan may inconvenience or unseat me, do I scheme to suppress His arrangement, or do I bow — and how would I tell the difference in this week's actual choices?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Notice the group chat or friend circle where someone with social power is quietly pushing a 'precaution' that hurts people who haven't done anything — excluding a classmate 'just in case,' piling onto someone online because a popular friend started it. Today, name it (even silently) as Kaṁsa-logic, and refuse to add the next message.
  • Before exams or auditions, watch for the temptation to sabotage others 'a little' to protect your own position — leaving a study resource off the group, spreading mild doubt about a competitor. That is the asura-minister voice. Catch it once this week and do the opposite: actively help one person you see as a rival.
  • Audit one feed (Instagram, X, a Discord) for accounts whose entire energy is preemptive attack on imagined threats. Unfollow two of them today. You become what you marinate in; an asuric court forms in the mind the same way it formed around Kaṁsa.
householder
  • In the next family or work decision driven by fear ('we should cut this person before they become a problem,' 'we should restrict the kids' freedom because something might happen'), pause and ask out loud: are we protecting something real, or are we Kaṁsa's ministers escalating a fear? Make one decision this week from sva-dharma and trust, not from preemptive harm.
  • Look at how money and opportunity flow through your household and your team. Is there a 'class of infants' — junior employees, in-laws, your own children's nascent interests — whose growth you are quietly suppressing because it might one day threaten your position or comfort? Identify one and actively bless its growth this month, even at cost to yourself.
  • When raising children, distinguish real protection (Kṛṣṇa-conscious shelter, sat-saṅga, healthy boundaries) from Kaṁsa-protection (controlling them so they cannot become anyone you did not authorize). Have one conversation this week where you let your child's emerging svabhāva surprise you instead of managing it.
senior
  • At this stage, the asuric temptation is not to kill rivals but to subtly diminish the next generation — withholding blessing, story, capital, authority — because their rising marks your declining. Today, deliberately empower one younger person in a way that costs you something (visibility, control, the last word).
  • Review the grudges and 'just in case' suspicions you still carry against family members or former colleagues. Each one is a small Kaṁsa-court in the heart, consuming the dhāma of your remaining years. Before the next ekādaśī, formally release one — speak it to Kṛṣṇa, and if possible, to the person.
  • Use this verse as a mortality meditation. Kaṁsa spent his reign trying to prevent a death that came anyway, on Kṛṣṇa's terms. Where are you still managing the universe to postpone the inevitable instead of using these years to deepen sādhana and surrender? Pick one such management project and consciously lay it down this week.
Common Pitfalls
  • Mistaking the ministers' ruthless 'practicality' for sober intelligence — applying cold cost-benefit logic to spiritual life and calling it maturity, when in truth it is rajas and tamas wearing the costume of decisiveness.
  • Reading this verse as merely a story about ancient demons and missing that the same impulse — preemptively destroying anything that might threaten one's position — operates inside one's own ahaṅkāra every day.
  • Drawing the wrong lesson on kṣamā: concluding that Kṛṣṇa's tolerance of Kaṁsa means we should tolerate evil indefinitely in our own sphere, rather than seeing that the Lord's patience serves the soul's ultimate good while still permitting righteous protection of the innocent in our sva-dharma.
Discussion Prompts
  • Kaṁsa's ministers propose killing every infant under ten days old to eliminate one child. Where in your own life have you watched a 'reasonable' precaution justify a clearly disproportionate harm — and did you call it out, or quietly go along because the authority figure (boss, parent, government) framed it as 'security'? Push past the easy condemnation of Kaṁsa and locate the same logic in yourself.
  • The verse exposes how envy of Kṛṣṇa, once enthroned in power, manufactures policy. Discuss with your partner: is there an institution in your life — a workplace, a family system, a friend group — whose 'rules' look rational on the surface but are actually downstream of one person's viṣaya-driven fear? Disagree honestly about whether you can stay inside such a system without complicity.
  • The asuras counsel Kaṁsa not from sva-dharma but from loyalty to a deluded master. When does loyalty become adharma? Share a concrete instance where you stayed loyal to a person, team, or ideology past the point you should have, and let the group press you on whether 'I was just following orders / supporting my friend' was honest.
Role Play

Two students play ministers to a modern 'Kaṁsa' — a charismatic startup founder who has just learned a competitor's product might disrupt his company within a year. He calls a closed-door meeting and asks his inner circle for proposals. One student must play a minister who, like the asuras here, escalates: propose an aggressive, ethically dubious campaign (smear the competitor's whole category, lobby regulators to crush dozens of innocent small players, poach and then discard talent) framed in the language of 'protecting the mission.' The second student must play a minister who tries to redirect the founder toward sva-dharma without losing the room. A third student plays the founder, who is genuinely afraid. Run it for six minutes, then debrief: what made the asuric counsel persuasive? Where did the dhārmika minister fail to land? When fear sits on the throne, what actually works?

Objections & Responses
  • This verse is just ancient mythological violence — infanticide framed as scripture. Why are we treating the counsel of demons as a spiritual lesson rather than a horror story we should distance ourselves from?
    You are right that it is a horror story — Vyāsa wrote it that way on purpose. Bhāgavatam does not endorse the asuras; it diagnoses them. The text is showing us, with clinical precision, how an envious heart in possession of power generates policy. The 'spiritual lesson' is not that we admire Kaṁsa's court; it is that we recognize its logic operating, in subtler clothes, in our own institutions and in our own minds. If you find the verse repellent, good — that revulsion is the first honest response. The next step is to ask where, in less obvious form, the same envy-of-the-Lord and the same readiness to harm 'just in case' lives in us. A teaching that only described saints would not equip us to recognize the asura when he sits across the conference table, or in our own chest.
  • If Kṛṣṇa is the supreme Personality of Godhead and fully in control, why does He allow this — countless innocent infants under threat — instead of stopping Kaṁsa at the source? Either He is not all-powerful or not all-good.
    The Gauḍīya answer does not flinch from this. Kṛṣṇa's līlā unfolds in a world where He honors the free will of His parts-and-parcels, including their envy. Kaṁsa is not a puppet; he is a soul exercising the freedom to oppose the Lord, and the consequences ripple outward. What Kṛṣṇa guarantees is not a sanitized world but the protection of surrendered souls and the eventual dissolution of the asura — and indeed in this very līlā Kaṁsa's plans are foiled and he is delivered. More importantly, the Bhāgavatam refuses the modern frame in which the only real harm is bodily harm. The soul is eternal; the bodies that fall in Kaṁsa's reign are not annihilated, and many of those souls are elevated by their proximity to the Lord's pastimes. This is not a glib answer that excuses cruelty — kṣamā does not mean we tolerate Kaṁsas — but it does relocate the question. The real catastrophe in the verse is not the children's bodies; it is Kaṁsa's soul, hardening into the very envy that will destroy him. That is what Kṛṣṇa is patiently allowing to play itself out, and that is the deeper compassion the verse is pointing toward.
Analogy

A wolf, hearing that one lamb has escaped its trap, resolves to gut every flock for ten miles around. The ministers' counsel — kill all newborn infants in every village — is the asuric mind's signature: when its target eludes, it widens the net until thousands of innocents are inside it. The Bhāgavatam will return to such moments to show: this is why the Lord must descend.

Stories
Pūtanā sent to Vraja

One of the immediate fruits of this very counsel was the demoness Pūtanā, sent by Kaṁsa to nurse all newborn infants with poisoned breast-milk. She entered the homes of Gokula, attempted Kṛṣṇa, and was killed by Him — the Lord granting her the position of a nurse in the spiritual world because she had at least come in the disguise of mother (SB 10.6).

The Bhāgavatam thus shows that even the asuric net catches itself: Pūtanā, going to kill Kṛṣṇa, instead met Him and was elevated. Kaṁsa's ministers plan widely; Bhagavān, accepting their plans into His līlā, redeems whomever He chooses. The asuric scheme becomes the stage on which more devotees are made.
