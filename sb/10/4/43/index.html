Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.43

Important Words
  • durmantribhiḥ — with bad ministers
    Compound instrumental — 'bad-counsel ones'. Dur-mantra: malformed counsel. The Bhāgavatam names them as the agents of Kaṁsa's ruin. The Gauḍīya emphasis on sat-saṅga is the obverse of this verse.
  • sammantrya — having consulted (with them)
    Gerund of sam-√mantr — 'having taken counsel together'. The asura's deliberation. Wrong counsel + careful deliberation = ruin pursued methodically.
  • durmatiḥ — of bad intelligence
    Compound nominative — 'bad-intelligenced'. Mati is intelligence; dur-mati is intelligence inverted. The verse's diagnosis: Kaṁsa's intelligence itself is now defective, not merely his information.
  • brahma-hiṁsām — violence against brāhmaṇas
    Compound — 'brahmin-killing'. The ministers' plan crystallized into one phrase. The Gauḍīya note: brahma-hiṁsā is named in śāstra as one of the five most heinous sins (mahā-pātaka). To pursue it deliberately is asuric perfection in reverse.
  • hitam — good, beneficial
    From √hi — that which is for one's good. The asuric tragedy of the verse: Kaṁsa mistakes brahma-hiṁsā for hita. The Gauḍīya frame: real hita is parama-hita, the highest good, which is bhakti to Bhagavān. To call hiṁsā 'hita' is the deepest possible inversion.
  • kāla-pāśa-āvṛtaḥ — covered / bound by the noose of time
    Compound — 'time-noose-enveloped'. Kāla here is Yamarāja's time. Once one is āvṛta (covered) by his pāśa, escape becomes hard; the surrounding intelligence has already been compromised. Only Kṛṣṇa-saṅga loosens the noose, and Kaṁsa is too late.
Śukadeva Gosvāmī continued: Thus, having considered the instructions of his bad ministers, Kaṁsa, who was bound by the laws of Yamarāja and devoid of good intelligence because he was a demon, decided to persecute the saintly persons, the brāhmaṇas, as the only way to achieve his own good fortune.
Purport

Śrīla Locana dāsa Ṭhākura has sung, āpana karama, bhuñjāye śamana, kahaye locana dāsa. Instead of taking good instructions from the sages and the śāstras, godless nondevotees act whimsically, according to their own plans. Actually, however, no one has his own plans because everyone is bound by the laws of nature and must act according to his tendency in material, conditional life. Therefore one must change one’s own decision and follow the decision of Kṛṣṇa and Kṛṣṇa’s devotees. Then one is rescued from punishment by Yamarāja. Kaṁsa was not uneducated. It appears from his talks with Vasudeva and Devakī that he knew all about the laws of nature. But because of his association with bad ministers, he could not make a clear decision about his welfare. Therefore the Caitanya-caritāmṛta ( Madhya 22.54) says: ‘sādhu-saṅga,’ ‘sādhu-saṅga’ — sarva-śāstre kaya lava-mātra sādhu-saṅge sarva-siddhi haya If one desires his real welfare, he must associate with devotees and saintly persons and in this way rectify the material condition of his life.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Śukadeva Gosvāmī continued: Thus, having considered the instructions of his bad ministers, Kaṁsa, who was bound by the laws of Yamarāja and devoid of good intelligence because he was a demon, decided to persecute the saintly persons, the brāhmaṇas, as the only way to achieve his own good fortune.
Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Kaṁsa was himself of bad intelligence and took advice from ministers equally of bad intelligence. Instead of hitam sometimes hitām is seen.

Personal Guidance
Bad counsel binds the conditioned soul to ruin; only sādhu-saṅga rescues us from acting whimsically against our own welfare.
Practical Applications
  • Audit the three voices you listened to most this week and ask honestly which were durmantris steering you away from Kṛṣṇa.
  • Replace one hour of worldly counsel today by hearing from a devotee — a class, a recorded lecture, or a phone call with a senior Vaiṣṇava.
  • Refuse to act on any major decision today until you have measured it against śāstra and the words of Kṛṣṇa's devotees.
Reflection Questions
  • Whose counsel have I been preferring over the śāstra and sādhus — and what 'good fortune' am I chasing through that bad association that, like Kaṁsa's, will only tighten the kāla-pāśa around me?
  • Where am I treating hostility toward devotees or saintly persons — even subtle dismissal, mockery, or impatience — as if it were clever self-protection, when it is actually the surest sabotage of my own welfare?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Audit your phone for one day: list the three accounts, group chats, or friends whose counsel most shaped a decision you made this week. Ask honestly — are these durmantris flattering my viṣaya, or are they pointing me toward sva-dharma, study, and sādhana? Mute or unfollow at least one.
  • Identify one 'Kaṁsa moment' in your week — a moment when you knew the right thing (finish the assignment, skip the party, tell the truth to a parent) but a peer reframed the wrong thing as 'what's actually best for you.' Write down the exact sentence they used. Naming the durmantri script is half of breaking it.
  • Before the next big choice (a course, an internship, a relationship boundary), put fifteen minutes of real sādhu-saṅga in front of it — read a Prabhupāda purport on the question, or call a senior devotee. Prabhupāda quotes 'lava-mātra sādhu-saṅge sarva-siddhi' — even a moment is enough; you are not too busy.
householder
  • Map your household's 'cabinet of ministers' — the financial advisor, the in-laws, the WhatsApp parent group, the colleague at work, the YouTube guru. For each, ask: does their counsel routinely treat brahma-hiṁsā (cutting time/money/attention away from devotees, scripture, deity service) as hitam — 'sensible,' 'realistic,' 'what's best for the kids'? Demote the durmantris in your decision-making weight.
  • Before the next family financial or career decision, formally consult one sādhu and one śāstric reference alongside the practical advisors. Not as a rubber stamp — as a real input that can overrule the others. Kaṁsa's tragedy was that the sādhus were available and he chose to persecute them instead of consult them.
  • Watch how you justify cutting back on sādhana when work or kids surge. If the inner voice says 'skipping japa this month is actually best for my family' — recognize that exact grammar: brahma-hiṁsāṁ hitaṁ mene. Reverse it for one week and observe what actually breaks (usually nothing) and what steadies (usually everything).
senior
  • Review the major decisions of the last two decades and identify the durmantris whose counsel you now regret following. Do not stew in it — name them clearly to one trusted person, then formally release the pattern. Kāla-pāśa is real; at this stage the rescue is decisive sādhu-saṅga, not further self-justification.
  • You are now positioned to be either a durmantri or a sādhu in the lives of your children and grandchildren. Notice which one you default to — flattering their materialist plans to keep peace, or gently and concretely pointing toward Kṛṣṇa. Choose one upcoming family conversation and prepare to speak as a sādhu, not as a comfortable elder.
  • Make a will, a vairāgya plan, and a sādhana plan in the same week, treating all three as a single act. The verse's phrase kāla-pāśāvṛtaḥ is not abstract for a person past sixty. Loosening attachments now — possessions, opinions, grudges — is the practical form of refusing Kaṁsa's mistake of postponing surrender until circumstances force it.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading this verse as being about 'those demonic people out there' instead of recognizing the Kaṁsa-logic in one's own decision-making — the moments when bad counsel feels like clear-headed strategy.
  • Reducing sādhu-saṅga to occasional lecture attendance or social presence, while one's actual operating advisors — colleagues, algorithms, ambitious peers — remain the durmantris steering daily choices.
  • Mistaking education, articulateness, or scriptural knowledge (which Kaṁsa also possessed) for protection from delusion, and therefore neglecting the humbling, ongoing dependence on devotees that this verse insists upon.
Discussion Prompts
  • Kaṁsa was not uneducated — he knew the laws of nature, yet his durmantris (bad ministers) drowned out that knowledge. In your own life, who are the durmantris — the voices, feeds, or friends whose counsel you keep taking even though you know better? Share one specific example with your partner, and have them push back if your 'bad minister' actually sounds reasonable to them.
  • The verse says Kaṁsa decided that brahma-hiṁsā (violence to brāhmaṇas and saintly persons) was hitam — his own welfare. Discuss: where in modern life do we similarly redefine harm to devotees, scriptures, or sādhus as 'progress,' 'reform,' or 'self-care'? Try to find a case where you disagree with each other.
  • Prabhupāda quotes the Caitanya-caritāmṛta — even a moment (lava-mātra) of sādhu-saṅga gives complete perfection. Yet most of us spend hours daily in asat-saṅga and minutes in sādhu-saṅga. Argue both sides: is the lava-mātra promise literal, or is it conditional on something the verse doesn't spell out?
Role Play

Two students play colleagues at a startup. One has just been told by the founder (the 'minister') that the only way to hit the next funding milestone is to quietly mislead a saintly old investor — a longtime devotee — about the company's actual numbers. The founder frames it as 'protecting everyone's livelihood.' The other student plays a friend the first one calls that night for advice. The friend must, without lecturing, help the colleague see the durmantri pattern from SB 10.4.43 — that calling brahma-hiṁsā 'hitam' is exactly Kaṁsa's mistake — and propose a concrete alternative course before morning. Run it twice with roles swapped. Class then debriefs: at what moment did the kāla-pāśa (the noose of time/Yamarāja) tighten, and at what moment did sādhu-saṅga begin to loosen it?

Objections & Responses
  • Calling Kaṁsa 'devoid of good intelligence because he was a demon' is circular and unfair — he was just acting rationally on the information his advisors gave him. Anyone in his position would have done the same.
    The verse is precise: durmatiḥ (bad intelligence) is paired with kāla-pāśāvṛtaḥ — bound by the noose of time. It is not circular; it is diagnostic. Kaṁsa had access to the same śāstra and the same sādhus he was now planning to persecute. Prabhupāda specifically notes Kaṁsa was educated and knew the laws of nature from his earlier exchange with Vasudeva and Devakī. The 'demon' label is not name-calling but a description of one who, having access to good counsel, deliberately selects bad counsel because it flatters his viṣaya-attachment. The Gauḍīya point is sober, not moralistic: we are all one durmantri away from this, which is why sādhu-saṅga is presented not as a nicety but as the only rescue.
  • This sounds like a license for devotees to dismiss any criticism as coming from 'bad ministers' and only listen to people who already agree with them — a closed echo chamber.
    A fair concern, and the verse itself is the safeguard. Kaṁsa's ministers told him precisely what he wanted to hear — that persecuting brāhmaṇas was hitam, his own welfare. The mark of a durmantri is not that they disagree with you; it is that they confirm your viṣaya-driven plan and counsel you against the sādhus and the śāstra. The Gauḍīya test is objective: does this counsel move me toward bhakti, toward the personal service of Kṛṣṇa, toward humility before guru-sādhu-śāstra — or away from it? Real sādhu-saṅga regularly stings; it is not an echo chamber but a mirror. If the counsel you call 'sādhu-saṅga' never contradicts you, that itself is a durmantri warning.
Analogy

A blindfolded man walks confidently toward the cliff because his friends, in the same blindfold, are cheering him on. Kaṁsa, bound by the noose of Kāla and stripped of intelligence, takes the worst counsel as wisdom. The Gauḍīya reading: when buddhi is gone, only the noose of time remains visible; the way out is sat-saṅga in time, before the noose tightens.

Stories
Duryodhana's rejection of Kṛṣṇa's peace offer

When Kṛṣṇa Himself went as the Pāṇḍavas' messenger and offered Duryodhana the simplest peace — five villages for five brothers — Duryodhana, advised by Karṇa, Śakuni, and Duḥśāsana, refused. Kṛṣṇa later said: yasya nigraha-īcchām anuvartate buddhiḥ, his intelligence had aligned with destruction (Mahābhārata, Udyoga Parva).

Kaṁsa in this verse is at the same hinge. Bound by Yamarāja's law (kāla-pāśa-āvṛta) and surrounded by durmantris, he weighs persecution of the sādhus as hita (welfare). The Bhāgavatam's diagnosis is sharp: durmati — bad intelligence. The Gauḍīya cure is sat-saṅga; without it, even the king with all resources walks the cliff path.
