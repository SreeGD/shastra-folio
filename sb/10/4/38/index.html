Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.38

यथामयोऽङ्गे समुपेक्षितो नृभि- र्न शक्यते रूढपदश्चिकित्सितुम् । यथेन्द्रियग्राम उपेक्षितस्तथा रिपुर्महान् बद्धबलो न चाल्यते ॥ ३८ ॥
yathāmayo ’ṅge samupekṣito nṛbhir
[yathaamayo ’nge samupekshito nribhir]

Important Words
  • āmayaḥ — a disease
    From ā-√mā — 'in-measured', i.e. that which gets into the body. A standard śāstric word for sickness. The image is precise: the early disease is curable; the rooted disease is not.
  • samupekṣitaḥ — neglected, ignored
    Past passive participle of sam-upa-√īkṣ — 'thoroughly looked-past'. The verb is used here in a negative sense (neglect), but the Bhāgavatam elsewhere uses similar grammar to commend looking past minor offenses; context entirely changes the verb's value.
  • rūḍha-padaḥ — with established foothold
    Compound — 'with sprouted footing'. The disease has rooted itself (rūḍha) and become impossible to dislodge. The Gauḍīya parallel: sin permitted to root becomes habit; habit becomes character; character becomes destiny. Strike at the seed.
  • indriya-grāmaḥ — the collection of senses
    Compound — 'sense-village'. The senses as a community. Gītā 2.60 echoes: even the wise yogi's mind can be carried away by the indriyas when neglected. The Vaiṣṇava cure: engage every sense in Kṛṣṇa's service (hṛṣīkeṇa hṛṣīkeśa-sevanam).
  • ripuḥ mahān — a great enemy
    Adjective + noun — 'a great enemy'. The ministers complete the analogy: neglected disease, neglected senses, neglected enemy — all become great. The asuric application here is murder; the Gauḍīya application is vigilance in sādhana.
  • baddha-balaḥ — of consolidated strength
    Compound — 'tied-strength'. The enemy whose force is now consolidated, knotted up, gathered. Once strength is baddha, untying it is hard. The verse's image-rich vocabulary makes the medicine memorable, even when the surgeon misuses it.
As a disease, if initially neglected, becomes acute and impossible to cure, or as the senses, if not controlled at first, are impossible to control later, an enemy, if neglected in the beginning, later becomes insurmountable.
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
As a disease, if initially neglected, becomes acute and impossible to cure, or as the senses, if not controlled at first, are impossible to control later, an enemy, if neglected in the beginning, later becomes insurmountable.
Commentary

As a disease in the body when neglected becomes acute (rudha pada) and as senses not brought under control in the beginning become uncontrollable, so the enemy, neglected and becoming strong, can become victorious.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

“If Viṣṇu is hiding what harm can he do to me?” This verse answers. Sometimes a powerful medicine cannot cure a disease left unattended. Another example is given concerning the senses. If the enemy becomes extremely strong he cannot be moved from his place, what to speak of killing him.

Personal Guidance
Whatever enemy you neglect at the start — a disease, an unchecked sense, a bad habit — grows insurmountable; strike it now while it is still small.
Practical Applications
  • Name the one indriya-leak you have been excusing this week (food, screen, speech) and impose a concrete restraint on it today before it hardens.
  • Refuse to 'wait and see' on a small lapse in your sādhana — recommit to your chanting and morning program this morning, not next Monday.
  • Identify one anārtha you tolerated last year that is now harder to uproot, and let that sting motivate decisive action on the small one in front of you now.
Reflection Questions
  • Which 'small disease' in my inner life — a craving, a resentment, an indulgence — have I been quietly tolerating because it doesn't yet feel acute, and what will it cost me once it has rūḍha-pada (taken root)?
  • Where am I currently extending kṣamā not as spiritual patience but as cowardice — letting an enemy of bhakti grow strong inside me because confronting it now would be uncomfortable?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Name one app, one feed, or one habit you have let grow for six months without resistance — set a concrete daily limit on it tonight, before it becomes rūḍha-pada in your nervous system.
  • In your friend group, identify the one influence (a person, a chat, a recurring late-night ritual) you have been 'patient' with while it slowly pulls you off your studies and sādhana — speak to one trusted senior or devotee about it this week.
  • Pick the single sense most active in your daily drift — usually the eyes (screens) or the tongue (snacking, gossip) — and impose one small, visible discipline on it for the next seven days, treating it as early treatment, not punishment.
householder
  • Audit one financial, marital, or parenting drift you have been 'letting ride' — an unspoken resentment, a child's habit, a creeping expense — and address it this week while it is still small enough to handle without rupture.
  • Examine which sense-indulgence you justify as 'earned' after a hard workday (drink, scroll, binge, vent) — these are the baddha-bala enemies that grow on a householder's fatigue; replace one with a fixed family kīrtana or reading slot.
  • Notice where kṣamā with your children or spouse has slid into negligence — patience with character is dharma, but patience with a rooting bad habit is not; have the loving, early conversation now.
senior
  • Identify one long-tolerated attachment — a possession, a grievance, a self-image from your career — and begin loosening it deliberately this month, before death forces a violent removal.
  • Mentor a younger family member or devotee about the specific 'small enemy' you yourself neglected and paid for over decades — your hindsight is their early treatment.
  • Let the body's growing limitations be your teacher: each diminishing sense is a chance to redirect attention from viṣaya to Kṛṣṇa, finishing the work of vairāgya that the verse warned would only get harder if postponed.
Common Pitfalls
  • Mistaking the verse's call for early intervention as a license for harshness or paranoia toward people, when the real 'enemy' addressed here is the unchecked indriya-grāma and its viṣayas within oneself.
  • Misreading kṣamā as passive tolerance of one's own anarthas — telling oneself 'I am being patient with my weaknesses' while a sense-habit silently consolidates baddha-bala.
  • Treating the teaching as a one-time vow ('I will fix this tomorrow') rather than a daily diagnostic — the disease grows precisely in the gap between recognizing it and acting on it.
Discussion Prompts
  • Share one 'small enemy' in your inner life that you have been neglecting — an indulgence, a resentment, a sense-craving. Why has it been easier to ignore than to address? Push back on each other if someone's example sounds too tidy.
  • The verse equates uncontrolled senses with an enemy whose forces have grown strong. Where do you disagree with a fellow practitioner about which sense-pull is most dangerous in your stage of life — and is that disagreement about principle or about taste?
  • Kṣamā (forgiveness, patience) is named as the connected value, yet the verse warns against patience with the senses. Discuss: where does kṣamā become complicity? Bring a real example where you confused tolerance with negligence.
Role Play

Two students are roommates in their second year of college. One has begun spending three to four hours nightly on short-form video and late-night snacking; grades are slipping and morning sādhana has quietly stopped. The other roommate has noticed but said nothing for months out of politeness. Cast one student as the drifting roommate (defensive, minimizing — 'it's just a phase, everyone does this'), one as the concerned roommate finally speaking up, and one as a senior devotee they go to for counsel. Play out the conversation where the concerned roommate must name the 'small disease' before it becomes 'rūḍha-pada' — rooted and incurable — without moralizing or shaming.

Objections & Responses
  • This sounds like spiritual paranoia — treating every small pleasure as an 'enemy.' Isn't that a recipe for scrupulosity and joylessness?
    The verse is not against pleasure; it is against negligence. A doctor who tells you to treat a small infection early is not anti-body — he is pro-body. Kṛṣṇa, through Vasudeva, is being pro-soul. The senses are not the enemy; the uncontrolled sense-grāma — the gang of senses left to roam unsupervised — becomes the enemy. A practitioner who eats prasāda with relish, who serves with full energy, who laughs in saṅga, has not failed this verse. The one who lets the tongue, the eyes, or the mind quietly take over the schedule has. Vairāgya is precision, not grimness.
  • Modern psychology says shame and self-policing make problems worse, not better. Telling people their habits are 'enemies' will just create more anxiety.
    There is a real distinction between shame ('I am bad') and clear-eyed diagnosis ('this habit, if untreated, will harm me'). The verse uses the doctor's language, not the judge's: āmaya — disease — neglected becomes incurable. No shame in having a disease; great foolishness in pretending it isn't there. The Gauḍīya path actually reduces anxiety because it names the problem honestly and offers a concrete remedy — sādhana, saṅga, prasāda, the holy name. What creates lasting anxiety is the modern strategy of normalizing every drift until it is too rooted to address. Honest naming early is the kindest thing one can do for oneself.
Analogy

A small leak in the dyke, left another day, becomes the flood. The ministers reach for this very image — āmaya (disease), indriya-grāma (uncontrolled senses), ripuḥ (enemy) — to justify total uprooting now. The image is true; the application is asuric. The Gauḍīya physician applies it daily, but inward — to the small leaks of inattention in his own sādhana, before they swell into apātha.

Stories
Yamarāja on the senses

In the Bhāgavatam, Yamarāja tells his messengers (Canto 6) that the senses, if left unchecked, drag the soul to hell more surely than any enemy. The same image the ministers now misuse — uncontrolled senses become impossible to control — Yamarāja uses to send his Yamadūtas to the right addresses.

The Gauḍīya tradition takes this verse-image as a sādhana-instruction. Sense-control is to be applied to oneself, not to one's enemies. Caitanya Mahāprabhu's response to a wandering mind was the holy name; the demon's response is murder. Same image, opposite direction. The wise reader extracts the image from the ministers' mouth and uses it on his own indriyas.
