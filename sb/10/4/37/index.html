Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.37

तथापि देवा: सापत्न्‍यान्नोपेक्ष्या इति मन्महे । ततस्तन्मूलखनने नियुङ्‌क्ष्वास्माननुव्रतान् ॥ ३७ ॥

Important Words
  • sāpatnyāt — due to rivalry
    From sa-patni — 'co-wife' relation, hence the rivalry of co-rivals. The asura-deva rivalry runs through the Vedas; the Bhāgavatam will resolve it (Canto 8) by showing that both are part of Bhagavān's pastimes.
  • na upekṣyāḥ — not to be neglected
    Verbal adjective — 'not-to-be-disregarded'. The ministers urge attention. The Gauḍīya note: the asura is never neutral toward dharma; he must either ignore it or destroy it, and ignoring is only the first stage.
  • tat-mūla-khanane — in digging out their root
    Compound locative — 'in the root-digging of that'. The verb is exactly the asura's signature move: root-digging. By v.39 the ministers will name Viṣṇu as the root they intend to dig. The Gauḍīya emphasis: that root cannot be dug; only the soul that tries to dig it gets uprooted.
  • niyuṅkṣva — engage / employ (us)
    Imperative of ni-√yuj — 'engage'. The ministers offer their service. The verb is identical to the one used for engaging in Bhagavān's seva; same grammar, opposite direction. The same human energy can be niyukta to Kṛṣṇa or against Him.
  • anuvratān — (us, your) faithful followers
    Compound — 'after-vowed'. Those whose vow follows the master's. In bhakti, the devotee is Kṛṣṇa's anuvrata; here, the ministers are Kaṁsa's. The form of allegiance is the same; the object determines the meaning.
Nonetheless, because of their enmity, our opinion is that the demigods should not be neglected. Therefore, to uproot them completely, engage us in fighting with them, for we are ready to follow you.
Purport

According to moral instructions, one should not neglect to extinguish fire completely, treat diseases completely, and clear debts completely. Otherwise they will increase and later be difficult to stop. Therefore the ministers advised Kaṁsa to uproot his enemies completely.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Nonetheless, because of their enmity, our opinion is that the demigods should not be neglected. Therefore, to uproot them completely, engage us in fighting with them, for we are ready to follow you.
Commentary

Though they are weak, the enemy cannot be neglected. This is according to niti sastra.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

We think you cannot neglect the devatās. “But the devatās have fled in fear anyway.” Engage us in uprooting Viṣṇu, the root of the devatās. He has appeared as a child and is hiding.

Concepts
Jiva
Personal Guidance
Kaṁsa's ministers urge total uprooting of enemies — a warning of how the asuric mind, once fearful of Kṛṣṇa, escalates to obliterate every trace of the divine.
Practical Applications
  • Notice today where you are tempted to 'uproot completely' someone who has wronged you — and refuse the asuric logic that frames vengeance as kartavya.
  • Identify one unfinished sādhana 'debt' (a chant, a service, a vow) and clear it fully today, applying the ministers' principle to bhakti instead of enmity.
  • Speak to Kṛṣṇa before any meeting where 'advisors' (inner or outer) push you toward harsh, total action — let Him, not rajas-tamas counsel, decide the response.
Reflection Questions
  • What 'small fire' in my life — an unaddressed grievance, a half-finished duty, a lingering debt — am I tolerating today that the ministers' logic warns will only grow harder to extinguish?
  • When I justify aggression or thoroughness as kartavya, am I actually serving the soul, or am I, like Kaṁsa's ministers, dressing up envy and self-preservation in the language of duty?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Name the one habit — the late-night scroll, the comparison spiral on Instagram, the 'just one episode' — that you keep half-quitting. This week, treat it like Prabhupāda's fire: extinguish it completely, not 80%. Tell one friend so the embers can't relight in private.
  • You have a 'debt' in some subject or skill you have been avoiding (a backlog of problem sets, a paper you keep restarting). Pick the oldest one and clear it fully before Friday. Half-paid debts in sādhana and in studies both compound the same way.
  • Watch how a friend group can become 'ministers' — egging each other on toward someone who has been labeled the enemy (a teacher, an ex, a rival group). Notice the Kaṁsa-mood when it arises in the group chat. Refuse to add the next message.
householder
  • Identify the one financial 'ember' in the household — a lingering EMI, an unfiled return, an unresolved dispute over money with a sibling or in-law. Apply the purport literally this month: clear it completely, not partially.
  • In raising children, distinguish material compassion (shielding them from every discomfort) from real compassion (rooting out a developing anārtha — a tantrum pattern, a screen dependence, a dishonesty). Pick one and address it at the root with your spouse this week, kindly but completely.
  • At work, notice where you are tempted to 'uproot a rival' in Kaṁsa's mood — undermining a colleague, hoarding information, building a faction. Replace that energy with uprooting one of your own anārthas (envy at a promotion, resentment of a boss). That is the enemy whose annihilation Kṛṣṇa actually sanctions.
senior
  • Make a written list of unfinished 'debts' — relational apologies pending, promises half-kept, papers and possessions left for others to sort. Begin clearing them completely while you have time and clarity. Tyāga at this stage means not bequeathing your loose ends to your children.
  • Examine which long-standing 'enmities' you are still quietly maintaining — the cousin you stopped speaking to, the colleague you never forgave. The verse warns that what is not extinguished grows back; in the next life as much as in this one. Uproot the resentment, not the person.
  • When mentoring younger sādhakas or grandchildren, refuse the role of Kaṁsa's minister — the elder who flatters and confirms whatever the younger already wants. Speak the inconvenient kartavya plainly, with karuṇā. Your remaining authority is for that, not for comfort.
Common Pitfalls
  • Borrowing the ministers' 'uproot it completely' logic to justify ruthlessness toward people we dislike — forgetting that this counsel is given to a demon against the devotees, and is a warning, not a model.
  • Applying the 'extinguish fire, treat disease, clear debts completely' principle only to outer affairs while leaving the real root — ahaṅkāra and material attachment — quietly intact.
  • Confusing material compassion (sparing oneself discomfort by avoiding hard duties) with tyāga, and thus letting genuine responsibilities — debts, illnesses, unresolved obligations — fester under a spiritual-sounding excuse.
Discussion Prompts
  • Kaṁsa's ministers urge him to 'uproot enemies completely.' When is this same logic — finish what you start, leave no loose ends — a genuine kartavya, and when is it the demonic mood dressed up as diligence? Share an example from your own life where you couldn't tell the difference until later.
  • The purport gives three things one must extinguish completely: fire, disease, debt. Pair up and each name one fire, one disease, and one debt in your sādhana right now that you have been half-finishing. Then disagree honestly about whether the other person's list is really 'incomplete' or whether they are being scrupulous.
  • The ministers speak with full conviction — 'engage us, we are ready to follow you.' Loyalty and readiness to act are sāttvic qualities, yet here they serve adharma. Where in your own life have you offered loyal, energetic service to the wrong cause? What protected you, or failed to protect you, from Kaṁsa's logic?
Role Play

Two students are partners in a small business. One has discovered that a competitor — a former friend — has been quietly poaching clients. Student A plays the 'minister': argues that they must 'uproot' the competitor completely through aggressive legal and reputational counter-attack, citing the principle that half-measures only let the problem grow back. Student B plays the practitioner trying to discern sva-dharma: must respond to real harm without sliding into Kaṁsa's mood of total annihilation of the 'enemy.' Run the conversation for five minutes, then switch roles. Afterward, the group identifies where each side spoke from kartavya and where each slid into rajas/tamas masquerading as duty.

Objections & Responses
  • This verse is just bad advisors egging on a tyrant. Why are we studying the speech of demons as if it teaches us anything? Surely the lesson is simply 'don't be like Kaṁsa.'
    Vyāsa records the ministers' counsel precisely because their logic is seductive and partially correct — fires, diseases, and debts truly must be finished. That is why Prabhupāda extracts the moral instruction from their mouths without endorsing their target. The danger is not that we will obviously side with Kaṁsa; it is that we will quietly adopt his framework — treating people as 'enemies to uproot' — while believing we are simply being thorough. Studying the asuric voice trains the ear to recognize it inside ourselves. Dismiss the verse as 'just bad guys talking' and you lose the mirror.
  • 'Uproot enemies completely' sounds like a recipe for ruthlessness — the opposite of karuṇā. How can a tradition that teaches compassion preserve and comment on advice like this without contradiction?
    Karuṇā in our line is not the material compassion of avoiding all conflict; it is service to the soul. The ministers err not by recommending decisive action — Arjuna himself is told to fight — but by misidentifying the enemy. The real enemies to uproot completely are lust, anger, greed, pride, illusion, envy: the inner Kaṁsa-faction. Applied there, 'do not leave embers, do not leave half-treated disease, do not leave unpaid debts of anāartha' is exact spiritual instruction. Tyāga is not softness; it is the willingness to finish the work of cutting attachment at the root, which is the most compassionate thing a sādhaka can do, for himself and for everyone he serves.
Analogy

Even after dismissing the giant in the next valley, the asura cannot quite sleep; he asks his men to mine the valley anyway. The ministers, having declared the demigods harmless, immediately propose total uprooting. Such is the asuric mind: it cannot rest while any trace of dharma remains anywhere. The Gauḍīya reading: bhakti-mūla is what they unconsciously fear, and what they cannot reach.

Stories
Rāvaṇa's resolve to uproot the ṛṣis

Before the central conflict of the Rāmāyaṇa, Rāvaṇa had spent decades persecuting the ṛṣis of Daṇḍakāraṇya. His ministers urged him, and he agreed, that the sages must be totally uprooted — for as long as their yajñas burned, his dominion was incomplete. The very forest into which Rāma and Sītā would later be exiled was the field of this uprooting.

The ministers' counsel in this verse — tat-mūla-khanane — is structurally the same. Kaṁsa is to dig out the root (mūla) of the demigods, which they will identify (in vv.39, 42) as Viṣṇu Himself and His Vaiṣṇavas. The asura always reaches for the root because he senses, dimly, that bhakti is the actual threat. The Bhāgavatam shows him reaching.
