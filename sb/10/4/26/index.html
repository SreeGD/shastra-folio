Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.26

एवमेतन्महाभाग यथा वदसि देहिनाम् । अज्ञानप्रभवाहंधी: स्वपरेति भिदा यत: ॥ २६ ॥

[evam etan mahaa-bhaaga]

[yathaa vadasi dehinaam]

Important Words
  • mahā-bhāga — O greatly fortunate one
    Vocative — Vasudeva returns Kaṁsa's earlier address (mahā-bhāgau, v.18) with grace, even after all that has happened. The saint speaks to the asura by his potential, not his record.
  • dehinām — of embodied beings
    Genitive plural. Dehin = one who has a deha (body), Gītā's standard term. The Gauḍīya distinction: the dehin is not the deha; the eternal jīvātmā temporarily wears the body, and bhakti reveals this clearly.
  • ajñāna-prabhavā — born of / arising from ignorance
    Compound — 'ignorance-arising'. Prabhavā is feminine, agreeing with aham-dhīḥ. The verse roots the I-thought in ajñāna; jñāna alone removes its philosophical root, but only bhakti removes its emotional grip.
  • aham-dhīḥ — the idea / sense of 'I' (in the body)
    Compound — 'I-thought'. The egoic identification with the body. In Gauḍīya theology, the false aham-dhīḥ is replaced not by no-aham but by the true aham — 'I am eternal servant of Kṛṣṇa' (jīvera svarūpa haya kṛṣṇera nitya-dāsa).
  • sva-parā iti — 'one's own and others' — thus
    Compound + quote-particle: the distinction that says 'this is mine, that is another's.' Bheda based on this is, the verse says, born of ajñāna. The Gauḍīya nuance: in the realm of Kṛṣṇa-bhakti, the only true 'mine' is Kṛṣṇa Himself.
O great personality Kaṁsa, only by the influence of ignorance does one accept the material body and bodily ego. What you have said about this philosophy is correct. Persons in the bodily concept of life, lacking self-realization, differentiate in terms of “This is mine” and “This belongs to another.”
Purport

Everything is done automatically by the laws of nature, which work under the direction of the Supreme Personality of Godhead. There is no question of doing anything independently, for one who has put himself in this material atmosphere is fully under the control of nature’s laws. Our main business, therefore, should be to get out of this conditioned life and again become situated in spiritual existence. Only due to ignorance does a person think, “I am a demigod,” “I am a human being,” “I am a dog,” “I am a cat,” or, when the ignorance is still further advanced, “I am God.” Unless one is fully self-realized, one’s life of ignorance will continue.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
O great personality Kaṁsa, only by the influence of ignorance does one accept the material body and bodily ego. What you have said about this philosophy is correct. Persons in the bodily concept of life, lacking self-realization, differentiate in terms of "This is mine" and "This belongs to another."
Commentary

Because of (yatah) the spell of false identity, one thinks of objects as mine and his (sva para iti bhida). In this way one thinks in terms of duality.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

You speak this philosophy, which I previously spoke but which you ignored. O great king (mahārāja)! Now you have attained scriptural knowledge! Another version has mahābhāga. The meaning is the same. Of course mahāraja can mean mahā arāja (greatly inglorious), because of his proximity to inauspiciousness. Mahābhāga can mean mahā abhāga (most unfortunate). The identity with the body with the conceptions of “I am the body, I am the enjoyer and doer” is pervaded by ignorance. From this arises separation of self from others.

Personal Guidance
The 'mine' and 'yours' that drives our striving is born of ajñāna; surrender that bodily ego to Kṛṣṇa and act under His direction.
Practical Applications
  • Notice each time today you say or think 'mine' about body, family, or possessions, and silently re-offer it to Kṛṣṇa as His.
  • Refuse the inner story that you are the independent doer — before any major task, acknowledge you are working under the laws of nature directed by Him.
  • Speak to one person you instinctively label as 'other' (a stranger, a rival, an outsider) and serve them today as a soul belonging to Kṛṣṇa, not as sva or para.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my day do I most automatically slip into 'this is mine' and 'that belongs to another' — and what would shift if I saw both as Kṛṣṇa's arrangement under prakṛti's laws?
  • Which identity am I currently defending most fiercely — 'I am a professional,' 'I am a parent,' 'I am spiritual' — and how is that ahaṅkāra-dhī obscuring my actual situation as an eternal servant?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Notice the next time you say 'my grades,' 'my followers,' 'my friend group' — pause and ask whether the 'my' is the soul speaking or the body-mind clinging. Try saying it once a day without the possessive and see what shifts.
  • When a classmate or roommate gets the internship, the partner, or the praise you wanted, watch the sva-para bhidā fire up: 'that should have been mine.' Name it as ajñāna in your journal that night — not as moral failure, just as the body-ego doing what bodies do.
  • On social media, you are training the ahaṅkāra-dhī all day: every like sharpens 'mine,' every comparison sharpens 'theirs.' Pick one platform and go a week consuming without posting — let the algorithm starve the false ego and notice who you are underneath it.
householder
  • In the next family money decision — a school choice, a major purchase, an aging parent's care — explicitly test which voice is speaking: 'this is mine to control' or 'I am a caretaker of what belongs to Kṛṣṇa.' Make the decision from the second voice, even once.
  • With your children, watch how often you say 'my son,' 'my daughter' in a possessive register — and how the relationship changes when, in your own heart, you hold them as souls entrusted to you for a season. They will feel the difference before you can articulate it.
  • At work, the sva-para bhidā runs the office: my project, their team, my bonus. For one week, do excellent work without claiming credit in the meeting — not to be invisible, but to test whether your effort survives without the 'mine' label attached.
senior
  • Walk through the house and name the things you have called 'mine' for forty or fifty years — the books, the photographs, the furniture. Begin loosening grip while you can still choose the loosening, rather than having it taken at the end.
  • When you mentor someone younger, resist the temptation to make them 'your' student or successor. Pass on what you have received as a trustee passing on a trust — your job is faithful transmission, not legacy-building for the body that will not last.
  • Sit each evening with the recognition that the body you have lived in — child, youth, adult, elder — is itself a sequence of 'mine' that the soul has already outgrown three times. Let that pattern teach you how to release the current body, and the people and things attached to it, when the time comes.
Common Pitfalls
  • Using 'everything happens by nature's laws' as a fatalistic excuse for passivity — forgetting that the verse points to getting out of conditioned life, not surrendering to it.
  • Intellectually agreeing 'I am not the body' while still organizing every practical decision around bodily ego, possessions, and tribal 'mine vs. theirs' lines.
  • Sliding from 'I am not this body' into the most advanced ignorance Prabhupāda warns of — 'I am God' — instead of recognizing oneself as Kṛṣṇa's eternal servant.
Discussion Prompts
  • Vasudeva agrees with Kaṁsa's philosophical statement that bodily distinctions are born of ignorance — yet he is saying this to the very man who has just murdered his children. Is this skillful diplomacy, genuine humility, or a kind of compromise? Defend your position to a partner who disagrees.
  • The verse names sva-pareti bhidā — the 'mine' versus 'another's' division — as a symptom of ajñāna. Pick a real attachment in your life right now (a possession, a relationship, a role) and argue with your partner whether dropping the 'mine' label would actually make you more loving or less responsible.
  • Prabhupāda lists 'I am God' as the most advanced stage of ignorance, more dangerous than 'I am a dog.' Many in the room may have absorbed Vedānta-flavored ideas where realizing 'I am God' is the goal. Sit with someone who leans that way (or play the role) and surface the real disagreement — don't paper it over.
Role Play

A family inheritance dispute. Two siblings (assign roles) are dividing their late father's estate. Sibling A insists, 'This house is mine — I cared for Dad the last five years.' Sibling B counters, 'Half is legally mine, and I won't be cheated.' A third student plays a Gauḍīya elder the family has invited to mediate. The elder must engage SB 10.4.26 honestly — naming the sva-para bhidā at the root — without drifting into a quietist 'nothing is yours, let it go' that ignores varṇāśrama duty and the real care work Sibling A actually did. Run the scene for ten minutes, then debrief: where did the elder slip into platitude, and where did real medicine land?

Objections & Responses
  • If 'mine' and 'another's' are illusions born of ignorance, then property rights, family bonds, and even consent are illusions too. This verse leads straight to moral nihilism — anyone can take anything because no one really owns anything.
    The verse dismantles the bodily ego's claim of ownership, not the working order of varṇāśrama. Vasudeva himself, speaking this verse, still protects his wife, still honors the agreement he just made with Kaṁsa, still acts within dharma. The point is not 'nothing is anyone's' but 'nothing is ultimately the body's' — everything belongs to Kṛṣṇa, and we are caretakers, not proprietors. That actually raises the bar on ethics: you cannot steal, harm, or violate, because every body and possession is His. Nihilism collapses meaning; this verse re-locates meaning in Him.
  • Modern psychology says a healthy ego — a stable sense of 'I' and 'mine' — is necessary for functioning, for boundaries, for mental health. Calling ahaṅkāra a product of ignorance sounds like spiritual bypass that produces doormats and cult-vulnerable personalities.
    Gauḍīya teaching distinguishes ahaṅkāra (false bodily identification) from ātma-jñāna (the awakened 'I' as eternal servant of Kṛṣṇa). Surrendering the false ego does not produce a vacuum — it produces a stronger, clearer self anchored in sva-svarūpa. Healthy psychological boundaries actually become easier, not harder: you can say no to exploitation precisely because your worth no longer depends on others' approval of your body, role, or possessions. The doormat is someone who has lost the false ego without finding the true self. The verse points toward the latter, not the former.
Analogy

A child draws a chalk line across a room and announces, 'this side is mine, that side is yours.' The chalk, the room, and the child himself all belong to the father; the line is the only thing that does not. Vasudeva tells Kaṁsa that all 'mine' and 'yours' is exactly such a chalk-line, born of ajñāna. Rub it out and the whole room is Bhagavān's.

Stories
Vidura and Maitreya on the bodily conception

When Vidura, after years of pilgrimage and Mahābhārata sorrow, came to Maitreya Ṛṣi at Haridvāra, Maitreya delivered the long teaching that fills Bhāgavatam Cantos 3 and 4 — beginning with the same diagnosis Vasudeva offers Kaṁsa here: the bodily conception (aham-dhīḥ in this body) is the root of all bheda, and the cure is recognizing that all souls are equally parts of the Supreme Person.

The Gauḍīya reading sharpens the cure: it is not enough to drop the bodily ego into impersonal sameness; one must take up the spiritual ego as eternal servant of Kṛṣṇa. Maitreya leads Vidura there; Vasudeva is trying to lead Kaṁsa there. One disciple receives, one does not.
