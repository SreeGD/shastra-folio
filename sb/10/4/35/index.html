Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.35

न त्वं विस्मृतशस्त्रास्त्रान् विरथान् भयसंवृतान् । हंस्यन्यासक्तविमुखान् भग्नचापानयुध्यत: ॥ ३५ ॥
na tvaṁ vismṛta-śastrāstrān
[na tvam vismrita-shastraastraan]
virathān bhaya-saṁvṛtān
[virathaan bhaya-samvritaan]

Important Words
  • vismṛta-śastra-astrān — those who have forgotten weapons and missiles
    Compound: 'forgotten-weapons-arrows'. The śastra is the hand-weapon, the astra the mantra-charged projectile. Even forgetting their use places one in the protected category.
  • virathān — those without chariots
    Compound: 'chariot-less'. The classical kṣatriya battle was chariot-fought; to lose one's chariot was to be effectively neutralized. The Mahābhārata records repeatedly this moment of vehicle-loss as a turning point of dharma.
  • bhaya-saṁvṛtān — those surrounded / covered by fear
    Compound: 'fear-enveloped'. To be saṁvṛta (covered, enclosed) by fear is to lose one's effective will. Dharma exempts such an enemy. The Gauḍīya reading: only sheltering in Bhagavān truly removes such bhaya-saṁvṛti.
  • anya-āsakta-vimukhān — those whose face is turned away because attached to other things
    Compound: 'other-attached-averted'. One who is not facing the fight, who is attached elsewhere. Even the kṣatra-dharma extends mercy to such an enemy. The Gauḍīya note: anya-āsakti, attachment to other-than-Kṛṣṇa, is itself the spiritual averted-ness; from that, the only refuge is the holy name.
  • bhagna-cāpān — those whose bows are broken
    Compound: 'broken-bowed'. The bow broken, the warrior is disarmed. The list builds methodically. The Bhāgavatam shows kṣatra-dharma in fine detail so that, when Kaṁsa's ministers later urge him past these protections, the reader feels the precise weight of what is being abandoned.
When the demigods are bereft of their chariots, when they forget how to use weapons, when they are fearful or attached to something other than fighting, or when their bows are broken and they have thus lost the ability to fight, Your Majesty does not kill them.
Purport

There are principles that govern even fighting. If an enemy has no chariot, is unmindful of the fighting art because of fear, or is unwilling to fight, he is not to be killed. Kaṁsa’s ministers reminded Kaṁsa that despite his power, he was cognizant of the principles of fighting, and therefore he had excused the demigods because of their incapability. “But the present emergency,” the ministers said, “is not intended for such mercy or military etiquette. Now you should prepare to fight under any circumstances.” Thus they advised Kaṁsa to give up the traditional etiquette in fighting and chastise the enemy at any cost.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
When the demigods are bereft of their chariots, when they forget how to use weapons, when they are fearful or attached to something other than fighting, or when their bows are broken and they have thus lost the ability to fight, Your Majesty does not kill them.
Commentary

Your sense of righteousness is a cause for the increase of strength of the devatas. Therefore you should give up this sense of righteousness. This is not the time for dharma.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

The devatās lived because you followed rules of dharma. Though they held their swords and arrows they forgot to use them because of extreme fear (vismṛta-śastrāstrān).

Concepts
Jiva
Personal Guidance
Even Kaṁsa honored the dharma of not striking the disarmed — yet his ministers urged him past it; notice where you abandon principle for urgency.
Practical Applications
  • Notice one moment today where 'emergency' tempts you to drop a principle you normally hold, and refuse to cross it.
  • Speak to someone weaker or unprepared with the restraint of a kṣatriya — never press an advantage just because you can.
  • Refuse the ministers' logic in your own head — the voice that says 'this situation is special, the rules don't apply now' — and surrender the matter to Kṛṣṇa instead.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my own life do I exploit someone's 'broken bow' — pressing my advantage when a person is fearful, distracted, or already disarmed — and call it winning?
  • Whose counsel am I currently listening to that, like Kaṁsa's ministers, urges me to abandon principle 'just this once' for the sake of an emergency I have invented?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Identify one classmate or online acquaintance who is currently 'disarmed' — failing a course, going through a breakup, being mocked in a group chat. Today, refuse to pile on, even by silence or a laughing emoji. That refusal is kartavya in your stage of life.
  • Notice the 'minister voice' in your own head before exams or applications — the one that says 'everyone cheats a little, this is an emergency, you can't afford principles right now.' Name it out loud to a trusted friend; naming weakens it.
  • When you win an argument with a friend or sibling and they have clearly given up — bow broken, so to speak — stop. Do not press the final point. Practice the dhīra-bhāva of restraint while you are young, before the stakes get higher.
householder
  • In family disagreements — with a spouse, an aging parent, a teenage child — watch for the moment the other person becomes 'fearful' or 'distracted from the fight.' That is the moment to lower your weapons, not to land the decisive blow. Real daya begins where your rhetorical advantage ends.
  • At work, when a colleague or junior is visibly out of their depth, refuse the easy promotion-by-comparison. Adambhitva here means not staging your competence against their collapse. Offer a hand instead, even when no one is watching.
  • Examine the family budget and career decisions for 'emergency' framing — 'we have to do this aggressive thing now, ethics later.' Kaṁsa's ministers used exactly this logic. Bring one such decision back into alignment with sva-dharma this week, even at material cost.
senior
  • In your final decades, you hold an unusual kind of power — over inheritance, family memory, who is forgiven and who is not. Identify someone 'disarmed' in your circle (an estranged relative, a fallen friend, a child who failed) and extend mercy now, while you still can. Legacy is decided here.
  • Mentor a younger person specifically on the principles you yourself bent under pressure earlier in life. Be honest about where the 'minister voice' won. This is seva more valuable than advice that paints you as a hero.
  • Loosen your grip on being right in old quarrels. The bows in those arguments have long been broken on both sides; continuing to strike is not dharma but ahaṅkāra in late dress. Offer the day's accumulated rightness to Kṛṣṇa each evening and go to sleep lighter.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading this verse as a celebration of Kaṁsa's restraint and missing that the ministers are pushing him to discard even that residual etiquette — mistaking the demonic logic of expedience for prudent counsel.
  • Reducing 'compassion for the disarmed' to bodily, material daya — sparing someone's feelings or comfort — while neglecting real compassion: serving the soul by speaking truth and pointing to Kṛṣṇa.
  • Using 'principles of fighting' as a pretext for self-righteousness — congratulating yourself for following a code in low-stakes moments while quietly dropping it the instant your kartavya becomes inconvenient or your position threatened.
Discussion Prompts
  • Kaṁsa's ministers explicitly tell him to abandon the etiquette of fighting because the 'emergency' is too great. Where in your own life have you used the word 'emergency' to justify dropping a principle you knew you should keep? Share with your partner — was it actually an emergency, or convenience dressed up as one?
  • The verse describes mercy extended to the disarmed, the fearful, the distracted, the broken. In a culture that rewards finishing your opponent — in arguments, at work, on social media — is restraint toward a weaker adversary a virtue or a weakness? Disagree out loud with your group; do not settle too quickly.
  • Kaṁsa once practiced kṣatriya etiquette and is now being coached out of it. Identify one small principle you have personally been 'coached out of' by people around you — colleagues, friends, family — and trace the steps. Was the drift gradual or sudden? Did you notice it happening?
Role Play

Two students are roommates studying for the same competitive exam. Student A discovers Student B is genuinely struggling — sleep-deprived, anxious, not retaining material — and has fallen far behind. Student A could share notes and a study plan, or could stay silent and quietly secure a higher class rank. A third student plays the 'minister' role, whispering to A: 'This is your one shot. Sentimentality is a luxury you cannot afford right now. Help them after the exam.' Run the scene three times — once where A listens to the minister, once where A refuses, and once where A tries to negotiate a middle path. After each round, the group discusses: which version honored real daya (compassion for the soul) versus material compassion, and where did A's own ahaṅkāra speak in the minister's voice?

Objections & Responses
  • This whole verse is about ancient warrior codes — chariots, bows, battlefield etiquette. It has nothing to say to a modern person who will never fight a war.
    The śāstra rarely lingers on logistics for their own sake. The point is not the chariot but the principle: power restrained by dharma is power; power unrestrained is asurik. Every one of us holds power over someone weaker — a junior at work, a child, a parent in decline, a stranger online. The question the verse silently asks is whether we strike at the disarmed, the fearful, the distracted. The battlefield has only changed locations.
  • Doesn't this verse actually praise Kaṁsa? It seems to flatter him for being a 'fair' fighter, when the whole canto is leading toward his destruction by Kṛṣṇa. That feels morally confused.
    It is the ministers who flatter Kaṁsa, not Vyāsa. Their speech is preserved precisely so we can hear how evil counsel sounds — reasonable, strategic, even citing the man's past virtues to coax him past them. Kṛṣṇa eventually kills Kaṁsa not because Kaṁsa was a bad warrior but because he surrendered the last remnants of his dharma to fear of his own death. The verse is a warning, not a tribute. Read it as the moment a man's last principle is being talked out of him.
Analogy

Even a tiger has the dignity of not pouncing on the deer who is already on its knees. Kaṁsa, the ministers admit, has kept this much of kṣatra-dharma: he does not strike the disarmed. The verse is the chapter's last glimmer of dharma in the demon. By the next verse, his ministers will be coaching him to lose even this — the asura's slide is, in śāstra, always a tutored slide.

Stories
Arjuna sparing Karṇa's wheel

In the Mahābhārata, when Karṇa's chariot-wheel sank into the earth and he stepped down to lift it, he called out to Arjuna, 'Wait — strike not a defenseless enemy!' Arjuna lowered his bow; Kṛṣṇa rebuked him, reminding him of all the times Karṇa had broken dharma against the Pāṇḍavas, and ordered the killing.

The ministers in this verse invoke a similar kṣatra-rule to flatter Kaṁsa. The Gauḍīya reading is two-edged: the rule is genuine, but its application requires viveka, which the asura's ministers will now help him discard. To strike the disarmed is generally adharma; to allow the asura his disarming-trick is also adharma. Only Kṛṣṇa's word — through guru and śāstra — can decide which case is which.
