Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.15

Important Words
  • pāpmanā — by sin / by the sinner (me)
    Instrumental — 'by me, the sinful one'. Kaṁsa, in this lucid moment, accepts agency for what daiva (he had earlier said) did. Real repentance owns the deed; only then can mercy receive it.
  • puruṣa-adaḥ — man-eater (Rākṣasa)
    Compound: 'eater of men'. Kaṁsa names himself with the most degraded epithet — like a Rākṣasa who eats his own offspring. The Bhāgavatam wants this self-naming on record; even one moment of accurate self-knowledge is worth preserving.
  • apatyam — offspring
    Not 'son' but 'offspring' — the impersonal noun makes the violation more chilling. The killed were one's own progeny; the demon's hand turned against the very flesh that came from his sister.
  • hiṁsitāḥ — killed / harmed
    Past passive participle of hims — to harm. The opposite of ahiṁsā. The Gauḍīya emphasis: hiṁsā is not only physical violence but any act that injures another's body or progress toward Bhagavān. Kaṁsa's hiṁsā is the maximal form.
  • sutāḥ — sons
    Plural — 'sons'. The verse insists on the count. Devakī's six previous children were killed; the seventh was Balarāma (transferred to Rohiṇī); the eighth is Kṛṣṇa. Each suta named here is a real life Kaṁsa took.
Alas, my sister! Alas, my brother-in-law! I am indeed so sinful that exactly like a man-eater [Rākṣasa] who eats his own child, I have killed so many sons born of you.
Purport

Rākṣasas are understood to be accustomed to eating their own sons, as snakes and many other animals sometimes do. At the present moment in Kali-yuga, Rākṣasa fathers and mothers are killing their own children in the womb, and some are even eating the fetus with great relish. Thus the so-called civilization is gradually advancing by producing Rākṣasas.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Alas, my sister! Alas, my brother-in-law! I am indeed so sinful that exactly like a man-eater [Rākṣasa] who eats his own child, I have killed so many sons born of you.
Commentary

In the following verses Kamsa begins to pacify Devaki and Vasudeva. Bhama means the brother-in-law. Purusadah means raksasa, one who eats human flesh. "Just as a raksasa kills his own children, I have killed many of your sons."

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Uttering aho twice indicates his pain. Bata indicates lamentation. Puruṣāda means a person who eats his own children. One time Durgā saw a woman Rākṣasa eating her own child. Out of compassion Durgā said “From this time the children of Rākṣasas will be very strong (and cannot be eaten). (Thus Kaṁsa should also refrain from further killing.)

Śrīdhara Svāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

Uttering aho twice indicates his pain. Bata indicates lamentation. I am sin personified because of my previous sins (pāpmanā). Pāpaṁ pāpānubandhi syāt: sin connects one to further sin.

Personal Guidance
Vasudeva names his complicity in his sons' deaths — true daya begins where we stop hiding from the harm our pāpa has caused.
Practical Applications
  • Name aloud today one harm your action or silence enabled — refuse the comfort of vague regret and own it specifically before Kṛṣṇa.
  • Notice where you call cruelty 'choice' or 'progress' — speak of abortion and child-harm in Kali-yuga as Prabhupāda does, without softening the word Rākṣasa.
  • Approach someone you have wronged today and confess plainly, as Kaṁsa's confession to Devakī and Vasudeva models — no self-justification, only remorse and request for forgiveness.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my life am I, like Kaṁsa lamenting too late, performing remorse for harm I am still structurally committed to causing — and what would real repentance, not just ritualized regret, actually cost me?
  • Whose suffering have I treated as collateral to my own security or pleasure (children, dependents, the unseen) — and how does Kali-yuga's normalization of that harm let me feel innocent while behaving like a Rākṣasa?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Audit your group chats and social feeds this week: where have you been a 'puruṣāda' with words — devouring a classmate's reputation, piling on in a pile-on, laughing at a meme that mocked someone's body or family? Name one specific instance, message that person privately, and own it without excuses.
  • In exam season, notice the temptation to 'kill the future' of a peer — withholding notes, spreading wrong information, gatekeeping resources to protect your rank. Do the opposite once this week: deliberately help a competitor, and watch the ahaṅkāra squirm.
  • If you are in a relationship or hookup culture, sit with the verse's plain meaning: a child in the womb is a soul, not a problem. Decide now, before any pressure arrives, what you will and will not do — and tell one trusted devotee or elder, so the decision is witnessed.
householder
  • Family-planning conversations with your spouse are sādhana, not logistics. This week, have one explicit conversation framed by this verse: are our reasons for or against another child rooted in service to Kṛṣṇa and to the soul who would come, or in viṣaya — career, lifestyle, comfort?
  • Identify the 'children' you have been quietly devouring at work — junior colleagues whose growth you have stunted, ideas you took credit for, reports you crushed in performance reviews to protect your bonus. Make one concrete restitution this week: a public acknowledgment, a promotion advocated for, an apology in writing.
  • With your own children, ask: am I raising souls or raising résumés? Pick one daily routine — meals, bedtime, the school drop — and re-cast it as service to a soul Kṛṣṇa has entrusted to you, not as a project you are managing.
senior
  • Sit with the people you may have harmed across decades — a sibling, a first spouse, an estranged child, a colleague you outmaneuvered. Make a written list. Before the body fails, write or speak one direct, un-defensive apology to each, asking nothing in return. This is the dhīra's housekeeping.
  • Mentor a young person who is at the age where they could become a Kaṁsa — early career, raw ambition, sharp tongue. Do not lecture. Tell them, simply, the cost of the harms you yourself caused, and how only Kṛṣṇa's shelter eventually softened it.
  • Re-read your will and your end-of-life plans through the lens of this verse: are you leaving wealth, position, or property in a way that will tempt your heirs into rākṣasa-behavior toward each other? Restructure now — equal, transparent, witnessed — so your death is not the trigger for a new generation's hiṁsā.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading this verse as being only about literal abortion in Kali-yuga and missing Kaṁsa's deeper warning: that ahaṅkāra-driven self-protection will eat what we are bound by sva-dharma to protect.
  • Mistaking Kaṁsa's eloquent self-flagellation ('aho... mayā... pāpmanā') for genuine surrender — performing daya toward those we have wronged while leaving the underlying viṣaya (here, his fear of death and lust for power) completely untouched.
  • Using the Rākṣasa imagery to condemn 'so-called civilization' out there while refusing to audit the small, daily ways one's own choices — career, comforts, consumption — quietly devour the welfare of one's own kin and dependents.
Discussion Prompts
  • Kaṁsa here weeps and calls himself a rākṣasa for killing his nephews — yet within hours he resumes his demonic ways. Share with your partner: have you ever had a Kaṁsa-style breakdown of remorse that did not actually change your behavior? What was missing — sādhana, association, surrender — that would have made the repentance real?
  • Prabhupāda compares Kaṁsa's child-killing to abortion in Kali-yuga and calls the so-called civilization 'gradually advancing by producing Rākṣasas.' Discuss honestly in your group: where do we draw the line between true compassion (daya) for the soul in the womb and the modern arguments of bodily autonomy, economic hardship, or 'quality of life'? Where do you find yourself half-agreeing with the modern view, and why?
  • Devakī and Vasudeva forgive Kaṁsa instantly when he speaks tenderly. Is that naive? Or is it the natural samatā of a devotee? Disagree with each other: when is forgiveness premature, and when is withholding it a failure of nyāya?
Role Play

Two students play Kaṁsa and a close friend. Kaṁsa has just realized — after years of cruelty in business, family, or speech — that he has 'eaten his own children': harmed those he was meant to protect (a younger sibling he bullied, a junior at work he sabotaged, a child he neglected). He turns to the friend in raw remorse, exactly like the verse: 'aho bhaginy aho bhāma… I am a puruṣāda.' The friend is a Gauḍīya practitioner. The friend must NOT offer cheap absolution ('it's okay, everyone makes mistakes') and must NOT crush him with condemnation. They must guide him toward real ahaṅkāra-melting kartavya: confession to those harmed, restitution where possible, and shelter at Kṛṣṇa's feet — knowing that Kaṁsa's tears alone, without surrender, will not save him. Run it for five minutes; then swap roles.

Objections & Responses
  • Calling parents who choose abortion 'rākṣasas' is judgmental, cruel, and ignores the real economic, medical, and psychological pressures women face. This kind of language is exactly why religious traditions lose credibility with modern people.
    A Gauḍīya teacher does not soften the diagnosis to flatter the patient. Prabhupāda is not insulting individuals; he is naming a civilizational pattern in which the womb — the most protected place in any sane society — has become the most dangerous. Real daya, real compassion, is for the soul that has just taken shelter in that womb and is now evicted by force. The modern pressures are real; the answer is not to redefine the act but to build a culture (varṇāśrama, extended family, devotee community) in which no mother feels she has to. We weep with Kaṁsa, we do not excuse him — and we do not let our discomfort with strong words make us complicit in the act.
  • Kaṁsa's tearful apology in this verse looks like manipulation — he is buying time. So why should I take any of this remorse seriously? Isn't the whole scene just a demon's theatrics?
    Yes, and that is precisely the lesson. Tears without surrender to Kṛṣṇa are theatrics; the same Kaṁsa who weeps here will plot again tomorrow. The Gauḍīya line does not measure repentance by emotion but by sādhana — by whether one actually takes shelter (śaraṇāgati). When you find yourself in a Kaṁsa-moment of remorse, do not trust the tears. Test them: am I now chanting, associating with devotees, accepting correction, making restitution? If not, the ahaṅkāra is simply re-arming itself in the costume of guilt. The verse is not endorsing Kaṁsa's grief; it is showing us how convincing — and how empty — un-surrendered remorse can be.
Analogy

A man who has eaten poisoned fruit suddenly sees the stain on his hands and cries out. Kaṁsa names himself a man-eater — puruṣa-ad — and his words taste of bile. Real remorse begins not with explanation but with naming the act. The Gauḍīya tradition holds that until the soul says the name of its sin aloud, it cannot say the Name of Hari with clean lips.

Stories
Ajāmila's remorse at the moment of death

Ajāmila, the fallen brāhmaṇa, on seeing the Yamadūtas come for him, recalled in horror every step of his fall — abandoning his chaste wife, taking a woman of low birth, cheating, stealing, killing animals. In that moment of full naming, he cried 'Nārāyaṇa' for his son, and it became the cry of his soul (SB 6.1–2).

Kaṁsa's confession here has the same structure but lacks the saving Name. He calls himself puruṣa-ad, like a man-eater of his own offspring. The Gauḍīya reading: confession is genuine, but without taking shelter of Bhagavān, the confession circles its own filth. Real prāyaścitta is harināma-saṅkīrtana.
