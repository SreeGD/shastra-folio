Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.19

भुवि भौमानि भूतानि यथा यान्त्यपयान्ति च । नायमात्मा तथैतेषु विपर्येति यथैव भू: ॥ १९ ॥

In this world, we can see that pots, dolls and other products of the earth appear, break and then disappear, mixing with the earth. Similarly, the bodies of all conditioned living entities are annihilated, but the living entities, like the earth itself, are unchanging and never annihilated [na hanyate hanyamāne śarīre].
Purport

Although Kaṁsa is described as a demon, he had good knowledge of the affairs of ātma-tattva, the truth of the self. Five thousand years ago, there were kings like Kaṁsa, who is described as an asura, but he was better than modern politicians and diplomats, who have no knowledge about ātma-tattva. As stated in the Vedas, asaṅgo hy ayaṁ puruṣaḥ: the spirit soul has no connection with the changes of the material body. The body undergoes six changes — birth, growth, sustenance, by-products, dwindling and then annihilation — but the soul undergoes no such changes. Even after the annihilation of a particular bodily form, the original source of the bodily elements does not change. The living entity enjoys the material body, which appears and disappears, but the five elements earth, water, fire, air and ether remain the same. The example given here is that pots and dolls are produced from the earth, and when broken or destroyed they mingle with their original ingredients. In any case, the source of supply remains the same. As already discussed, the body is made according to the desires of the soul. The soul desires, and thus the body is formed. Kṛṣṇa therefore says in Bhagavad-gītā (18.61) : īśvaraḥ sarva-bhūtānāṁ hṛd-deśe ’rjuna tiṣṭhati bhrāmayan sarva-bhūtāni yantrārūḍhāni māyayā “The Supreme Lord is situated in everyone’s heart, O Arjuna, and is directing the wanderings of all living entities, who are seated as on a machine, made of the material energy.” Neither the Supersoul, Paramātmā, nor the individual soul changes its original, spiritual identity. The ātmā does not undergo birth, death or changes like the body. Therefore a Vedic aphorism says, asaṅgo hy ayaṁ puruṣaḥ: although the soul is conditioned within this material world, he has no connections with the changes of the material body.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
In this world, we can see that pots, dolls and other products of the earth appear, break and then disappear, mixing with the earth. Similarly, the bodies of all conditioned living entities are annihilated, but the living entities, like the earth itself, are unchanging and never annihilated [na hanyate hanyamāne śarīre [Bg. 2.20]].
Commentary

"You should not lament after considering what is atma and non-atma." With this intention he speaks. "Just as clay pots are created and destroyed, so the bodies of the jiva are created and destroyed. And as the earth itself is not affected by these changes, so the jiva is not affected, though the material bodies undergo change."

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

All bodies made of elements on earth are destroyed. Or just as clay pots have a beginning and end, seen by all, so all bodies have a beginning and end. The ātmā however does not undergo such changes just as earth element is not destroyed, being the shelter of other objects. However the comparison with earth does not indicate that ātmā is the cause of all things (and matter is illusion) according to Vaiṣṇava philosophy. Or, the example of earth may also indicate that ātmā is the cause, since Kaṁsa took shelter of Advaita philosophy out of hatred of the Lord. And with this philosophy the example may show that jīva is pervasive like the earth. Or, bodies change just as verbal roots change into various verb forms.

Śrīdhara Svāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

An example is given. On earth, creatures such as kings or grass come and go. They cannot remain together at all times. Their bodies change but the ātmā does not. This part is eternal. Though situated in the body, the ātmā does not change like the body. Because of its nature it does not change. It remains. Temporary things must separate. There is no suffering for the indestructible, since it always exists. Everything on earth is created and is destroyed. Or whatever exists on earth is seen to have birth and death. Ātmā does not have changes like birth and death (viparya).

Concepts
JivaAtma
Personal Guidance
Bodies form and break like clay pots returning to earth, but the ātmā — like the earth itself — never changes; live from that unchanging self today.
Practical Applications
  • Notice one bodily change you are quietly grieving today — a wrinkle, a fatigue, a loss of capacity — and remind yourself: this is the pot cracking, not me.
  • Speak to someone aging or ill in your life as the eternal soul they are, not as the dwindling body in front of you.
  • Refuse to invest your identity in a single bodily form today by offering one act of sādhana — chanting, reading, or service — to the unchanging ātmā within.
Reflection Questions
  • When I picture my own body breaking down like a pot returning to clay, where does the panic still grip me — and what does that panic reveal about who I think I am?
  • Kaṁsa, an asura, grasped ātma-tattva clearly enough to speak this verse — where am I letting intellectual understanding of the soul's permanence sit untouched in my head while my daily fears still rehearse the body's annihilation?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Before your next exam or audition, write at the top of your page: 'My body's performance today is a pot; I am not the pot.' Work hard, but locate your worth somewhere the result cannot touch.
  • Spend one week off the platform where you most curate a 'body image' — Instagram, Snapchat, whichever. Notice how much of your identity was renting space in pixels of a face that is already changing.
  • When a friend group shifts and you are suddenly outside it, sit with this verse: bodies and bodily arrangements appear and disappear; the soul who loved and was loved is unchanged. Reach out to one person whose soul you actually valued, not the social configuration.
householder
  • When you next argue with your spouse or scold your child, pause and ask: am I addressing the soul in front of me, or the inconvenient pot-shaped behavior? Speak the next sentence to the soul.
  • Review one major financial decision this month — a purchase, an investment, a career move — through the lens of the six bodily changes. Are you building for a body that will dwindle, or for the soul's journey that continues? Adjust one line item accordingly.
  • With your children, name a possession that has broken or been outgrown — a toy, a phone, a piece of clothing. Use it as the doll in the verse: show them gently that they are not their things and not their bodies, without making it morbid.
senior
  • Make a list of attachments you are still gripping — a role, a title, a property, a grievance — and against each, write the verse's image: 'pot, doll, clay.' Loosen one grip this week, concretely, in a way someone else can see.
  • Identify one younger person — a grandchild, a junior colleague, a student — and over the next month, transmit not your achievements but your settled conviction that they are not the body. Let your steadiness in the face of your own aging be the teaching.
  • Each evening, before sleep, rehearse leaving the body as one rehearses a journey: offer the day to Kṛṣṇa, remember that asaṅgo hy ayaṁ puruṣaḥ, and let the breath go without clutching. Make death a practiced threshold, not an ambush.
Common Pitfalls
  • Using 'the soul is unchanging' as cold consolation that bypasses real grief, instead of letting it steady you while you still honor the loss of a particular bodily relationship.
  • Drifting into impersonalist language — 'I am the eternal Self, the body is just clay' — and forgetting that the unchanging ātmā belongs to Kṛṣṇa and is meant for His service, not for self-congratulatory detachment.
  • Mistaking Kaṁsa's accurate philosophy for sufficient realization: knowing ātma-tattva intellectually, like he did, while still living driven by ahaṅkāra and fear — verse-knowledge without surrender is asuric, not Vaiṣṇava.
Discussion Prompts
  • Kaṁsa, an asura, knew ātma-tattva better than most of us claim to. Share with your partner: where in your life have you mastered the theory of the soul's eternality but still flinch like a materialist when the body is threatened — your own or a loved one's? Be specific, not pious.
  • The verse uses pots and dolls — broken clay returning to clay. Disagree productively: is grief at a death therefore irrational, or does the personalist Gauḍīya line preserve a legitimate place for grief that the impersonalist reading erases? What's the difference between māyā-grief and proper feeling?
  • Kaṁsa used this knowledge to calm himself so he could continue persecuting Devakī. So 'knowing the soul is eternal' is clearly not enough. What must accompany ātma-jñāna for it to actually transform a life rather than rationalize cruelty? Push each other past the easy answer.
Role Play

Two students pair up. Student A has just received news that a close family member has been diagnosed with a terminal illness and has perhaps six months to live. Student A is shaken, oscillating between denial and panic. Student B is a slightly senior devotee whom Student A has come to for counsel. Student B must use the substance of SB 10.4.19 — bhuvi bhaumāni bhūtāni, the pot-and-doll example, the six bodily changes, asaṅgo hy ayaṁ puruṣaḥ — to console without sliding into either (a) cold philosophical lecture that bypasses real grief, or (b) sentimental material compassion that forgets the soul. After 7 minutes, swap roles and reverse the scenario: this time the diagnosis is Student B's own. Debrief together: where did the conversation go hollow? Where did it become real seva of the soul rather than seva of the body's comfort?

Objections & Responses
  • This is just a coping mechanism. Telling a grieving person 'the body is a pot, the soul is unchanged' is exactly the kind of bypass that lets religious people avoid the hard work of feeling loss. It's emotional anesthesia dressed up as philosophy.
    The Gauḍīya line does not anesthetize feeling — it relocates it. Caitanya Mahāprabhu Himself wept oceans for separation from Kṛṣṇa; the ācāryas are not stoics. What this verse refuses is the specific grief that mistakes the pot for the potter's child — mourning the clay as if the person were the clay. The soul is a person, not an abstraction, and our affection for a soul does not end when its current body breaks. So we grieve the separation, we honor the relationship, and we refuse the lie that annihilation has occurred. That is not bypass; it is more accurate feeling, not less feeling.
  • Modern science has no evidence for an unchanging soul that transmigrates. Pots breaking and reforming from clay is a poetic image, not a proof. Why should I accept this over a naturalist account where consciousness ends with the brain?
    The verse is not offered as empirical proof — it is offered as a working hypothesis verifiable in your own experience. Notice: the body you inhabited at five is gone, every cell replaced; yet you say 'I' as the same person. That continuity-through-bodily-change is the datum the verse is pointing at. Naturalism has no clean account of why that 'I' persists; it tends to declare the question malformed. The Gauḍīya answer is testable not in a lab but in sādhana — chant the holy name with attention, study under a bona fide guru, and see whether the hypothesis illuminates your experience or not. Kaṁsa accepted it on the strength of Vedic authority and his own reasoning; he is not asking you to do less than examine.
