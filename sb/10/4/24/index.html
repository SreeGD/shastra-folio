Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.24

मोचयामास निगडाद् विश्रब्ध: कन्यकागिरा । देवकीं वसुदेवं च दर्शयन्नात्मसौहृदम् ॥ २४ ॥

Important Words
  • mocayām āsa — he released / caused to be released
    Periphrastic causative perfect: 'he made-to-be-freed'. The grammar emphasizes agency — Kaṁsa himself orders the unshackling. The asura's tears, while they last, produce one good act.
  • nigaḍāt — from the shackles / iron fetters
    Ablative — 'from the iron'. Nigaḍa is a chain or shackle. The verse marks the physical liberation; the Bhāgavatam never confuses this with mokṣa, which is liberation from material existence itself.
  • viśrabdhaḥ — trusting / confident
    Past passive participle of vi-√śrambh — 'trusting fully'. Kaṁsa, taking the goddess's words at face value, trusts that the danger is past. The verse subtly notes: his trust is in the wrong direction; he trusts the voice (that another child will kill him) and not the message (that killing is futile).
  • kanyakā-girā — by the words of the girl-child (Yoga-māyā)
    Compound instrumental: 'by the girl-speech'. Kaṁsa acts on Yoga-māyā's words. The Bhāgavatam often shows the Lord's potencies guiding even asuric action — for a moment, for a purpose — within the larger arrangement.
  • ātma-sauhṛdam — his own friendly affection / kinship
    Compound — 'self-friendliness'. The verse uses sauhṛda — the very thing v.8 said was uprooted by sva-artha — to describe Kaṁsa's brief gesture. Friendship, once uprooted, can sprout again under the right moisture; but the root must be deeply replanted, not just sprinkled.
Fully believing in the words of the goddess Durgā, Kaṁsa exhibited his familial affection for Devakī and Vasudeva by immediately releasing them from their iron shackles.
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Fully believing in the words of the goddess Durgā, Kaṁsa exhibited his familial affection for Devakī and Vasudeva by immediately releasing them from their iron shackles.
Commentary

Believing Durga’s words, and showing friendship, Kamsa undid the iron shackles (nigada) of Vasudeva and Devaki.

Śrīdhara Svāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

At that time he released them from their shackles. He acted without crookedness (sauhṛdam), agreeing to his own words. Or he showed them friendship. He gave them some dear objects to please them.

Personal Guidance
Even a tyrant softens when fear lifts — but soft feelings born of relief are not bhakti; only surrender to Kṛṣṇa truly frees the heart.
Practical Applications
  • Notice today where your kindness toward family appears only after a threat passes — and ask whether that affection is sevā or just self-interest dressed up.
  • Refuse to mistake a moment of emotional release for genuine anahaṅkāra; check whether the false ego has merely shifted ground rather than dissolved.
  • Speak to one person you have wronged and offer a concrete act of service today, not a sentimental apology that costs you nothing.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my life am I, like Kaṁsa, performing acts of 'familial affection' or generosity only because a higher power has assured me my own position is safe — and what would change if that assurance were withdrawn?
  • Which shackles have I outwardly removed from someone I have wronged, while inwardly still holding the same intent that put them there in the first place?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Notice the next time a friend or classmate suddenly turns warm toward you after they've heard something that benefits them — a grade, a college admit, a breakup. Don't reject the warmth, but don't reorganize your week around it either. Watch what happens in 72 hours.
  • On social media this week, identify one account whose 'kindness' or 'positivity' content is actually relief-seeking — content that softens because the creator's fear got addressed, not because they grew. Unfollow or mute. Replace with one source rooted in actual sādhana.
  • When a parent or teacher releases you from a restriction after you've said the magic words they wanted to hear, ask yourself honestly: did I just play Kaṁsa's trick on them? Going forward, win freedom by becoming trustworthy, not by speaking the script that soothes their fear.
householder
  • In your marriage or with your children, watch for the moment you 'release them from the iron shackles' — drop a rule, end a punishment, soften a stance — because their words made you feel safe, not because anything actually changed. Decisions made from relief are usually wrong. Sleep on it.
  • At work, when a difficult employee or vendor suddenly becomes warm and accommodating, do not let your ahaṅkāra interpret this as your leadership winning them over. Ask what news they just received. Real sauhṛdam in business is rare; relief-driven warmth is constant.
  • With aging parents or in-laws, beware the family 'reconciliation' that arrives suddenly after years of cold distance — especially when inheritance, caregiving, or status is shifting. Honor the gesture with courtesy, but keep your discernment. Anahankarah here means neither bitterness nor naiveté.
senior
  • As you loosen your hold on family, business, and possessions, watch for false sauhṛdam from those positioning around you. Your genuine task is to give freely from vairāgya, not to be 'released' by flatterers. Decide what you will give and to whom — before anyone asks.
  • Reflect on the people you forgave in your sixties whom you had not forgiven in your forties. Some of those forgivenesses were real bhakti-grown softening; some were Kaṁsa-style relief because the threat had passed. With a trusted devotee, examine which were which — and what that teaches you about the quality of your remaining surrender.
  • When mentoring younger devotees or family members, do not perform Kaṁsa's gesture — releasing them from your standards because their words moved you in the moment. The greater seva is to hold the standard steadily, with warmth but without collapse, until they can stand inside it themselves.
Common Pitfalls
  • Mistaking Kaṁsa's release of Devakī and Vasudeva for genuine repentance — students read it as a moment of conversion, when it is in fact relief that his own death seems averted; surrender that is contingent on personal safety is not surrender.
  • Treating outward acts of kindness (loosening shackles, soft words, displays of sauhṛda) as proof of inner change, and so excusing in oneself a pattern of cruelty interrupted only when it becomes inconvenient.
  • Assuming 'belief in the words of a goddess' is automatically auspicious — Kaṁsa believed Durgā and was relieved, not transformed; faith placed in the wrong instruction, even from a divine source, can leave the ahaṅkāra fully intact and re-arm it for further harm.
Discussion Prompts
  • Kaṁsa releases Devakī and Vasudeva immediately upon hearing words that flatter his self-interest — he calls it 'familial affection.' Share with your partner: when have you mistaken a sudden softening of your hard line for genuine compassion, when in fact it was just relief that your fear had been addressed? Where is the line between real sauhṛdam and self-soothing?
  • The verse hinges on Kaṁsa's viśrabdhaḥ — fully believing the goddess's words. He believed the wrong thing for the right-sounding reason. Disagree productively: is it worse to surrender too quickly to a comforting voice, or to harden against every voice out of suspicion? Bring a real example from your week.
  • Devakī and Vasudeva accept release from a brother who has murdered their previous children. Discuss: is forgiveness here a virtue or a survival strategy under varṇāśrama duty? Where in your own life are you calling 'reconciliation' what is really exhaustion or fear?
Role Play

Two students play siblings. One has wronged the other badly over years (broken trust, taken money, lied about them). The 'wrong-doer' approaches with sudden warmth after receiving news that makes their own future look uncertain — they offer release from a long-running grievance, framing it as familial love. The 'wronged' sibling must decide in real time: accept the gesture as Vasudeva did, push back and name the pattern, or hold a middle position. After three minutes, swap roles. Then the class examines: where was real sauhṛdam, where was Kaṁsa's viśrabdhaḥ-driven self-relief, and how did each player read the difference?

Objections & Responses
  • This verse is morally confused. Kaṁsa is a child-murderer. Showing him as capable of 'familial affection' and having Devakī and Vasudeva passively accept his release sanitizes domestic violence. Why are we drawing spiritual lessons from an abuser's mood-swing?
    The Bhāgavata is not endorsing Kaṁsa's affection — it is exposing it. The very next verses show his ministers undoing this softness within hours. Vyāsa is showing us, with surgical precision, what counterfeit sauhṛdam looks like: a tyrant whose ahaṅkāra has been temporarily soothed, mistaking relief for love. Devakī and Vasudeva, for their part, are not naive — they are exemplars of Anahankarah, accepting what Kṛṣṇa arranges through any instrument, even a murderous brother, while keeping their inner allegiance intact. The verse trains us to see through the performance of affection, not to imitate the performer.
  • The 'goddess Durgā' speaking and Kaṁsa believing her — this is mythological furniture. Modern readers cannot take instruction from a story whose mechanism is a supernatural voice from a child who turns into a deity. What is the practical takeaway for someone who doesn't believe any of this happened?
    Set aside the question of historicity for a moment and look at the structure: a man hears exactly the words his ahaṅkāra wants to hear, and immediately his behavior softens — not because he has changed, but because his fear has been redirected. That mechanism is observable in any meeting room, any family argument, any political speech this week. The Gauḍīya teacher's point is not 'believe in the goddess' but 'notice how cheaply your hardness dissolves when flattered, and how quickly it returns when threatened.' If the supernatural framing helps you see this in yourself, use it. If it doesn't, the diagnostic still stands.
Analogy

A jailer, suddenly afraid of his own jail, opens every cell at once — but he has not yet taken off his uniform. Kaṁsa's release of Devakī and Vasudeva is real in body, but not yet in heart. The shackles of iron come off; the shackles of asuric saṅga are still on. The next morning will show which set was the true bondage.

Stories
Yaśodā and the binding of Kṛṣṇa

When Mother Yaśodā tried to bind little Kṛṣṇa to the grinding mortar with a rope, the rope was always two fingers short, no matter how many ropes she joined. Only when she had exhausted her own strength and Kṛṣṇa Himself consented, did He allow the binding — by the devotee's love.

Kaṁsa's act here is the opposite: he removes shackles he had wrongly put on a devotee. The Bhāgavatam pairs these two binding-images across the canto. Material chains can only hold those Kṛṣṇa has not protected; even when the demon adds them, Bhagavān removes them when His līlā demands. The bondage of love that follows in Vraja is a different kind altogether.
