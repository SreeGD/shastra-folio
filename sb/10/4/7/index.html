Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.7

Important Words
  • upaguhya — embracing closely
    From upa-√guh — to clasp, to hide in the embrace. Devakī's body becomes the last shelter she can give. The same root is used for Mother Yaśodā embracing Kṛṣṇa; here it is the embrace that cannot hold.
  • dīna-dīna-vat — like one most fallen / piteous
    Reduplication for intensity — 'most piteously of the pitiable.' Compare Caitanya's daśama-skandha mood in the Śikṣāṣṭaka. The devotee's tears are not weakness in the eyes of the Lord; they are the most fragrant offering.
  • yācitaḥ — being begged
    Passive participle — Kaṁsa is the one being entreated, yet he refuses. The cruel heart hardens precisely against the most piteous request; the soft heart melts at the same.
  • vinirbhartsya — harshly chastising / rebuking
    Strong verb of rebuke. Kaṁsa not only refuses but scolds the grieving mother. The asuric mind, when refusing the saint, also abuses her — a pattern Prahlāda, the gopas of Vraja, and the Six Gosvāmīs all met.
  • khalaḥ — the cruel one / wretch
    Khala in śāstra: one whose heart is fixed in envy. The opposite of sādhu. The Bhāgavatam uses this word to label Kaṁsa's act morally, lest the reader sentimentalize him.
Śukadeva Gosvāmī continued: Piteously embracing her daughter and crying, Devakī begged Kaṁsa for the child, but he was so cruel that he chastised her and forcibly snatched the child from her hands.
Purport

Although Devakī was crying like a very poor woman, actually she was not poor, and therefore the word used here is dīnavat. She had already given birth to Kṛṣṇa. Therefore, who could have been richer than she? Even the demigods had come to offer prayers to Devakī, but she played the part of a poor, piteously afflicted woman because she wanted to save the daughter of Yaśodā.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Śukadeva Gosvāmī continued: Piteously embracing her daughter and crying, Devakī begged Kaṁsa for the child, but he was so cruel that he chastised her and forcibly snatched the child from her hands.
Commentary

Evam atma jam means "just as if the child were her own daughter" (since she was aware that it was not her daughter.) She is described as being "like" the most miserable person (dina dinavat) instead of being the most miserable, because the child was not actually hers. Threatening Devaki, he grabbed the child from her hands.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

The child is called her own (ātmā-jām) because out of close friendship with Yaśodā she considered herself non-different from her. She was like a very miserable person (dīnā-dīnavat). According to karmadhārayavat-uttara-padeṣvityadikārasthena prakāre guṇavacanasya (Pāṇini 8.11) repetition of dīna means “very miserable.” She cried miserably, even though she knew the child was not hers. Khalaḥ means cruel or low according to Viśva-kośa.

Śrīdhara Svāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

The child is called her own (ātmā-jām) because she had exchanged this child with hers or because out of close friendship with Yaśodā she considered herself non-different from her. She wept like a person in the most miserable position (dīna-dīnavat) because of the death of her children and because of the death of Yaśodā’s daughter.

Personal Guidance
Devakī's tears were not weakness but karuṇā in disguise — the devotee's heart bears the part of the poor to shelter another soul.
Practical Applications
  • Notice today where you confuse worldly helplessness with spiritual poverty — Devakī wept yet possessed Kṛṣṇa Himself.
  • Speak up for one vulnerable soul in your circle today, even if pleading 'dīnavat' costs you your dignity before the cruel.
  • Refuse to mistake another's harshness as the final word — keep serving, as Devakī did, knowing Kṛṣṇa orchestrates the outcome.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my own life am I, like Kaṁsa, snatching from another's hands what I am terrified of losing — and calling that decisiveness?
  • Where am I, like Devakī, pleading dīnavat for an outcome Kṛṣṇa has already arranged, forgetting that the One I love is already safe and at work behind the scene I am crying over?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • When a friend is being bullied online, don't just post a generic 'be kind' story — quietly reach out to the target by DM the way Devakī reached for the child, offering real presence, not performative allyship.
  • Notice the next time you 'play small' in a group chat or classroom to protect someone weaker (a quieter friend, a younger sibling) from being singled out — that strategic dīnavat-style humility, used for another's safety, is karuṇā in action, not weakness.
  • When a peer-pressure 'Kaṁsa moment' arrives — someone demanding you give up something or someone you're meant to protect (a friend's secret, your study time, your principles) — practice pleading once, then standing firm; tears are allowed, surrender of the soul's duty is not.
householder
  • In family decisions where you carry more spiritual or financial 'wealth' than others realize, deliberately set it aside and meet your spouse or in-laws as equals — Devakī, who had given birth to Kṛṣṇa, still cried like a poor woman; your seniority is not a weapon.
  • When your child is being mistreated — at school, by a relative, in a custody or healthcare setting — distinguish material compassion (just spare them discomfort) from real compassion (protect their soul's trajectory) before you intervene; sometimes the right move is fierce, sometimes it is Devakī's tears.
  • Identify one 'daughter of Yaśodā' in your life this week — a vulnerable person not technically your responsibility (a neighbor's child, a junior at work, an aging parent of a friend) — and intercede for them with the same intensity you'd bring for your own.
senior
  • Use the wealth of your years the way Devakī used hers — hidden. Mentor without announcing yourself as mentor; pray for your grandchildren without lecturing them; let your spiritual riches travel underground.
  • Mortality will come like Kaṁsa's hand — uninvited, forcible, indifferent to your tears. Begin now to release the 'children' you most cling to (reputation, possessions, even certain relationships) into Kṛṣṇa's hands before they are snatched, so the surrender is yours, not extracted.
  • When you weep at a funeral, an illness, or a family rupture, do not be ashamed of the dīnavat tears — but inwardly remember you are not poor; you have heard of Kṛṣṇa, you have taken shelter, the demigods of your sādhana stand behind you. Cry for the situation, not for your soul.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading Devakī's tears as ordinary maternal grief and missing that, having already given birth to Kṛṣṇa, she is the richest of mothers — playing the part of a poor woman precisely to protect Yaśodā's daughter; reducing this to 'a sad mom' flattens the whole līlā.
  • Identifying with Kaṁsa's cruelty only abstractly ('demons are bad') instead of recognizing the same khalaḥ-impulse in oneself: the moment one wrenches a person, possession, or result from another's grasp out of fear, envy, or self-preservation.
  • Concluding that pleading and tears are spiritually weak. Devakī's dīnavat posture is not impersonalist resignation but a deliberate, surrendered move within Kṛṣṇa's plan — mistaking strategic humility for helplessness leads students to either suppress legitimate grief or perform a brittle 'detached' stoicism.
Discussion Prompts
  • Devakī performs grief — she is not actually a 'poor woman,' yet she cries 'like a very poor woman' to save Yaśodā's daughter. Is this a holy deception, a strategic karuṇā, or does it cross into dishonesty? Where in your own life have you had to 'play poor' to protect someone, and how do you tell that apart from manipulation?
  • Kaṁsa is moved by Devakī's tears for one moment, then snatches the child anyway. Discuss: when is emotional appeal the right tool, and when is it useless against a hardened heart? Share a time you kept pleading when you should have switched tactics — or stopped pleading too early.
  • The purport says even the demigods came to offer prayers to Devakī — she lacks nothing. Yet she still suffers visibly. Does a devotee's external suffering contradict their inner richness, or is the willingness to suffer outwardly part of the richness itself? Push each other on this — don't let your partner settle for 'both are true.'
Role Play

Two students. Student A is a hospice volunteer sitting with the family of a dying patient; the family is begging A to lie to the patient and say 'you'll be fine, you're going home soon' to spare him distress. Student B is the volunteer's supervisor, who insists on truthful presence. A has thirty seconds to decide. Run the scene twice — once where A lies out of material compassion (sparing the body's fear), once where A speaks the truth as real compassion (serving the soul's readiness). Afterward, debrief: which was closer to Devakī's tears for the daughter of Yaśodā? Where does protective deception in service of a higher good differ from soft lies that abandon the soul?

Objections & Responses
  • Devakī is essentially deceiving Kaṁsa — performing helplessness she does not feel. Isn't the Gauḍīya tradition supposed to be about truthfulness (satya)? How is this not just a holy lie dressed up as devotion?
    Satya in our line is never bare factual disclosure detached from dharma — it is speech that serves the truth of Kṛṣṇa's mission. Devakī is not lying about reality; she is concealing a fact from a demon who would use that fact to commit murder. The deeper truth — that Kṛṣṇa is protected, that this child is Yogamāyā, that the demigods stand behind her — is exactly what her tears are guarding. A satya that hands a sword to a killer is not satya at all. Real truthfulness is loyal to Kṛṣṇa first, to factual disclosure second.
  • A modern reader sees a mother weeping and a tyrant snatching a baby — this is just a horror story. Why dress up child-violence in spiritual language about dīnavat and Yogamāyā? Doesn't that aestheticize trauma?
    We are not dressing up the horror — we are refusing to stop at the horror. Kaṁsa's cruelty is real and Vyāsadeva does not soften it. But if we read only the surface, we miss that Kṛṣṇa has already escaped, that this 'child' is Yogamāyā who will laugh in Kaṁsa's face, that Devakī's tears are themselves an act of protective love for another mother's daughter. The Gauḍīya teacher does not anaesthetize you to the tyrant's violence; he insists you also see the Lord's arrangement running underneath it. Both are true at once, and refusing either one is the actual evasion.
Analogy

A wolf snatches a lamb from the shepherd's own arms — that is Kaṁsa with the infant girl. But the lamb in his grip is no ordinary creature; she is Yoga-māyā, who will slip his hands as easily as moonlight slips through fingers. The devotee's tears appear futile to the cruel eye, but every tear is recorded by Bhagavān.

Stories
Vidura silenced by Dhṛtarāṣṭra

Throughout the Mahābhārata, Vidura warned the blind king Dhṛtarāṣṭra against the cruelty of his sons, and was again and again chastised, dismissed, and at one point insulted out of the court. Like Devakī's pleas, Vidura's words were not heeded by the man who held the power.

Yet the Bhāgavatam treats Vidura's failed pleas as sacred; they were never wasted, because Kṛṣṇa heard them. Devakī's tears here, similarly, do not save the child by their own force — Yoga-māyā does that — but they enter the eternal record of the devotee's heart. The cruel snatching of the child is, on one plane, an offense; on another, the līlā arranged for Bhagavān's purposes.
