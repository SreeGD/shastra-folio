Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.30

आकर्ण्य भर्तुर्गदितं तमूचुर्देवशत्रव: । देवान् प्रति कृतामर्षा दैतेया नातिकोविदा: ॥ ३० ॥

After hearing their master’s statement, the envious asuras, who were enemies of the demigods and were not very expert in their dealings, advised Kaṁsa as follows.
Purport

There are two different types of men — the asuras and the suras. dvau bhūta-sargau loke ’smin daiva āsura eva ca viṣṇu-bhaktaḥ smṛto daiva āsuras tad-viparyayaḥ ( Padma Purāṇa ) Those who are devotees of Lord Viṣṇu, Kṛṣṇa, are suras, or devas, whereas those who are opposed to the devotees are called asuras. Devotees are expert in all transactions ( yasyāsti bhaktir bhagavaty akiñcanā sarvair guṇais tatra samāsate surāḥ ). Therefore they are called kovida, which means “expert.” Asuras, however, although superficially showing expertise in passionate activities, are actually all fools. They are neither sober nor expert. Whatever they do is imperfect. Moghāśā mogha-karmāṇaḥ. According to this description of the asuras given in Bhagavad-gītā (9.12) , whatever they do will ultimately be baffled. It was such persons who advised Kaṁsa because they were his chief friends and ministers.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
After hearing their master's statement, the envious asuras, who were enemies of the demigods and were not very expert in their dealings, advised Kaṁsa as follows.
Commentary

Nati kovidah means ignorant. Ati refers to qualification. The demons, without any qualification for knowledge, hearing Kamsa’s words, and with anger towards the devatas, then began to speak.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

By their nature they were inimical to the devatās. Moreover they were angry with the devatās (kṛtāmarṣāḥ).

Concepts
Jiva
Personal Guidance
Counsel from those envious of devotees is never kovida — true expertise belongs to Kṛṣṇa's servants; choose your advisors accordingly.
Practical Applications
  • Audit who you turn to for counsel today, and refuse advice from anyone hostile to bhakti, however clever they sound.
  • Before acting on a strong suggestion, ask: does this come from a sura (Viṣṇu-bhakta) or an asura (tad-viparyaya)?
  • Notice when envy — yours or another's — is dressed up as strategic 'expertise,' and step back from it before it shapes your next move.
Reflection Questions
  • Whose counsel do I habitually seek when I am already inflamed — counselors who will sharpen my envy toward 'enemies,' or those whose vision is steadied in Kṛṣṇa?
  • Where in my life am I mistaking asuric cleverness — quick, passionate, baffled at the end (mogha-karmāṇaḥ) — for genuine kovida-expertise rooted in bhakti?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Audit your group chat from the past week: when you were anxious about an exam, a crush, a falling-out — who did you message first? Were they speaking to your soul or to your fear? Notice the pattern; you are choosing your asura advisors daily.
  • Identify one 'expert' voice in your feed (an influencer, a 'productivity guru', a cynical senior) whose advice consistently sounds sharp but leaves you emptier. Mute them for two weeks. Replace that slot with a single Prabhupāda lecture or one verse a day.
  • Before your next big decision — internship, breakup, choice of major — write down which two friends you would naturally consult. Then write down: which of them would tell me something I do not want to hear, for the sake of my eternal welfare? If neither would, that is the real finding, and the real work.
householder
  • Your inner circle of advisors — spouse aside, the three or four people you actually take big decisions with — list them tonight. For each, ask honestly: do they orient me toward Kṛṣṇa, or toward the next promotion, the bigger house, the safer image? You may need to quietly demote some voices and elevate others.
  • When raising children, distinguish material compassion (shielding them from every discomfort, optimizing every outcome) from real compassion (forming their character so they can stand as suras in an asuric age). Choose one decision this month — a school choice, a phone policy, a family ritual — where you deliberately favor the second over the first.
  • Notice how often household 'strategy talks' about money, in-laws, or career moves drift into the exact register of Kaṁsa's council — fear-driven, defensive, calculating against perceived enemies. Once a week, open such a conversation by chanting first, or by reading one verse aloud. Watch what changes in the room.
senior
  • Look back at the major counsel you gave your children and juniors over the last forty years. Where were you a kovida advisor — speaking to the soul — and where were you, honestly, an asuric one, advising only worldly safety? Where you can, repair it now: write the letter, make the call, name the regret.
  • Younger people will increasingly come to you as the 'wise elder.' Refuse the role of merely worldly counselor. Train yourself, before answering any serious question, to first silently ask: what would Prabhupāda say to this person? Then speak. Your remaining decades are for being a sura advisor in a world starving for one.
  • Begin loosening attachments not as resignation but as preparation: each month, give away one possession, forgive one old grievance, and offer one long-held plan to Kṛṣṇa. Aging is the body's own teaching that nāti-kovida strategies — of accumulation, control, image — are running out of runway. Let that teaching land.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading this verse as being about 'those bad people back then' and missing that the asura-mentality is a tendency I host whenever I treat devotees as obstacles to my plans.
  • Equating worldly competence and shrewdness with kovida; the verse warns that activity in rajas/tamas, however impressive, is ultimately mogha — fruitless — without bhakti.
  • Surrounding oneself with ministers who only confirm one's existing kāma and krodha (as Kaṁsa did) and calling that 'getting good advice,' instead of seeking guru and sādhu-saṅga that may correct rather than flatter.
Discussion Prompts
  • The verse calls Kaṁsa's advisors nāti-kovidāḥ — 'not very expert' — even though they were powerful, well-placed ministers. Share with your partner: where in your own life have you mistaken worldly competence (career success, social fluency, intellectual sharpness) for actual kovida-ness? Push back if your partner's example sounds like humble-bragging.
  • Prabhupāda divides humanity sharply into two camps: viṣṇu-bhaktas (suras) and tad-viparyayāḥ (asuras). Many modern readers find this binary uncomfortable. In your group, argue both sides — first defend the binary as Prabhupāda presents it, then voice the strongest objection to it. Where does the discomfort actually come from?
  • Kaṁsa, frightened by a divine voice, turned to his asura friends for counsel — and they gave him exactly the advice that sealed his destruction. Discuss a specific time you sought counsel from people who only confirmed what your lower nature already wanted to hear. What would have changed if you had gone to a devotee instead? Be concrete; no abstractions.
Role Play

Two students play the scene: one is 'Kaṁsa' — a young professional who has just received unsettling news (a medical scare, a relationship warning, a career shock). The other plays an 'asura advisor' — a close friend who is loyal, articulate, and worldly-wise, but has no spiritual orientation. The advisor must give counsel that sounds reasonable, caring, even sophisticated, yet steers Kaṁsa deeper into fear-driven, self-protective action. After three minutes, swap: the same friend now plays a sura advisor — a devotee — responding to the same news. The class then dissects: what changed in tone, in framing, in the proposed action? Where did the asura advice feel seductive? Where did the sura advice feel uncomfortable but true?

Objections & Responses
  • Calling people 'asuras' just because they don't share your theology is exactly the kind of in-group/out-group thinking that fuels religious violence. Isn't this verse essentially dehumanizing non-devotees?
    The śāstra is not drawing a tribal line between Hindus and others, or between our sampradāya and theirs. The line is functional, not ethnic: a sura is one whose consciousness is oriented toward Viṣṇu and the welfare of all souls; an asura is one whose consciousness is oriented against that welfare — including, often, against his own. Kaṁsa was born in a pious dynasty; his ministers were learned. The label tracks orientation of the heart, not birth or affiliation. And critically, the category is permeable — Vālmīki, Jagāi and Mādhāi, countless others crossed over. Far from dehumanizing, the teaching insists every being can become a sura the moment surrender begins. What it refuses to do is flatter the asuric mentality by pretending it leads anywhere good.
  • The purport says asuras are 'actually all fools' even when they look expert. But history is full of brilliant non-devotees — scientists, artists, statesmen — who built civilizations. Calling them fools sounds like sour grapes from a tradition that lost its political power.
    Prabhupāda is not denying the asura's technical brilliance — Kaṁsa's ministers were genuinely capable men, and Rāvaṇa was a Vedic scholar. The word kovida in śāstra means expert in the thing that actually matters: knowing who you are, where you are going, and Whom you must please. By that measure, a man who builds a magnificent life and then dies confused about its purpose is, however reluctantly we say it, not expert. The Gītā's verdict moghāśā mogha-karmāṇaḥ — 'their hopes baffled, their works baffled' — is not a sneer; it is a diagnosis. Modern civilization's own crisis of meaning, despite extraordinary technical achievement, is something the asuric framework predicts exactly. Real expertise integrates the eternal with the temporal. Anything less is, eventually, foolish — no matter how dazzling along the way.
