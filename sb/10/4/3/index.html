Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.4.3

स तल्पात् तूर्णमुत्थाय कालोऽयमिति विह्वल: । सूतीगृहमगात् तूर्णं प्रस्खलन् मुक्तमूर्धज: ॥ ३ ॥

Kaṁsa immediately got up from bed, thinking, “Here is Kāla, the supreme time factor, which has taken birth to kill me!” Thus overwhelmed, Kaṁsa, his hair scattered on his head, at once approached the place where the child had been born.
Purport

The word kālaḥ is significant. Although the child was born to kill Kaṁsa, Kaṁsa thought that this was the proper time to kill the child so that he himself would be saved. Kāla is actually another name of the Supreme Personality of Godhead when He appears only for the purpose of killing. When Arjuna inquired from Kṛṣṇa in His universal form, “Who are You?” the Lord presented Himself as kāla, death personified to kill. By nature’s law, when there is an unwanted increase in population, kāla appears, and by some arrangement of the Supreme Personality of Godhead, people are killed wholesale in different ways, by war, pestilence, famine and so on. At that time, even atheistic political leaders go to a church, mosque or temple for protection by God or gods and submissively say, “God willing.” Before that, they pay no attention to God, not caring to know God or His will, but when kāla appears, they say “God willing.” Death is but another feature of the supreme kāla, the Supreme Personality of Godhead. At the time of death, the atheist must submit to this supreme kāla, and then the Supreme Personality of Godhead takes away all his possessions ( mṛtyuḥ sarva-haraś cāham ) and forces him to accept another body ( tathā dehāntara-prāptiḥ ). This the atheists do not know, and if they do know, they neglect it so that they may go on with their normal life. The Kṛṣṇa consciousness movement is trying to teach them that although for a few years one may act as a great protector or great watchman, with the appearance of kāla, death, one must take another body by the laws of nature. Not knowing this, they unnecessarily waste their time in their occupation as watchdogs and do not try to get the mercy of the Supreme Personality of Godhead. As it is clearly said, aprāpya māṁ nivartante mṛtyu-saṁsāra-vartmani: without Kṛṣṇa consciousness, one is condemned to continue wandering in birth and death, not knowing what will happen in one’s next birth.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Kaṁsa immediately got up from bed, thinking, "Here is Kāla, the supreme time factor, which has taken birth to kill me!" Thus overwhelmed, Kaṁsa, his hair scattered on his head, at once approached the place where the child had been born.
Commentary

The time has come (kalah ayam) for killing the child. Or the sentence can mean "Out of fear, Kamsa was thinking, that the birth of the child was his own death (kala)."

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

The time has come to kill the child. Or death has now come. He was filled with fear, falling here and there as he walked. Instead of tūrṇam sometimes śīghram is seen.

Concepts
JivaRasa
Personal Guidance
Kāla — death personified — comes for everyone; the wise do not wait for that hour to remember Kṛṣṇa, they surrender to Him now.
Practical Applications
  • Name aloud one thing you treat as permanent — body, position, possession — and offer it to Kṛṣṇa today as already belonging to kāla.
  • Refuse the 'God willing' reflex of crisis-only prayer; chant Hare Kṛṣṇa once before each meal as the steady remembrance Kaṁsa never had.
  • Notice when fear, like Kaṁsa's, drives you to scheme against an outcome, and replace one such scheme with a concrete act of śaraṇāgati — sit, breathe, and say, 'Kṛṣṇa, I am Yours.'
Reflection Questions
  • Like Kaṁsa, where am I treating the arrival of kāla — aging, illness, a deadline I cannot outrun — as an enemy to be eliminated rather than as Kṛṣṇa Himself approaching me, and what would change if I recognized the visitor?
  • What 'watchdog' role have I been clinging to — protector of my career, my family's status, my body — that I quietly believe will save me from death, and what is that vigilance costing my actual sādhana?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • When exam results, a rejection from a friend group, or a bad grade lands like a small kāla — a signal something must shift — pause before you reach for distraction (scroll, vent, blame). Ask: 'What is this telling me about what I was actually building my identity on?'
  • Pick one performance metric you are quietly worshipping (followers, rank, a crush's attention) and for one week treat it the way the dhīra would treat a body — real but not ultimate. Do the work, but stop checking the count obsessively.
  • Notice the moment you say 'God willing' or 'fingers crossed' before a result comes out. That nervous gesture is a real prayer leaking through. Replace it once a day with one round of japa or one śloka offered to Kṛṣṇa — a deliberate version of the same impulse.
householder
  • List the three biggest 'watchdog' duties consuming your week — protecting income, status, the children's future. For each, write one sentence on how it serves the soul of the people involved, not just their bodies. Where the sentence is hard to write, the role needs reframing, not abandoning.
  • When a financial scare, health scare, or career scare arrives this year, treat it as kāla knocking — not a problem to outrun, but a question to answer. Before reacting, take the family to the altar and read one verse together; let the decision come from that center, not from panic.
  • Build one explicit kāla-acknowledgment into family life: a monthly conversation with your spouse and older children about death, wills, what you actually believe happens, and what spiritual inheritance you are leaving. Do it as a normal household practice, not a morbid one.
senior
  • Inventory what you are still 'guarding' — property, reputation, control of grown children's choices. Choose one item this month and consciously loosen the grip: hand over the decision, give away the asset, stop offering the opinion. Feel the vairāgya as relief, not loss.
  • Kāla is no longer abstract at this stage. Establish a daily 'leaving rehearsal' — ten minutes of chanting and remembering Kṛṣṇa with the explicit thought, 'If today were the last, this is the consciousness I want.' Let this reshape how you spend the remaining hours.
  • Identify two younger people — a grandchild, a neighbor's kid, a junior colleague still working — and become for them the elder you wish you had had: someone who speaks plainly about the soul, varṇāśrama, and surrender, without preaching. Mentor by being unmistakably oriented toward Kṛṣṇa, not toward holding on.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading this verse as merely a story about a wicked king 'over there' and missing that the Kaṁsa-reflex — scrambling out of bed to fight kāla — is the default posture of every conditioned soul, including the student.
  • Mistaking the lesson as fatalism ('death is coming, so why act?') instead of its real point: act in Kṛṣṇa consciousness now, because kāla strips away every other shelter at the moment of death.
  • Reducing kāla to an impersonal 'time' or 'natural law' and forgetting Prabhupāda's emphasis that kāla is a personal feature of Kṛṣṇa Himself — the same Person to whom we are invited to surrender before He arrives in His devouring form.
Discussion Prompts
  • Kaṁsa heard the prophecy and reacted with violent self-preservation. Share with your partner: when have you 'heard a kāla-warning' in your own life — a clear signal that something must change — and instead of surrendering, doubled down on protecting your position? Be specific. Push back on each other if the example sounds too neat.
  • The purport says atheistic leaders only say 'God willing' when kāla appears. Is there a meaningful difference between someone who turns to Kṛṣṇa under threat of death and someone who turns to Him in health and prosperity? Argue both sides — does fear-driven surrender count, or is it just spiritual insurance?
  • Prabhupāda calls the modern occupation 'watchdog' work — protecting bodies and possessions while ignoring the soul's trajectory. Name one role you currently play that, honestly examined, is watchdog work. Disagree with each other: is it possible to do watchdog work as bhakti, or does the role itself need to be abandoned?
Role Play

A teacher pairs students. One plays a 55-year-old corporate executive who has just received a serious cancer diagnosis and, for the first time, is asking real questions about death; the other plays a Gauḍīya friend who has known them for twenty years but has never been invited to discuss spiritual matters. The executive opens with: 'I've spent thirty years building this company. I always thought I had time. What do I do now?' The friend must respond without leaning on platitudes, without overwhelming them with siddhānta, and without flinching from the kāla reality of the situation. After five minutes, swap roles. Debrief: where did the friend slip into watchdog-comforting versus genuine compassion for the soul?

Objections & Responses
  • This whole framing is fear-based religion. You're saying people only turn to God when death scares them — that's manipulation, not spirituality. A mature path shouldn't need the threat of kāla to motivate surrender.
    The verse and purport don't recommend fear as the doorway — they observe it as the doorway most conditioned souls actually use. Kṛṣṇa Himself in BG 7.16 names the ārta, the distressed, as one of the four kinds who approach Him, and calls all four sukṛtinaḥ — pious. Fear of kāla is not the highest motivation, but it is a real one, and the Gauḍīya line meets you where you are. The point is not to manufacture fear; it is to stop pretending you are exempt from death so that genuine inquiry can begin. From that honest start, the relationship matures into love. Calling this manipulation assumes there is some other, painless route to taking mortality seriously — and the watchdog life Prabhupāda describes is precisely the proof that there isn't.
  • Kaṁsa is a tyrant who tries to murder a baby. Using his story to teach me about my own life feels like a stretch — I'm not a demon-king, and equating my ordinary attachments with his violence is melodramatic.
    The śāstra holds Kaṁsa up not because you resemble him in scale but because he displays, in extreme form, what every ahaṅkāra-bound soul does in subtle form: hearing a signal that the false self must die, and reaching instead for a weapon to preserve it. Your weapon is probably not a sword — it's the calendar that keeps you too busy to chant, the rationalization that protects a comfortable habit, the mood that drives off anyone who threatens your self-image. The verse magnifies the pattern so you can recognize the smaller version in yourself. If the comparison feels melodramatic, sit with it longer; the discomfort is often the recognition arriving.
