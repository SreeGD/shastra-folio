Important Words
  • ambuja-īkṣaṇam — lotus-eyed
    Ambuja (lotus, 'water-born') + īkṣaṇa (eye). The first feature Vasudeva sees — Kṛṣṇa's eyes. In rasa-śāstra the kamala-nayana is the gateway to His sweetness; the Gosvāmīs return to this epithet again and again because vision of those eyes is the doorway to bhāva.
  • catuḥ-bhujam — four-armed
    Catuḥ (four) + bhuja (arm). The Vaikuṇṭha-Viṣṇu form. Doctrinally crucial: Kṛṣṇa is svayaṁ bhagavān and only briefly displays the catuḥ-bhuja Nārāyaṇa form to authenticate Himself, then withdraws it. The Gauḍīya conclusion: Kṛṣṇa is the source of all viṣṇu-tattva, not the reverse.
  • śaṅkha-gadā-ādi — conch, club, and the rest
    Compound naming the cardinal Viṣṇu emblems. Each weapon has theological significance: śaṅkha (the sound of Veda), cakra (time and the cutting of māyā), gadā (the crushing of pride), padma (the lotus heart that receives Him). Vasudeva is seeing the full insignia of Vaikuṇṭha.
  • śrīvatsa-lakṣmam — marked with the Śrīvatsa
    The curl of hair on Viṣṇu's chest where Lakṣmī resides eternally. In Gauḍīya theology this mark is permanent on Viṣṇu but is one of the marks distinguishing Viṣṇu-tattva from Kṛṣṇa-svarūpa proper — Kṛṣṇa later as the cowherd boy will not display Śrīvatsa. Here He shows it to identify Himself to Vasudeva.
  • pīta-ambaram — wearing yellow garments
    Pīta (yellow) + ambara (cloth). The signature pītāmbara is shared by Kṛṣṇa and Nārāyaṇa. In Gauḍīya iconography this yellow cloth is itself an object of meditation — caitanya-caritāmṛta dwells on its lightning-against-cloud effect against Kṛṣṇa's dark form.
Vasudeva then saw the newborn child, who had very wonderful lotuslike eyes and who bore in His four hands the four weapons śaṅkha, cakra, gadā and padma. On His chest was the mark of Śrīvatsa and on His neck the brilliant Kaustubha gem. Dressed in yellow, His body blackish like a dense cloud, His scattered hair fully grown, and His helmet and earrings sparkling uncommonly with the valuable gem Vaidūrya, the child, decorated with a brilliant belt, armlets, bangles and other ornaments, appeared very wonderful.
Purport

To support the word adbhutam, meaning “wonderful,” the decorations and opulences of the newborn child are fully described. As confirmed in the Brahma-saṁhitā (5.30) , barhāvataṁsam asitāmbuda-sundarāṅgam: the hue of the Lord’s beautiful form resembles the blackish color of dense clouds ( asita means “blackish,” and ambuda means “cloud”). It is clear from the word catur-bhujam that Kṛṣṇa first appeared with four hands, as Lord Viṣṇu. No ordinary child in human society has ever been born with four hands. And when is a child born with fully grown hair? The descent of the Lord, therefore, is completely distinct from the birth of an ordinary child. The Vaidūrya gem, which sometimes appears bluish, sometimes yellow and sometimes red, is available in Vaikuṇṭhaloka. The Lord’s helmet and earrings were decorated with this particular gem.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Vasudeva then saw the newborn child, who had very wonderful lotuslike eyes and who bore in His four hands the four weapons śaṅkha, cakra, gadā and padma. On His chest was the mark of Śrīvatsa and on His neck the brilliant Kaustubha gem. Dressed in yellow, His body blackish like a dense cloud, His scattered hair fully grown, and His helmet and earrings sparkling uncommonly with the valuable gem Vaidūrya, the child, decorated with a brilliant belt, armlets, bangles and other ornaments, appeared very wonderful.
Commentary

Verses nine and ten describe the astonishing child that Vasudeva saw, listing the features of astonishment-- his lotus eyes, four arms holding conch and club etc. Vaidurya refers to a jewel which shines blue, yellow and red. The Lord wore a crown of vaidurya, of triangular shape.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

The two verses are one sentence. He had the small, tender form of a baby. The weapons quickly appeared (ut) or were most excellent in order to attack Kaṁsa and others. The specific arrangement is mentioned in Gautamīya-tantra and Bṛhad-gautamīya-tantra. dakṣyordhve smarec cakraṁ gadāṁ ca tad adaḥ kare vāmasyordhva śārṅga-dhanuḥ śaṅkhaṁ ca tad adaḥ smaret In his right upper hand is the cakra and in his right lower hand is the club. In his left upper hand is his bow and in his lower left hand was the conch. However text 30 mentions the lotus in place of the bow. The bow is indicated for a particular type of worship. However this is not impossible for the Lord since at all times he can hold both, without contradiction. His extraordinary beauty is indicated by the good fortune of his complexion like a dense rain cloud. The vaidūrya or vālavāyaja is a gem which glows blue, yellow and red. It shone most prominently among all the jewels that he wore. His many locks of hair were mixed with the effulgence of the vaidūrya in his crown from above and the effulgence of his earrings below. Or he had many locks of hair embraced by the crown and earrings so that the locks appeared to be a great vaidūrya gem, shining with many colors. The kirīṭa is triangular in shape, like a row of leaves. The mukuṭa covers all the hair. He shone greatly because of the effulgence of his belt, armlets and bracelets. Or he shone independently of the ornaments. He was decorated with the splendor of his innate beauty. This is also mentioned in the next verse. Thus Vasudeva saw him even though it was very dark. In Gautamīya-tantra Kṛṣṇa is depicted riding on Garuḍa and surrounded by his eight principal queens. That is not mentioned here since his parents never saw that.

Personal Guidance
Kṛṣṇa descends in full Viṣṇu opulence — four-armed, weaponed, jewelled — to remind us His birth is no ordinary event but the Lord's own personal advent.
Practical Applications
  • Before chanting today, picture the newborn Kṛṣṇa exactly as Vasudeva saw Him — śaṅkha, cakra, gadā, padma, Śrīvatsa, Kaustubha — and let that adbhuta form, not an abstract 'divine,' be your object of meditation.
  • Refuse to flatten Kṛṣṇa into 'the universe' or 'consciousness' — when you speak of Him today, name His personal features (lotus eyes, blackish hue like a dense cloud, yellow dress) so the personalist conviction stays sharp.
  • Notice one moment today when you are tempted to credit your own talents or possessions for some success, and instead bow inwardly to the Lord whose Vaidūrya-helmet opulence makes every mortal ornament a borrowed shadow.
Reflection Questions
  • When I encounter Kṛṣṇa's four-armed, ornamented form in this verse, what part of me wants to file it away as 'mythology' rather than receive it as the literal descent of the Supreme — and what is that resistance protecting?
  • Vasudeva is granted the vision of the catur-bhuja form precisely in a moment of mortal danger and powerlessness; where in my own life am I refusing to look up from my fear long enough to see who has actually arrived?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Pick one image of Kṛṣṇa as catur-bhuja Viṣṇu — śaṅkha, cakra, gadā, padma — set it as your phone lockscreen for a week, and notice how it changes the first three seconds of every scroll session.
  • When peers pressure you toward an impersonal 'spiritual but not religious' stance ('all gods are the same, energy is everything'), practice naming Kṛṣṇa specifically — not as one option among many, but as the Person Vasudeva actually saw. Try it once this week, even if it costs social comfort.
  • Before an exam or a high-stakes social moment, instead of generic 'manifesting,' visualize the adbhuta-bālaka — four-armed, śrīvatsa-marked, Kaustubha-bright — and ask Him for steadiness (tejas), not for the outcome you want.
householder
  • When making a family decision (school for the kids, a job change, a major purchase), pause and ask: am I consulting Kṛṣṇa as a Person I am answerable to, or am I treating 'the universe' as a vague vending machine? The verse insists on the personal Lord — practice that distinction this week.
  • Decorate one corner of the home with a deity or picture of catur-bhuja Viṣṇu / Bāla-Kṛṣṇa, and let your children see you bow there before you eat. They will not remember your lectures on dharma; they will remember whom you bowed to.
  • When wealth, status, or career success starts to feel like your own achievement (ahaṅkāra), recall Vasudeva's response — he saw the ornaments and immediately understood they belonged to the Lord, not to him. Offer one specific success this month explicitly to Kṛṣṇa, in words, aloud.
senior
  • Aging strips ornaments from the body — strength, beauty, position — and that loss can feel like erasure. Meditate instead on the eternally ornamented form of the Lord: the body fades, but the Person to whom we are returning is more, not less, adorned. Let this reframe one fear about decline this week.
  • Vasudeva is a father at the moment of beholding the Lord as a child. In your mentoring of children, grandchildren, or younger devotees, do not pass on a vague 'be good, be spiritual' — pass on the specific Person, the catur-bhuja form, the Name. Tell one story this week that names Kṛṣṇa, not 'the divine.'
  • Practice vairāgya as Vasudeva does in this verse: not by rejecting beauty, but by recognizing whose beauty it always was. Walk through your home and silently re-attribute three things you have called 'mine' — the house, a possession, a relationship — back to Kṛṣṇa, in preparation for the handover that mortality will eventually require.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading the Śrīvatsa, Kaustubha, śaṅkha, cakra, gadā, padma and Vaidūrya ornaments as 'symbolic poetry' — quietly impersonalizing the verse and stripping Kṛṣṇa of His personal, opulent form.
  • Treating the four-armed Viṣṇu appearance as 'a different deity from Kṛṣṇa,' falling into demigod-confusion instead of recognizing one Supreme Personality of Godhead revealing His aiśvarya before concealing it as the two-armed bāla.
  • Using the verse's 'wonder' (adbhutam) as an aesthetic feeling — admiring the description like a painting — without letting it dismantle the assumption that the Lord's birth is comparable to an ordinary human birth.
Discussion Prompts
  • Vasudeva sees a four-handed child holding śaṅkha, cakra, gadā, and padma — yet within moments he will plead with the Lord to assume the form of an ordinary infant. Discuss in pairs: was Vasudeva right to ask Kṛṣṇa to hide His opulence, or was that a moment of fear masquerading as parental concern? Where in your own life do you ask Kṛṣṇa to 'tone down' so you can manage Him?
  • The verse insists this child is adbhuta — distinct in every detail from an ordinary newborn (four arms, fully grown hair, Kaustubha, Vaidūrya helmet). Modern devotees sometimes prefer the 'sweet baby Kṛṣṇa' and quietly skip these majestic descriptions. Argue both sides: does emphasizing aiśvarya (opulence) at His advent help or hinder mādhurya (sweetness) in our bhakti? Where do you actually stand?
  • Anahaṅkāra (freedom from false ego) is one of this verse's connected values, yet the Lord appears blazing with crown, belt, armlets, and bangles. Discuss: how can the most ornamented Person in the cosmos be the model of egolessness? What does this say about renunciation that merely strips ornaments off the body?
Role Play

Two students pair up. Student A plays a sincere seeker who has just told their family they want to take bhakti seriously; Student B plays a well-meaning relative who says, 'I respect spirituality, but why this specific blue four-armed God with weapons and jewelry? Isn't the formless Absolute more mature, more universal?' Student A must respond drawing only on what SB 10.3.9-10 actually shows — that Kṛṣṇa Himself chose to appear with form, weapons, and ornaments before Vasudeva's eyes; that the personalist revelation is not a concession to childish minds but the Lord's own self-disclosure. Swap roles after five minutes. Then debrief: where did the personalist answer feel strong, and where did it feel like you were performing rather than convinced?

Objections & Responses
  • This is obviously mythological embellishment. A newborn cannot have four arms, fully grown hair, a jeweled crown, and a belt. Treating this literally is exactly the kind of thing that makes outsiders dismiss our tradition.
    Prabhupāda anticipates this directly in the purport: 'No ordinary child in human society has ever been born with four hands. And when is a child born with fully grown hair?' That is precisely the point. The text is not claiming an ordinary birth happened in an unusual way — it is declaring that the descent of the Lord (avatāra) is categorically distinct from janma. If we reduce the verse to symbol, we have already decided in advance that Kṛṣṇa cannot actually appear; we have made our skepticism the test of scripture rather than scripture the test of our skepticism. The Gauḍīya answer is not to argue biology but to ask: am I willing to let the Lord be more than my categories allow?
  • Why does Kṛṣṇa need a Kaustubha gem, a Vaidūrya helmet, a śrīvatsa mark, a yellow dhotī, armlets, bangles, and a belt? Real spirituality is supposed to be about renouncing ornaments, not displaying them. This sounds like divinized vanity.
    This is the impersonalist reflex — to assume that purity must look like absence. But ornaments on Kṛṣṇa are not what ornaments are on us. On us, ornaments cover ahaṅkāra; on Him, they are extensions of His svarūpa, beautifying Him for the pleasure of His devotees. The Brahma-saṁhitā describes His form as sac-cid-ānanda — every feature, including the Kaustubha and the pītāmbara, is spiritual substance, not material decoration. Vairāgya in our line is not about stripping reality of beauty; it is about withdrawing affection from the temporary so it can rest in the eternally beautiful Person. Vasudeva is not being asked to admire a bejeweled emperor — he is being shown that the Absolute is a Person rich enough to need nothing, and generous enough to appear in full splendor anyway.
Analogy

When an emperor visits a humble house in disguise, he leaves the disguise off just long enough for the host to know who has come — the silks, the seal-ring, the bodyguards flash for an instant, then vanish into ordinary dress. Kṛṣṇa shows Vasudeva the four-armed Viṣṇu form precisely to identify Himself, then will fold it away. The opulence is not display; it is the credential.

Stories
Rāma showing His four-armed form to Kausalyā

The Rāmāyaṇa records that immediately after His birth, Rāma briefly showed Kausalyā His four-armed Nārāyaṇa form — śaṅkha, cakra, gadā, padma — so that she would know whom she had received. Then, at her request, He withdrew the form and lay before her as a child.

The Bhāgavatam repeats the pattern verse-for-verse here. Bhagavān grants His parents the dṛṣṭi to know what they hold before the human relationship begins. Sambandha is established first; only then does līlā unfold.
