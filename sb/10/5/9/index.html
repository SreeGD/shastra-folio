Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.5.9

The gopī wives of the cowherd men were very pleased to hear that mother Yaśodā had given birth to a son, and they began to decorate themselves very nicely with proper dresses, ornaments, black ointment for the eyes, and so on.
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
The gopī wives of the cowherd men were very pleased to hear that mother Yaśodā had given birth to a son, and they began to decorate themselves very nicely with proper dresses, ornaments, black ointment for the eyes, and so on.
Commentary

Gopyah refers chiefly to the sisters in law of Yasoda, as well as others.

Jīva Gosvāmī Gauḍīya
Commentary

The women included the wives of Nanda’s brothers. The word ca indicates all others. The cowherds heard first, and then the women, since they were inside the houses. They also were overjoyed. This is described. Hearing of the birth of a son to Yaśodā (who gave fame to Vraja) who had no sons, they were overjoyed because of their natural prema for her and the son. Therefore they decorated themselves with cloth and cosmetics.

Concepts
Jiva
Personal Guidance
Hearing of Kṛṣṇa's birth, the gopīs adorn themselves — true joy in another's good fortune dresses the heart for service, not the self.
Practical Applications
  • Notice the next time you hear of someone else's blessing and refuse the inward pang of envy — instead, prepare yourself outwardly and inwardly to celebrate with them.
  • Before your morning sādhana today, dress and groom with care as an offering, treating the act as preparation to approach Kṛṣṇa rather than to be seen by others.
  • Speak to one person today about a piece of good news that isn't yours, and let your delight be visible — practice the gopī-bhāva of shared rejoicing.
Reflection Questions
  • When I hear that Kṛṣṇa has 'appeared' in my day — through scripture, kīrtana, a saint's word — do I respond with the gopīs' eager preparation, or do I receive Him in whatever distracted, half-dressed condition I happen to be in?
  • What 'ornaments' (bodily, mental, devotional) am I quick to put on for a social occasion that I never bother to put on for Kṛṣṇa, and what does that ranking reveal about where my prema actually sits?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • When a classmate posts an exam result, internship, or admit that you wanted, do not just like-and-scroll. Send a specific, embodied congratulation within five minutes — voice note, not emoji — the way the gopīs left their houses to go honor Yaśodā's son.
  • Before going out with friends this weekend, examine the time you spend on hair, clothes, makeup: is it to be seen by them, or have you offered the going-out itself to Kṛṣṇa first (a quick praṇāma, an intention)? Same act, different consciousness — that is the gopī shift.
  • Identify one friend whose recent good news you quietly resented (a relationship, a college acceptance, a follower count). Today, go to them — physically or via call — and celebrate it concretely. Notice in your body what changes when you refuse the comparison reflex.
householder
  • When a colleague gets the promotion, a cousin's child gets into the school yours did not, or a neighbor buys the house — do not perform congratulation, embody it. Show up with sweets within 24 hours. Train the family in this reflex; the gopīs were a community, not isolated devotees.
  • Re-examine household money spent on dress, decoration, festivals. Cut what is for show; keep and even increase what is for offering — a clean home for guests, proper clothes for temple visits, ornaments worn when receiving sādhus or going to Kṛṣṇa's darśana. Yukta-vairāgya, not phalgu-vairāgya.
  • Teach your children, by visible example, to celebrate other children's wins — the cousin's prize, the friend's birthday — with real preparation (dressing up, going, bringing something). They are learning prema or learning competition from you, in this verse's exact register.
senior
  • Bless the births, weddings, and milestones in the next generation without inserting your story. The gopīs did not mention their own sons when they ran to Yaśodā's house. Practice unalloyed delight in another's good fortune as preparation for leaving this body.
  • Look at the ornaments, clothes, possessions you have accumulated. Begin, this year, to give away what is purely for self-display, and keep only what serves Kṛṣṇa or others — the cloth offered to the Deity, the jewelry gifted to a granddaughter at her marriage, the home opened to devotees. Decoration becomes seva on the way out.
  • Mentor younger householders in the dying art of showing up — physically attending births, weddings, funerals, illnesses in the community — instead of texting. You carry the memory of a culture where the village dressed and went; transmit it before that knowledge dies with your generation.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading the gopīs' decoration as mere material vanity and dismissing the verse — missing that their adornment is an outward expression of inward prema, not a sense-gratification project.
  • Flipping to the opposite extreme and treating outward care (clean cloth, tilaka, a tidy altar, fresh flowers) as 'externals' beneath the 'real' devotee — using a counterfeit vairāgya to excuse slovenliness in Kṛṣṇa's service.
  • Imitating the gopīs' mood without their qualification — performing a sentimental, dressed-up 'celebration of Kṛṣṇa's birth' while sādhana, śravaṇa, and sva-dharma remain neglected, mistaking festive emotion for advancement.
Discussion Prompts
  • The gopīs decorate themselves the moment they hear of Kṛṣṇa's birth — they do not strip down, they dress up. Where does the modern devotional reflex to 'renounce externals' actually miss what vairāgya means here? Share an example from your own life where outward beautification was genuinely seva, not viṣaya.
  • Compare the gopīs' ornaments-for-Kṛṣṇa with the same ornaments worn for one's own enjoyment, social status, or Instagram. Walk through with your partner: what is the actual internal difference? Is it intent alone, or does the object/audience also change the act? Push back on each other.
  • Yaśodā's joy spreads instantly through the whole village of gopīs — none of them say 'that's her son, not mine.' Where in your own community (saṅga, family, workplace) do you withhold celebration of someone else's good fortune because it is not yours? Be specific. What does the gopīs' response cost them, and what does it cost you to imitate it?
Role Play

Two students play co-workers at the same firm. Co-worker A has just received a major promotion and bonus that B was also in the running for. A walks into the shared office visibly happy. B has thirty seconds to respond — face, words, body. Run the scene three times: (1) B reacts from ahaṅkāra (the 'that should have been me' self); (2) B reacts with performative congratulation while privately seething (the impersonalist 'be detached' pose); (3) B reacts as a gopī of Vraja would on hearing of Yaśodā's son — genuine, embodied, decorating the moment. After each round, the watching group calls out exactly which words and gestures shifted, and the teacher draws out: where in the third round did Kṛṣṇa actually become the center, displacing the 'me' calculus?

Objections & Responses
  • Decorating yourself with clothes, ornaments, and kohl is exactly the kind of bodily identification (dehātma-buddhi) that the Gītā tells us to transcend. Isn't this verse celebrating shallow, materialistic, even vain behavior — and dressing it up as devotion?
    The objection assumes vairāgya means rejection of the body and its uses. The Gauḍīya line, following Rūpa Gosvāmī, calls that phalgu-vairāgya — dry, false renunciation. Real vairāgya is yukta-vairāgya: every faculty, including the body and its ornaments, engaged in Kṛṣṇa's service. The gopīs are not decorating themselves to be seen; they are decorating themselves because a son has been born to Yaśodā in their village, and they will go to honor Him. The kohl, the cloth, the ornaments are now seva-upakaraṇa — instruments of service. The same lipstick worn for one's own viṣaya-enjoyment is bondage; worn to enter Kṛṣṇa's presence properly attired, it is bhakti. The Gītā does not ask us to abandon the body; it asks us to stop misidentifying with it and offer it. This verse shows precisely that offering.
  • This is a cute village scene but it has nothing to do with my actual life. I am not a cowherd wife in Vraja. Reading practical instruction into ornament-wearing villagers feels like forcing relevance.
    Notice what the verse actually records: news of a birth in a neighbor's house, and the spontaneous, embodied joy of an entire community of women who treat that child as their own. That is not parochial — that is the rarest thing in modern life. We live atomized; another's good news is processed as a threat to our own standing. The gopīs model what prema looks like at the level of ordinary daily reception of news. You do receive news daily — promotions, births, engagements, awards belonging to others. The verse is asking, very practically: what is your first reflex? Withholding, comparing, scrolling past? Or decorating, going, celebrating? The setting is Vraja; the reflex is testable in your group chat this evening. And the deeper Gauḍīya point: the gopīs' love is the highest reach of bhakti precisely because it is not abstract — it is dressed, walking, knocking on a door. Your life gives you the same chance, in your idiom, today.
