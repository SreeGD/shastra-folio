Chapter 25 · Class prep

BG 25.1

The great sage Maitreya continued speaking to Vidura: My dear Vidura, in this way Lord Śiva instructed the sons of King Barhiṣat. The sons of the King also worshiped Lord Śiva with great devotion and respect. Finally, Lord Śiva became invisible to the princes.

Essence. Śiva instructs the princes; they worship him; Śiva becomes invisible. The teacher's disappearance after teaching is its own gift; the student must now stand alone with what was given.

BG 25.2

All the Pracetā princes simply stood in the water for ten thousand years and recited the prayers given to them by Lord Śiva.

Essence. The Pracetās stood in water for 10,000 years reciting Śiva's prayers. Sustained practice in one place over enormous time is the engine of real austerity; modern impatience cannot match it.

BG 25.3

While the princes were undergoing severe austerities in the water, their father was performing different types of fruitive activities. At this time the great saint Nārada, master and teacher of all spiritual life, became very compassionate upon the King and decided to instruct him about spiritual life.

Essence. Meanwhile, the father continued fruitive activity; compassionate Nārada decides to instruct him. Some fathers need teaching more than their sons; the path doesn't always run top-down.

BG 25.4

Nārada Muni asked King Prācīnabarhiṣat: My dear King, what do you desire to achieve by performing these fruitive activities? The chief aim of life is to get rid of all miseries and enjoy happiness, but these two things cannot be realized by fruitive activity.

Essence. Nārada asks: what do you want from fruitive activities? Their goals — happiness and miseries' removal — aren't reached this way. The question itself diagnoses the futility of the means.

BG 25.5

The King replied: O great soul, Nārada, my intelligence is entangled in fruitive activities; therefore I do not know the ultimate goal of life. Kindly instruct me in pure knowledge so that I can get out of the entanglement of fruitive activities.

Essence. The king admits: my intelligence is entangled; please instruct me in pure knowledge. The honest confession of entanglement is the threshold of liberation.

BG 25.6

Those who are interested only in a so-called dutiful life — namely remaining as a householder entangled by sons and a wife and searching after wealth — think that such things are life’s ultimate goal. Such people simply wander in different types of bodies throughout this material existence without finding out the ultimate goal of life.

Essence. Those who think dutiful householder life is the ultimate goal wander through bodies without finding what they want. The 'good life' as ultimate goal is a wandering, not an arrival.

BG 25.7

The great saint Nārada said: O ruler of the citizens, my dear King, please see in the sky those animals which you have sacrificed without compassion and without mercy in the sacrificial arena.

Essence. See in the sky those animals you sacrificed without compassion. The unseen witnesses to our cruelty wait; ritual is not a license for routine harm.

BG 25.8

All these animals are awaiting your death so that they can avenge the injuries you have inflicted upon them. After you die, they will angrily pierce your body with iron horns.

Essence. After your death they will pierce your body with iron horns in revenge. Excessive violence in ritual creates real karmic debt with the victims; you'll meet them again.

BG 25.9

In this connection I wish to narrate an old history connected with the character of a king called Puraṣjana. Please try to hear me with great attention.

Essence. Let me narrate the story of King Purañjana — please hear with great attention. Sometimes the teacher prefers parable to direct rebuke; the same content lands deeper as story.

BG 25.10

My dear King, once in the past lived a king named Puraṣjana, who was celebrated for his great activities. He had a friend named Avijṣāta [“the unknown one”]. No one could understand the activities of Avijṣāta.

Essence. King Purañjana — 'one who lives within a body' — had a friend Avijñāta ('the unknown'). Every embodied soul has an unknown companion (the Lord) whose activity can't be tracked.

BG 25.11

King Puraṣjana began to search for a suitable place to live, and thus he traveled all over the world. Even after a great deal of traveling, he could not find a place just to his liking. Finally he became morose and disappointed.

Essence. Purañjana wandered everywhere looking for a satisfying place to live but found none. The geographical search for satisfaction is always vain; the dissatisfaction is internal.

BG 25.12

King Puraṣjana had unlimited desires for sense enjoyment; consequently he traveled all over the world to find a place where all his desires could be fulfilled. Unfortunately he found a feeling of insufficiency everywhere.

Essence. Unlimited sense-enjoyment desires made everywhere feel insufficient. Capacity for enjoyment outruns supply everywhere; the issue isn't the supply.

BG 25.13

Once, while wandering in this way, he saw on the southern side of the Himālayas, in a place named Bhārata-varṣa [India], a city that had nine gates all about and was characterized by all auspicious facilities.

Essence. South of the Himālayas, in Bhārata-varṣa, he found a nine-gated city with every facility. The body itself is the city; nine gates of perception and elimination.

BG 25.14

That city was surrounded by walls and parks, and within it were towers, canals, windows and outlets. The houses there were decorated with domes made of gold, silver and iron.

Essence. The city had walls, parks, towers, canals, windows, gold-silver-iron domed houses. Each part of the body has its function and ornament; the city is exquisitely designed.

BG 25.15

The floors of the houses in that city were made of sapphire, crystal, diamonds, pearls, emeralds and rubies. Because of the luster of the houses in the capital, the city was compared to the celestial town named Bhogavatī.

Essence. Floors of sapphire, crystal, diamond, pearl, emerald, ruby — the heart-capital lustrous as Bhogavatī. The heart is the capital, gem-encrusted; respect it accordingly.

BG 25.16

In that city there were many assembly houses, street crossings, streets, restaurants, gambling houses, markets, resting places, flags, festoons and beautiful parks. All these surrounded the city.

Essence. Assembly houses, street crossings, restaurants, gambling, markets, festoons, parks — the city contains every category of activity. Embodied life has many neighborhoods; some serve dharma, some don't.

BG 25.17

On the outskirts of that city were many beautiful trees and creepers encircling a nice lake. Also surrounding that lake were many groups of birds and bees that were always chanting and humming.

Essence. Outside, beautiful trees, creepers, a lake, birds, bees — sense organs serve sexual enjoyment among other things. The body's external organs are the gardens of sense-life; pleasant, but distracting.

BG 25.18

The branches of the trees standing on the bank of the lake received particles of water carried by the spring air from the falls coming down from the icy mountain.

Essence. Trees on the bank received spray from icy waterfalls; the body has different humors (rasas). The body's internal climate is itself moved by 'waterfalls' of mood and humor.

BG 25.19

In such an atmosphere even the animals of the forest became nonviolent and nonenvious like great sages. Consequently, the animals did not attack anyone. Over and above everything was the cooing of the cuckoos. Any passenger passing along that path was invited by that atmosphere to take rest in that nice garden.

Essence. Even the forest animals became nonviolent in that atmosphere; cuckoos cooed; the path invited rest. Some inner atmospheres pacify everything; cultivate them and your 'animals' become tame.

BG 25.20

While wandering here and there in that wonderful garden, King Puraṣjana suddenly came in contact with a very beautiful woman who was walking there without any engagement. She had ten servants with her, and each servant had hundreds of wives accompanying him.

Essence. He met a beautiful woman wandering with ten servants, each with hundreds of wives. Intelligence walks with the ten senses, each commanding many sub-functions; recognize the entourage.

BG 25.21

The woman was protected on all sides by a five-hooded snake. She was very beautiful and young, and she appeared very anxious to find a suitable husband.

Essence. She was protected by a five-hooded snake and seemed anxious for a husband. The vital airs (five-hooded snake) protect intelligence; intelligence seeks a master (the soul).

BG 25.22

The woman’s nose, teeth and forehead were all very beautiful. Her ears were equally very beautiful and were bedecked with dazzling earrings.

Essence. Beautiful nose, teeth, forehead, dazzling earrings. Intelligence's beauty includes specific sensory powers — smell, taste, sight, hearing — each ornamented.

BG 25.23

The waist and hips of the woman were very beautiful. She was dressed in a yellow sārī with a golden belt. While she walked, her ankle bells rang. She appeared exactly like a denizen of the heavens.

Essence. Beautiful waist and hips, yellow sārī, golden belt, ringing ankle bells — like a heavenly being. The body of intelligence dressed for the soul's encounter; this is no accidental beauty.

BG 25.24

With the end of her sārī the woman was trying to cover her breasts, which were equally round and well placed side by side. She again and again tried to cover them out of shyness while she walked exactly like a great elephant.

Essence. She tried to cover her breasts (attachment and envy) and walked like an elephant. The body of intelligence carries attachment and envy as twin features — shy yet present.

BG 25.25

Puraṣjana, the hero, became attracted by the eyebrows and smiling face of the very beautiful girl and was immediately pierced by the arrows of her lusty desires. When she smiled shyly, she looked very beautiful to Puraṣjana, who, although a hero, could not refrain from addressing her.

Essence. Purañjana was pierced by her arrows and could not refrain from addressing her. Even the heroic soul is undone by the smile of intelligence offering sensory life.

BG 25.26

My dear lotus-eyed, kindly explain to me where you are coming from, who you are, and whose daughter you are. You appear very chaste. What is the purpose of your coming here? What are you trying to do? Please explain all these things to me.

Essence. He asks: who are you, whose daughter, why are you here? The soul's first sensible move when meeting intelligence is to ask its origin and purpose.

BG 25.27

My dear lotus-eyed, who are those eleven strong bodyguards with you, and who are those ten specific servants? Who are those women following the ten servants, and who is the snake that is preceding you?

Essence. Who are those eleven bodyguards, ten servants, the snake — explain. The right disciple-of-the-soul asks about every member of intelligence's retinue.

BG 25.28

My dear beautiful girl, you are exactly like the goddess of fortune or the wife of Lord Śiva or the goddess of learning, the wife of Lord Brahmā. Although you must be one of them, I see that you are loitering in this forest. Indeed, you are as silent as the great sages. Is it that you are searching after your own husband? Whoever your husband may be, simply by understanding that you are so faithful to him, he will come to possess all opulences. I think you must be the goddess of fortune, but I do not see the lotus flower in your hand. Therefore I am asking you where you have thrown that lotus.

Essence. Are you Lakṣmī, Pārvatī, Sarasvatī? Are you searching for your missing husband? When you encounter intelligence, ask whether it's serving a higher form already — don't assume it's available.

BG 25.29

O greatly fortunate one, it appears that you are none of the women I have mentioned because I see that your feet are touching the ground. But if you are some woman of this planet, you can, like the goddess of fortune — who, accompanied by Lord Viṣṇu, increases the beauty of the Vaikuṇṭha planets — also increase the beauty of this city by associating with me. You should understand that I am a great hero and a very powerful king on this planet.

Essence. Your feet touch the ground — so you must be earthly; like Lakṣmī, accompanied by Viṣṇu, you could increase the beauty of any place. Even ordinary intelligence is potentially divine if rightly accompanied.

BG 25.30

Certainly your glancing upon me today has very much agitated my mind. Your smile, which is full of shyness but at the same time lusty, is agitating the most powerful cupid within me. Therefore, O most beautiful, I ask you to be merciful upon me.

Essence. Your glance has agitated my mind; your shy-yet-lusty smile awakens cupid — please be merciful. The internal cupid is real; soul-to-intelligence chemistry is no joke.

BG 25.31

My dear girl, your face is so beautiful with your nice eyebrows and eyes and with your bluish hair scattered about. In addition, very sweet sounds are coming from your mouth. Nonetheless, you are so covered with shyness that you do not see me face to face. I therefore request you, my dear girl, to smile and kindly raise your head to see me.

Essence. Your face, hair, voice — but shyness makes you avoid looking face to face. The soul addresses intelligence asking for direct gaze; without it, the dance is incomplete.

BG 25.32

Nārada continued: My dear King, when Puraṣjana became so attracted and impatient to touch the girl and enjoy her, the girl also became attracted by his words and accepted his request by smiling. By this time she was certainly attracted by the King.

Essence. She accepted Purañjana's request with a smile. The aggressive courting works; the woman becomes attracted to the man. Initiating attention is sometimes what unlocks the response.

BG 25.33

The girl said: O best of human beings, I do not know who has begotten me. I cannot speak to you perfectly about this. Nor do I know the names or the origin of the associates with me.

Essence. She admits: I don't know who begat me; I can't speak perfectly of myself or my companions. Intelligence often doesn't know its own origin; ignorance is its default condition.

BG 25.34

O great hero, we only know that we are existing in this place. We do not know what will come after. Indeed, we are so foolish that we do not care to understand who has created this beautiful place for our residence.

Essence. We only know we are here; we don't care to understand who created this place. The unexamined intelligence is content with location, indifferent to origin.

BG 25.35

My dear gentleman, all these men and women with me are known as my friends, and the snake, who always remains awake, protects this city even during my sleeping hours. So much I know. I do not know anything beyond this.

Essence. These men and women are my friends; the snake protects the city even while I sleep. Sleep's protection is the vital force; even when consciousness sleeps, something stays awake.

BG 25.36

O killer of the enemy, you have somehow or other come here. This is certainly great fortune for me. I wish all auspicious things for you. You have a great desire to satisfy your senses, and all my friends and I shall try our best in all respects to fulfill your desires.

Essence. Your arrival is fortunate; we'll satisfy your senses. Intelligence offers itself for the soul's sense-fulfillment; the offer is real but limited.

BG 25.37

My dear lord, I have just arranged this city of nine gates for you so that you can have all kinds of sense gratification. You may live here for one hundred years, and everything for your sense gratification will be supplied.

Essence. I have arranged this nine-gated city for you; live here a hundred years with all gratification supplied. The body's term is a hundred years; intelligence promises full provision over that span.

BG 25.38

How can I expect to unite with others, who are neither conversant about sex nor capable of knowing how to enjoy life while living or after death? Such foolish persons are like animals because they do not know the process of sense enjoyment in this life and after death.

Essence. I can't expect to unite with those ignorant of sex's enjoyment in this life and after; they're like animals. Intelligence positions its alliance with soul as superior to other less-sophisticated unions.

BG 25.39

The woman continued: In this material world, a householder’s life brings all kinds of happiness in religion, economic development, sense gratification and the begetting of children, sons and grandsons. After that, one may desire liberation as well as material reputation. The householder can appreciate the results of sacrifices, which enable him to gain promotion to superior planetary systems. All this material happiness is practically unknown to the transcendentalists. They cannot even imagine such happiness.

Essence. Householder life delivers dharma, artha, kāma, plus children and then liberation; one can appreciate them all here. The seducer's pitch isn't false — it's just incomplete.

BG 25.40

The woman continued: According to authorities, the householder life is pleasing not only to oneself but to all the forefathers, demigods, great sages, saintly persons and everyone else. A householder life is thus beneficial.

Essence. Householder life pleases self, forefathers, demigods, sages, all others — it's beneficial. The case for householder life cites many beneficiaries; the breadth of benefit obscures the spiritual ceiling.

BG 25.41

O my dear hero, who in this world will not accept a husband like you? You are so famous, so magnanimous, so beautiful and so easily gotten.

Essence. Who in this world won't accept a husband like you — famous, magnanimous, beautiful, easily obtained? Flattery completes the seduction; the soul is now thoroughly addressed in its self-image.

BG 25.42

O mighty-armed, who in this world will not be attracted by your arms, which are just like the bodies of serpents? Actually you relieve the distress of husbandless women like us by your attractive smile and your aggressive mercy. We think that you are traveling on the surface of the earth just to benefit us only.

Essence. Your arms — like serpents — relieve the distress of husbandless women; you travel to benefit fortunate ones like us. The seduction's final move is to position the seducer as benefactor; the soul is now indispensable in his own mind.

BG 25.43

The great sage Nārada continued: My dear King, those two — the man and the woman — supporting one another through mutual understanding, entered that city and enjoyed life for one hundred years.

Essence. The two enjoyed life for a hundred years through mutual understanding. The soul-intelligence pact is a real long collaboration; that doesn't make it the right one.

BG 25.44

Many professional singers used to sing about the glories of King Puraṣjana and his glorious activities. When it was too hot in the summer, he used to enter a reservoir of water. He would surround himself with many women and enjoy their company.

Essence. Singers sang his glory; in summer he entered reservoirs and surrounded himself with women. Conditioned life cycles through wakefulness, dream, and pleasure-seeking; the king does the round.

BG 25.45

Of the nine gates in that city, seven were on the surface and two were subterranean. A total of nine doors were constructed, and these led to different places. All the gates were used by the city’s governor.

Essence. Nine gates — seven on the surface (eyes, ears, nostrils, mouth), two below (rectum, genitals). The body's traffic flows through these specific portals; map them, govern them.

BG 25.46

My dear King, of the nine doors, five led toward the eastern side, one led toward the northern side, one led toward the southern side, and two led toward the western side. I shall try to give the names of these different doors.

Essence. Five gates faced east (sense-organs forward), one north, one south, two west. Directional design is part of the body's architecture; nothing is random.

BG 25.47

The two gates named Khadyotā and Āvirmukhī were situated facing the eastern side, but they were constructed in one place. Through those two gates the King used to go to the city of Vibhrājita accompanied by a friend whose name was Dyumān.

Essence. Khadyotā (glowworm) and Āvirmukhī (torchlight) — the two eyes — let the king visit Vibhrājita with Dyumān (sun). Vision is the first principle by which intelligence travels; sun is its enabler.

BG 25.48

Similarly in the east there were two sets of gates named Nalinī and Nālinī, and these were also constructed in one place. Through these gates the King, accompanied by a friend named Avadhūta, used to go to the city of Saurabha.

Essence. Nalinī and Nālinī (nostrils) with friend Avadhūta (air) lead to Saurabha (fragrance). The breath is the friend that opens the world of smell; honor it.

BG 25.49

The fifth gate situated on the eastern side was named Mukhyā, or the chief. Through this gate, accompanied by his friends named Rasajṣa and Vipaṇa, he used to visit two places named Bahūdana and Āpaṇa.

Essence. Mukhyā (the chief — mouth) with Rasajña (taste-knower) and Vipaṇa (speech) goes to Bahūdana (food) and Āpaṇa (market). Mouth is chief: eating and speaking, both done at the same portal.

BG 25.50

The southern gate of the city was known as Pitṛhū, and through that gate King Puraṣjana used to visit the city named Dakṣiṇa-paṣcāla, accompanied by his friend Śrutadhara.

Essence. Pitṛhū (right ear) with Śrutadhara goes to Dakṣiṇa-pañcāla; karma-kāṇḍa hearing leads to fruitive realms. The ear you tune in with shapes the realm you end up visiting.

BG 25.51

On the northern side was the gate named Devahū. Through that gate, King Puraṣjana used to go with his friend Śrutadhara to the place known as Uttara-paṣcāla.

Essence. Devahū (north ear) takes the king with Śrutadhara to Uttara-pañcāla; the other ear is for higher hearing. Two ears, two destinations; left/right correspond to fruitive and spiritual hearing.

BG 25.52

On the western side was a gate named Āsurī. Through that gate King Puraṣjana used to go to the city of Grāmaka, accompanied by his friend Durmada.

Essence. Āsurī (west gate, genitals) leads to Grāmaka with friend Durmada (foolish pride). Sense gratification through the lower gate routes through pride; not just biology.

BG 25.53

Another gate on the western side was known as Nirṛti. Puraṣjana used to go through this gate to the place known as Vaiśasa, accompanied by his friend Lubdhaka.

Essence. Nirṛti (rectum) with friend Lubdhaka (greed) leads to Vaiśasa (slaughter). The path of elimination paired with greed leads to consumption-driven destruction.

BG 25.54

Of the many inhabitants of this city, there are two persons named Nirvāk and Peśaskṛt. Although King Puraṣjana was the ruler of citizens who possessed eyes, he unfortunately used to associate with these blind men. Accompanied by them, he used to go here and there and perform various activities.

Essence. Nirvāk (blind) and Peśaskṛt (mute) — the legs — though blind, the king relied on them. The legs walk where they want, blindly; we follow them and call it our direction.

BG 25.55

Sometimes he used to go to his private home with one of his chief servants [the mind], who was named Viṣūcīna. At that time, illusion, satisfaction and happiness used to be produced from his wife and children.

Essence. Sometimes he went to his private home with Viṣūcīna (mind); illusion, satisfaction and happiness came from wife and children. The heart-home is where the mind takes the soul; family-feelings are the décor.

BG 25.56

Being thus entangled in different types of mental concoction and engaged in fruitive activities, King Puraṣjana came completely under the control of material intelligence and was thus cheated. Indeed, he used to fulfill all the desires of his wife, the Queen.

Essence. Entangled in mental concoction, under material intelligence's control, he fulfilled all the queen's desires. The soul under intelligence-control becomes wife-servant; the formal hierarchy reverses.

BG 25.57-61

When the Queen drank liquor, King Puraṣjana also engaged in drinking. When the Queen dined, he used to dine with her, and when she chewed, King Puraṣjana used to chew along with her. When the Queen sang, he also sang. Similarly, when the Queen cried, he also cried, and when the Queen laughed, he also laughed. When the Queen talked loosely, he also talked loosely, and when the Queen walked, the King walked behind her. When the Queen would stand still, the King would also stand still, and when the Queen would lie down in bed, he would also follow and lie down with her. When the Queen sat, he would also sit, and when the Queen heard something, he would follow her to hear the same thing. When the Queen saw something, the King would also look at it, and when the Queen smelled something, the King would follow her to smell the same thing. When the Queen touched something, the King would also touch it, and when the dear Queen was lamenting, the poor King also had to follow her in lamentation. In the same way, when the Queen felt enjoyment, he also enjoyed, and when the Queen was satisfied, the King also felt satisfaction.

Essence. When she drank, he drank; when she sang, he sang; when she cried, he cried — like a pet animal dancing to her command. The soul mirrors intelligence's every mood once it's surrendered direction; this is bondage.

BG 25.62

In this way, King Puraṣjana was captivated by his nice wife and was thus cheated. Indeed, he became cheated in his whole existence in the material world. Even against that poor foolish King’s desire, he remained under the control of his wife, just like a pet animal that dances according to the order of its master.

Essence. Captivated, cheated, he stayed under control like a pet animal dancing to orders. The diagnosis is clear: cheating happens through the very desires we thought were ours.
