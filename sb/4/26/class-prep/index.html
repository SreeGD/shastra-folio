BG 26.1-3

The great sage Nārada continued: My dear King, once upon a time King Puraṣjana took up his great bow, and equipped with golden armor and a quiver of unlimited arrows and accompanied by eleven commanders, he sat on his chariot driven by five swift horses and went to the forest named Paṣca-prastha. He took with him in that chariot two explosive arrows. The chariot itself was situated on two wheels and one revolving axle. On the chariot were three flags, one rein, one chariot driver, one sitting place, two poles to which the harness was fixed, five weapons and seven coverings. The chariot moved in five different styles, and five obstacles lay before it. All the decorations of the chariot were made of gold.

Essence. Purañjana with bow, gold armor, eleven commanders and five-horse chariot went hunting in Pañca-prastha. The body is chariot, soul is rider; under the modes one goes hunting — destruction in the name of sport.

BG 26.4

It was almost impossible for King Puraṣjana to give up the company of his Queen even for a moment. Nonetheless, on that day, being very much inspired by the desire to hunt, he took up his bow and arrow with great pride and went to the forest, not caring for his wife.

Essence. Though he could not leave his queen for a moment, today he proudly went hunting without her. Even the most attached souls periodically leave intelligence to chase unguided impulse.

BG 26.5

At that time King Puraṣjana was very much influenced by demoniac propensities. Because of this, his heart became very hard and merciless, and with sharp arrows he killed many innocent animals in the forest, taking no consideration.

Essence. Demonic propensities hardened his heart; he killed many innocent animals without consideration. Cruelty escalates when intelligence is left at home; soft heart needs daily companionship to stay soft.

BG 26.6

If a king is too attracted to eating flesh, he may, according to the directions of the revealed scriptures on sacrificial performances, go to the forest and kill some animals that are recommended for killing. One is not allowed to kill animals unnecessarily or without restrictions. The Vedas regulate animal-killing to stop the extravagance of foolish men influenced by the modes of passion and ignorance.

Essence. Even flesh-eating kings can hunt only certain animals by scriptural sanction; unnecessary killing isn't allowed. The Vedas regulate even harmful indulgences; ungoverned indulgence isn't tradition, it's defiance.

BG 26.7

Nārada Muni continued to speak to King Prācīnabarhiṣat: My dear King, any person who works according to the directions of the Vedic scriptures does not become involved in fruitive activities.

Essence. One who works per Vedic directions doesn't get bound by fruitive activity. The rules aren't restrictive overhead; they're the path out of bondage.

BG 26.8

Otherwise, a person who acts whimsically falls down due to false prestige. Thus he becomes involved in the laws of nature, which are composed of the three qualities [goodness, passion and ignorance]. In this way a living entity becomes devoid of his real intelligence and becomes perpetually lost in the cycle of birth and death. Thus he goes up and down from a microbe in stool to a high position in the Brahmaloka planet.

Essence. Whimsical action through false prestige drops the actor into modes and modes' bondage; real intelligence is lost. Without anchoring rules, even smart people get lost in mode-traffic.

BG 26.9

When King Puraṣjana was hunting in this way, many animals within the forest lost their lives with great pain, being pierced by the sharp arrowheads. Upon seeing these devastating, ghastly activities performed by the King, all the people who were merciful by nature became very unhappy. Such merciful persons could not tolerate seeing all this killing.

Essence. Animals died in great pain; compassionate witnesses became unhappy. Devotees are pained by demoniac killing; their pain is real, even if uninvited.

BG 26.10

In this way King Puraṣjana killed many animals, including rabbits, boars, buffalo, bison, black deer, porcupines and other game animals. After killing and killing, the King became very tired.

Essence. Rabbits, boars, buffalo, bison, deer, porcupines — many killed; the king grew tired. Indulgence in ignorance eventually exhausts itself; even villainy has fatigue.

BG 26.11

After this, the King, very much fatigued, hungry and thirsty, returned to his royal palace. After returning, he took a bath and had an appropriate dinner. Then he took rest and thus became freed from all restlessness.

Essence. Fatigued, hungry, thirsty, he returned, bathed, dined, rested — freed from restlessness. The materialist's weekend: cycle of intense work followed by ritualized recovery, no spiritual movement.

BG 26.12

After this, King Puraṣjana decorated his body with suitable ornaments. He also smeared scented sandalwood pulp over his body and put on flower garlands. In this way he became completely refreshed. After this, he began to search out his Queen.

Essence. He decorated himself with ornaments, sandalwood, garlands, then searched for the queen. Cleaned up, body-fresh — but the search now is for sense-resumption, not for clarity.

BG 26.13

After taking his dinner and having his thirst and hunger satisfied, King Puraṣjana felt some joy within his heart. Instead of being elevated to a higher consciousness, he became captivated by Cupid, and was moved by a desire to find his wife, who kept him satisfied in his household life.

Essence. After dinner, instead of rising to higher consciousness, he was captivated by Cupid and moved to find his wife. The post-rest moment can launch a soul up or send it back down; choose deliberately.

BG 26.14

At that time King Puraṣjana was a little anxious, and he inquired from the household women: My dear beautiful women, are you and your mistress all very happy like before, or not?

Essence. He asked the household women: are you all happy as before? Inquiry after the queen is partial wisdom; consulting devotees-of-the-house can re-orient.

BG 26.15

King Puraṣjana said: I do not understand why my household paraphernalia does not attract me as before. I think that if there is neither a mother nor devoted wife at home, the home is like a chariot without wheels. Where is the fool who will sit down on such an unworkable chariot?

Essence. Without mother or devoted wife, home is a chariot without wheels — useless. The household's essential figures provide motion; without them, the vehicle won't roll.

BG 26.16

Kindly let me know the whereabouts of that beautiful woman who always saves me when I am drowning in the ocean of danger. By giving me good intelligence at every step, she always saves me.

Essence. Tell me where she is — she saves me by giving good intelligence at every step. He correctly identifies the queen as his rescuer; this recognition is the start of return.

BG 26.17

All the women addressed the King: O master of the citizens, we do not know why your dear wife has taken on this sort of existence. O killer of enemies, kindly look! She is lying on the ground without bedding. We cannot understand why she is acting this way.

Essence. The women report: she's lying on the ground without bedding — we don't know why. Neglected intelligence behaves like a mendicant; the soul's absence reduces her to ground-level.

BG 26.18

The great sage Nārada continued: My dear King Prācīnabarhi, as soon as King Puraṣjana saw his Queen lying on the ground, appearing like a mendicant, he immediately became bewildered.

Essence. Seeing her on the ground like a mendicant, he became bewildered. The visible degradation of what he neglected finally registers; bewilderment is the first form of remorse.

BG 26.19

The King, with aggrieved mind, began to speak to his wife with very pleasing words. Although he was filled with regret and tried to pacify her, he could not see any symptom of anger caused by love within the heart of his beloved wife.

Essence. With pleasing words he tried to pacify her; she showed no symptom of love-anger in her heart. Some wounds don't even register the apology; they've gone past anger to indifference.

BG 26.20

Because the King was very expert in flattery, he began to pacify his Queen very slowly. First he touched her two feet, then embraced her nicely, seating her on his lap, and began to speak as follows.

Essence. Expert in flattery, he touched her feet, embraced her, sat her on his lap, and spoke. Reconciliation begins with bodily gestures of humility, not with explanations.

BG 26.21

King Puraṣjana said: My dear beautiful wife, when a master accepts a servant as his own man, but does not punish him for his offenses, the servant must be considered unfortunate.

Essence. A master who doesn't punish his offending servant makes the servant unfortunate. Punishment of those you love is part of caring for them; absence of it produces drift.

BG 26.22

My dear slender maiden, when a master chastises his servant, the servant should accept this as great mercy. One who becomes angry must be very foolish not to know that such is the duty of his friend.

Essence. When a master chastises a servant, the servant should accept it as mercy; the angry servant is foolish. Inverted: when wisdom 'chastises' the soul, the soul should receive it as mercy, not insult.

BG 26.23

My dear wife, your teeth are very beautifully set, and your attractive features make you appear very thoughtful. Kindly give up your anger, be merciful upon me, and please smile upon me with loving attachment. When I see a smile on your beautiful face, and when I see your hair, which is as beautiful as the color blue, and see your raised nose and hear your sweet talk, you will become more beautiful to me and thus attract me and oblige me. You are my most respected mistress.

Essence. Your teeth are beautiful, your thoughtful features, your hair — please smile, give up anger. Even desperate flattery often misses the actual wound; sweet words can't reach what required earlier action.

BG 26.24

O hero’s wife, kindly tell me if someone has offended you. I am prepared to give such a person punishment as long as he does not belong to the brāhmaṇa caste. But for the servant of Muraripu [Kṛṣṇa], I excuse no one within or beyond these three worlds. No one can freely move after offending you, for I am prepared to punish him.

Essence. If anyone has offended you, I'll punish them (except a brāhmaṇa or Vaiṣṇava). The honorable man identifies whose abuse he won't tolerate — and also names the categories he won't act against.

BG 26.25

My dear wife, until this day I have never seen your face without tilaka decorations, nor have I seen you so morose and without luster or affection. Nor have I seen your two nice breasts wet with tears from your eyes. Nor have I ever before seen your lips, which are ordinarily as red as the bimba fruit, without their reddish hue.

Essence. I've never seen you without tilaka, this morose, your breasts tear-wet, your bimba-red lips faded. Detailed observation of a beloved's distress is itself a kind of apology; you notice what you neglected.

BG 26.26

My dear Queen, due to my sinful desires I went to the forest to hunt without asking you. Therefore I must admit that I have offended you. Nonetheless, thinking of me as your most intimate subordinate, you should still be very much pleased with me. Factually I am very much bereaved, but being pierced by the arrow of Cupid, I am feeling lusty. But where is the beautiful woman who would give up her lusty husband and refuse to unite with him?

Essence. I went hunting without asking you; I admit my offense; please regard me as your intimate subordinate again. The right apology names the offense, the right relationship, and the request for restoration.
