Chapter 28 · Class prep

BG 28.1

The great sage Nārada continued: My dear King Prācīnabarhiṣat, afterward the King of the Yavanas, whose name is fear itself, as well as Prajvāra, Kālakanyā, and his soldiers, began to travel all over the world.

Essence. Fear-king, Prajvāra, Kālakanyā and the soldiers traveled the world. The trio of forces — fear, fever, old age — campaigns globally; no one is exempt from their reach.

BG 28.2

Once the dangerous soldiers attacked the city of Puraṣjana with great force. Although the city was full of paraphernalia for sense gratification, it was being protected by the old serpent.

Essence. The soldiers attacked the city with force; the old serpent (vital force) still protected it. The defenders hold the line longer than expected; thank them while they fight.

BG 28.3

Gradually Kālakanyā, with the help of dangerous soldiers, attacked all the inhabitants of Puraṣjana’s city and thus rendered them useless for all purposes.

Essence. Kālakanyā with soldiers rendered the inhabitants useless. Old age progressively retires the body's working components; usefulness expires section by section.

BG 28.4

When Kālakanyā, daughter of Time, attacked the body, the dangerous soldiers of the King of the Yavanas entered the city through different gates. They then began to give severe trouble to all the citizens.

Essence. Soldiers entered through the nine gates and gave trouble to all citizens. The body's nine portals are the entry points for disease; what enters there enters everywhere.

BG 28.5

When the city was thus endangered by the soldiers and Kālakanyā, King Puraṣjana, being overly absorbed in affection for his family, was placed in difficulty by the attack of Yavana-rāja and Kālakanyā.

Essence. Absorbed in family affection, Purañjana was placed in difficulty. The deepest absorptions hurt most when they're disrupted; family-attachment compounds the suffering.

BG 28.6

When King Puraṣjana was embraced by Kālakanyā, he gradually lost all his beauty. Having been too much addicted to sex, he became very poor in intelligence and lost all his opulence. Being bereft of all possessions, he was conquered forcibly by the Gandharvas and the Yavanas.

Essence. Embraced by Kālakanyā, his beauty faded, intelligence weakened, opulence vanished; he was conquered. Sex addiction past prime makes all losses simultaneous; the package deal is brutal.

BG 28.7

King Puraṣjana then saw that everything in his town was scattered and that his sons, grandsons, servants and ministers were all gradually opposing him. He also noted that his wife was becoming cold and indifferent.

Essence. Town scattered, sons and grandsons gradually opposing, wife cold and indifferent. Internal organs and faculties go from cooperative to oppositional; even your own body turns against you.

BG 28.8

When King Puraṣjana saw that all his family members, relatives, followers, servants, secretaries and everyone else had turned against him, he certainly became very anxious. But he could not counteract the situation because he was thoroughly overwhelmed by Kālakanyā.

Essence. Everyone turned against him; he became anxious but couldn't counteract Kālakanyā. Some losses can't be reversed by anxiety; old age must be received, not resisted.

BG 28.9

The objects of enjoyment became stale by the influence of Kālakanyā. Due to the continuance of his lusty desires, King Puraṣjana became very poor in everything. Thus he did not understand the aim of life. He was still very affectionate toward his wife and children, and he worried about maintaining them.

Essence. Enjoyment-objects became stale; lust persisted; he still worried about family and didn't understand life's aim. Stale enjoyment with persistent craving is a hellish loop; aim has to be rediscovered.

BG 28.10

The city of King Puraṣjana was overcome by the Gandharva and Yavana soldiers, and although the King had no desire to leave the city, he was circumstantially forced to do so, for it was smashed by Kālakanyā.

Essence. The city was overcome; though he didn't want to leave, he was forced out by Kālakanyā. Departure isn't optional eventually; the question is whether you arrange or are dragged.

BG 28.11

Under the circumstances, the elder brother of Yavana-rāja, known as Prajvāra, set fire to the city to please his younger brother, whose other name is fear itself.

Essence. Prajvāra (final fever) set fire to the city to please his younger brother (fear). The final illness is itself an offering to fear; the deathbed-fire is a recognized stage.

BG 28.12

When the city was set ablaze, all the citizens and servants of the King, as well as all family members, sons, grandsons, wives and other relatives, were within the fire. King Puraṣjana thus became very unhappy.

Essence. All citizens, servants, family members were in the fire; Purañjana was unhappy. Family being burned-with-you is the deepest grief; the totality of loss arrives at once.

BG 28.13

The city’s superintendent of police, the serpent, saw that the citizens were being attacked by Kālakanyā, and he became very aggrieved to see his own residence set ablaze after being attacked by the Yavanas.

Essence. The serpent (vital force) saw the citizens attacked and his residence burning; he was aggrieved. Even the vital force grieves at its own dissolution; the principle of life is sentient.

BG 28.14

As a serpent living within the cavity of a tree wishes to leave when there is a forest fire, so the city’s police superintendent, the snake, wished to leave the city due to the fire’s severe heat.

Essence. Like a snake in a tree-hollow during a forest fire, the serpent wished to leave the burning city. The vital force naturally seeks to evacuate; you don't have to push it out, you just have to let it go.

BG 28.15

The limbs of the serpent’s body were slackened by the Gandharvas and Yavana soldiers, who had thoroughly defeated his bodily strength. When he attempted to leave the body, he was checked by his enemies. Being thus baffled in his attempt, he began to cry loudly.

Essence. His limbs slackened by soldiers; he was checked from leaving and cried loudly. Diseases sometimes block even the soul's exit; the last stage can be technical, not just emotional.

BG 28.16

King Puraṣjana then began to think of his daughters, sons, grandsons, daughters-in-law, sons-in-law, servants and other associates, as well as his house, his household paraphernalia and his little accumulation of wealth.

Essence. He thought of daughters, sons, grandsons, in-laws, servants, house and little wealth. The dying mind reaches for the smallest details; nothing is too small to be clung to at the end.

BG 28.17

King Puraṣjana was overly attached to his family and conceptions of “I” and “mine.” Because he was overly attracted to his wife, he was already quite poverty-stricken. At the time of separation, he became very sorry.

Essence. Overly attached to 'I' and 'mine,' he was already poverty-stricken; separation hurt severely. Even before separation, attachment-as-poverty has set in; the suffering precedes the loss.

BG 28.18

King Puraṣjana was anxiously thinking, “Alas, my wife is encumbered by so many children. When I pass from this body, how will she be able to maintain all these family members? Alas, she will be greatly harassed by thoughts of family maintenance.”

Essence. Alas, how will my wife maintain all these family members after I'm gone? Dying worry centers on survivors' welfare; it's noble in flavor and bondage-producing in effect.

BG 28.19

King Puraṣjana then began to think of his past dealings with his wife. He recalled that his wife would not take her dinner until he had finished his, that she would not take her bath until he had finished his, and that she was always very much attached to him, so much so that if he would sometimes become angry and chastise her, she would simply remain silent and tolerate his misbehavior.

Essence. He recalled she wouldn't dine or bathe until he had — utterly devoted. Memories of a beloved's devotion intensify the bond at the end; nothing accidental about how scripture details this.

BG 28.20

King Puraṣjana continued thinking how, when he was in a state of bewilderment, his wife would give him good counsel and how she would become aggrieved when he was away from home. Although she was the mother of so many sons and heroes, the King still feared that she would not be able to maintain the responsibility of household affairs.

Essence. She gave counsel; mourned absence; mother of many heroes — but he feared she wouldn't manage alone. Polluted consciousness at death takes the form of caring about exactly the wrong things.

BG 28.21

King Puraṣjana continued worrying: “After I pass from this world, how will my sons and daughters, who are now fully dependent on me, live and continue their lives? Their position will be similar to that of passengers aboard a ship wrecked in the midst of the ocean.”

Essence. How will my sons and daughters live? — Like passengers on a wrecked ship. The shipwreck metaphor is accurate; without proper preparation, dependents are exactly that vulnerable.

BG 28.22

Although King Puraṣjana should not have lamented over the fate of his wife and children, he nonetheless did so due to his miserly intelligence. In the meantime, Yavana-rāja, whose name was fear itself, immediately drew near to arrest him.

Essence. He shouldn't have lamented but did from miserly intelligence; Yavana-rāja approached to arrest. Lament beyond what's productive becomes the doorway through which Fear arrests you.

BG 28.23

When the Yavanas were taking King Puraṣjana away to their place, binding him like an animal, the King’s followers became greatly aggrieved. While they lamented, they were forced to go along with him.

Essence. They took him away bound like an animal; his followers lamented as they were forced along. At death, the senses and life-airs go along with the soul; the procession is not solitary.

BG 28.24

The serpent, who had already been arrested by the soldiers of Yavana-rāja and was out of the city, began to follow his master along with the others. As soon as they all left the city, it was immediately dismantled and smashed to dust.

Essence. The serpent followed; the city was immediately dismantled and smashed to dust. Once the king and his faculties leave, the city collapses immediately; the body is held up only by the resident.

BG 28.25

When King Puraṣjana was being dragged with great force by the powerful Yavana, out of his gross ignorance he still could not remember his friend and well-wisher, the Supersoul.

Essence. Out of gross ignorance, he still couldn't remember his friend Supersoul. The biggest loss at the deathbed is failing to remember the Lord; the rest is replaceable.

BG 28.26

That most unkind king, Puraṣjana, had killed many animals in various sacrifices. Now, taking advantage of this opportunity, all these animals began to pierce him with their horns. It was as though he were being cut to pieces by axes.

Essence. The animals he had killed in sacrifices pierced him with horns; he was cut to pieces. Karma keeps its appointments at death; nothing 'in the name of religion' actually escapes accounting.

BG 28.27

Due to his contaminated association with women, a living entity like King Puraṣjana eternally suffers all the pangs of material existence and remains in the dark region of material life, bereft of all remembrance for many, many years.

Essence. Contaminated by association with women, he suffers many years in the dark region of material life. The penalty is multi-life and dark; sense-attachment isn't a single-life mistake.

BG 28.28

King Puraṣjana gave up his body while remembering his wife, and consequently in his next life he became a very beautiful and well-situated woman. He took his next birth as the daughter of King Vidarbha in the very house of the King.

Essence. Remembering his wife at death, he became a beautiful woman — daughter of King Vidarbha. What you think of at death determines your next body literally; scripture is technical, not metaphorical.

BG 28.29

It was fixed that Vaidarbhī, daughter of King Vidarbha, was to be married to a very powerful man, Malayadhvaja, an inhabitant of the Pāṇḍu country. After conquering other princes, he married the daughter of King Vidarbha.

Essence. Vaidarbhī was fixed to be married to Malayadhvaja of the Pāṇḍu country, who conquered other princes and married her. Fate sometimes provides a better match in the next life; not all karmic returns are punitive.

BG 28.30

King Malayadhvaja fathered one daughter, who had very black eyes. He also had seven sons, who later became rulers of that tract of land known as Draviḍa. Thus there were seven kings in that land.

Essence. Malayadhvaja had a black-eyed daughter and seven sons who became rulers of Draviḍa. The line produces leaders; one good marriage seeds an entire region's stewardship.

BG 28.31

My dear King Prācīnabarhiṣat, the sons of Malayadhvaja gave birth to many thousands and thousands of sons, and all of these have been protecting the entire world up to the end of one Manu’s life span and even afterward.

Essence. His grandsons protected the world up to and after one Manu's span. The line keeps protecting through cosmic ages; dharmic lineages outlast individuals.

BG 28.32

The great sage named Agastya married the first-born daughter of Malayadhvaja, the avowed devotee of Lord Kṛṣṇa. From her one son was born, whose name was Dṛḍhacyuta, and from him another son was born, whose name was Idhmavāha.

Essence. Agastya married the eldest daughter; from her came Dṛḍhacyuta, then Idhmavāha. The mind (Agastya) married to discriminating intellect produces firm-resolve and offering-bearer; faculties have offspring.

BG 28.33

After this, the great saintly King Malayadhvaja divided his entire kingdom among his sons. Then, in order to worship Lord Kṛṣṇa with full attention, he went to a solitary place known as Kulācala.

Essence. Malayadhvaja divided his kingdom among his sons and went alone to Kulācala to worship Kṛṣṇa. The mature exit involves dividing what you've built and going to a quiet place; both halves are necessary.

BG 28.34

Just as the moonshine follows the moon at night, immediately after King Malayadhvaja departed for Kulācala, his devoted wife, whose eyes were very enchanting, followed him, giving up all homely happiness, despite family and children.

Essence. Like moonshine follows the moon, his devoted wife followed despite home and children. The right spouse follows even into vānaprastha; some bonds belong on the next mountain.

BG 28.35-36

In the province of Kulācala, there were rivers named Candravasā, Tāmraparṇī and Vaṭodakā. King Malayadhvaja used to go to those pious rivers regularly and take his bath there. Thus he purified himself externally and internally. He took his bath and ate bulbs, seeds, leaves, flowers, roots, fruits and grasses and drank water. In this way he underwent severe austerities. Eventually he became very skinny.

Essence. He bathed in Candravasā, Tāmraparṇī, Vaṭodakā; ate bulbs, seeds, leaves; gradually his body grew thin. Forest-life is sequenced; bathing-eating-thinning, in that order, transforms the body for the soul.

BG 28.37

Through austerity, King Malayadhvaja in body and mind gradually became equal to the dualities of cold and heat, happiness and distress, wind and rain, hunger and thirst, the pleasant and the unpleasant. In this way he conquered all relativities.

Essence. By austerity he became equal to cold/heat, happiness/distress, hunger/thirst — conquering relativity. The duals collapse under sustained practice; you don't need new circumstances, you need transformed relativity.

BG 28.38

By worshiping, executing austerities and following the regulative principles, King Malayadhvaja conquered his senses, his life and his consciousness. Thus he fixed everything on the central point of the Supreme Brahman [Kṛṣṇa].

Essence. By worship, austerity and rules, he conquered senses, life, consciousness; fixed everything on Kṛṣṇa. Many practices converging on one focal point; that's the design of a successful spiritual life.

BG 28.39

In this way he stayed immovable in one place for one hundred years by the calculation of the demigods. After this time, he developed pure devotional attraction for Kṛṣṇa, the Supreme Personality of Godhead, and remained fixed in that position.

Essence. He stayed immovable for 100 demigod-years and developed pure devotional attraction for Kṛṣṇa. Pure devotion can take a very long time; not all aspirants get there fast.

BG 28.40

King Malayadhvaja attained perfect knowledge by being able to distinguish the Supersoul from the individual soul. The individual soul is localized, whereas the Supersoul is all-pervasive. He became perfect in knowledge that the material body is not the soul but that the soul is the witness of the material body.

Essence. He attained perfect knowledge by distinguishing Supersoul from individual soul. Distinction-keeping is the breakthrough; conflation looks easy but blocks the realization.

BG 28.41

In this way King Malayadhvaja attained perfect knowledge because in his pure state he was directly instructed by the Supreme Personality of Godhead. By means of such enlightening transcendental knowledge, he could understand everything from all angles of vision.

Essence. He attained knowledge directly instructed by the Lord; from that he could understand everything. Direct divine instruction beats all study; once it begins, comprehension generalizes.

BG 28.42

King Malayadhvaja could thus observe that the Supersoul was sitting by his side, and that he, as the individual soul, was sitting by the side of the Supersoul. Since both were together, there was no need for separate interests; thus he ceased from such activities.

Essence. He saw Supersoul sitting by his side; no separate interests; he ceased separate activities. When two interests are seen as one, separate striving dissolves; no need to manage two agendas.

BG 28.43

The daughter of King Vidarbha accepted her husband all in all as the Supreme. She gave up all sensual enjoyment and in complete renunciation followed the principles of her husband, who was so advanced. Thus she remained engaged in his service.

Essence. Vaidarbhī accepted her husband as Supreme, gave up sense-enjoyment, followed him in renunciation. The disciple accepts the master as ultimate; that acceptance enables renunciation, not the other way around.

BG 28.44

The daughter of King Vidarbha wore old garments, and she was lean and thin because of her vows of austerity. Since she did not arrange her hair, it became entangled and twisted in locks. Although she remained always near her husband, she was as silent and unagitated as the flame of an undisturbed fire.

Essence. Old garments, lean body, matted hair — silent, unagitated as a flameless fire. The fully transformed disciple ceases external symptoms of struggle; the fire just glows.

BG 28.45

The daughter of King Vidarbha continued as usual to serve her husband, who was seated in a steady posture, until she could ascertain that he had passed away from the body.

Essence. She continued serving until she could ascertain her husband had passed. Service continues through the master's transition; you don't quit at the visible end.

BG 28.46

While she was serving her husband by massaging his legs, she could feel that his feet were no longer warm and could thus understand that he had already passed from the body. She felt great anxiety upon being left alone. Bereft of her husband’s company, she felt exactly as the deer feels upon being separated from its mate.

Essence. Massaging his legs, she felt his feet cold; recognized he was gone; felt like a deer separated from its mate. The detection point is bodily; the lament that follows is appropriate; both are part of the practice.

BG 28.47

Being now alone and a widow in that forest, the daughter of Vidarbha began to lament, incessantly shedding tears, which soaked her breasts, and crying very loudly.

Essence. Alone and widowed, she lamented loudly, tears soaking her breasts. Loud grief is appropriate when the master passes; restrained mourning is not the higher form.

BG 28.48

O best of kings, please get up! Get up! Just see this world surrounded by water and infested with rogues and so-called kings. This world is very much afraid, and it is your duty to protect her.

Essence. Get up! Get up! The world is surrounded by water and rogues; it's your duty to protect her. Even in mourning, the disciple invokes the master's role as protector; the call to duty doesn't go silent.

BG 28.49

That most obedient wife thus fell down at the feet of her dead husband and began to cry pitifully in that solitary forest. Thus the tears rolled down from her eyes.

Essence. The obedient wife fell at her dead husband's feet, weeping pitifully. Falling-at-feet remains the right posture even after death; reverence outlasts breath.

BG 28.50

She then prepared a blazing fire with firewood and placed the dead body of her husband upon it. When this was finished, she lamented severely and prepared herself to perish in the fire with her husband.

Essence. She built a blazing pyre, placed the body, and prepared to perish with him. Saha-maraṇa was a real spiritual practice; the disciple's willingness to enter the same fire is the highest fidelity.

BG 28.51

My dear King, one brāhmaṇa, who was an old friend of King Puraṣjana, came to that place and began to pacify the Queen with sweet words.

Essence. An old brāhmaṇa friend of Purañjana came and pacified the queen with sweet words. The Supersoul (Kṛṣṇa) appears as old brāhmaṇa friend at the most desperate moments; the heart's intimate friend is always nearby.

BG 28.52

The brāhmaṇa inquired as follows: Who are you? Whose wife or daughter are you? Who is the man lying here? It appears you are lamenting for this dead body. Don’t you recognize Me? I am your eternal friend. You may remember that many times in the past you have consulted Me.

Essence. Who are you, whose wife? Don't you recognize Me, your eternal friend you've consulted many times? The Supersoul names His prior visibility; we've consulted Him before and forgotten.

BG 28.53

The brāhmaṇa continued: My dear friend, even though you cannot immediately recognize Me, can’t you remember that in the past you had a very intimate friend? Unfortunately, you gave up My company and accepted a position as enjoyer of this material world.

Essence. You had a very intimate friend, but you gave up My company to enjoy the material world. The fall begins with abandonment of an existing relationship; you were never alone, you just left.

BG 28.54

My dear gentle friend, both you and I are exactly like two swans. We live together in the same heart, which is just like the Mānasa Lake. Although we have been living together for many thousands of years, we are still far away from our original home.

Essence. You and I are like two swans in the same heart-lake; we've lived together for thousands of years but are far from our original home. Cohabitation is real; relocation is the work — same lake, wrong continent.

BG 28.55

My dear friend, you are now My very same friend. Since you left Me, you have become more and more materialistic, and not seeing Me, you have been traveling in different forms throughout this material world, which was created by some woman.

Essence. Since leaving Me you've been traveling in different forms in this world, made by some woman (māyā). The material world is itself maternal — māyā's product — not random and not yours.

BG 28.56

In that city [the material body] there are five gardens, nine gates, one protector, three apartments, six families, five stores, five material elements, and one woman who is lord of the house.

Essence. Five gardens, nine gates, one protector, three apartments, six families, five stores, five elements, one woman lord. The body's anatomy as a city: enumerate its inhabitants to understand who you are.

BG 28.57

My dear friend, the five gardens are the five objects of sense enjoyment, and the protector is the life air, which passes through the nine gates. The three apartments are the chief ingredients — fire, water and earth. The six families are the aggregate total of the mind and five senses.

Essence. Five gardens are sense-objects; protector is life-air; three apartments are fire-water-earth; six families are mind plus five senses. Each element of the parable has a body-system referent; the figure is precise.

BG 28.58

The five stores are the five working sensory organs. They transact their business through the combined forces of the five elements, which are eternal. Behind all this activity is the soul. The soul is a person and an enjoyer in reality. However, because he is now hidden within the city of the body, he is devoid of knowledge.

Essence. Five stores are working organs; the soul is the actual enjoyer but hidden in the body. The enjoyer is hidden; misidentification with stores and gardens is the standard confusion.

BG 28.59

My dear friend, when you enter such a body along with the woman of material desires, you become overly absorbed in sense enjoyment. Because of this, you have forgotten your spiritual life. Due to your material conceptions, you are placed in various miserable conditions.

Essence. You entered with the woman of material desires and got absorbed in sense-enjoyment, forgetting spiritual life. The entry-with-desires is the moment of forgetting; remember who you arrived as.

BG 28.60

Actually, you are not the daughter of Vidarbha, nor is this man, Malayadhvaja, your well-wishing husband. Nor were you the actual husband of Puraṣjanī. You were simply captivated in this body of nine gates.

Essence. You are not actually Vidarbha's daughter; Malayadhvaja isn't your husband; you weren't Purañjanī's husband either. The identities you held were captivations of a nine-gated body; none of them were ultimate.

BG 28.61

Sometimes you think yourself a man, sometimes a chaste woman and sometimes a neutral eunuch. This is all because of the body, which is created by the illusory energy. This illusory energy is My potency, and actually both of us — you and I — are pure spiritual identities. Now just try to understand this. I am trying to explain our factual position.

Essence. Sometimes you think yourself man, sometimes woman, sometimes eunuch — all because of the māyā-made body. Gender and identity at the body-level are temporary roles; the spirit is none of them.

BG 28.62

My dear friend, I, the Supersoul, and you, the individual soul, are not different in quality, for we are both spiritual. In fact, My dear friend, you are qualitatively not different from Me in your constitutional position. Just try to consider this subject. Those who are actually advanced scholars, who are in knowledge, do not find any qualitative difference between you and Me.

Essence. You, individual soul, and I, Supersoul, are qualitatively one — both spiritual — though quantitatively distinct. Quality matches; quantity doesn't. Keep both halves of that statement together.

BG 28.63

As a person sees the reflection of his body in a mirror to be one with himself and not different, whereas others actually see two bodies, so in our material condition, in which the living being is affected and yet not affected, there is a difference between God and the living entity.

Essence. Like a body and its mirror reflection — same shape but different bodies — God and the living entity are in apparent unity with subtle distinction. The reflection isn't the original; oneness has a structure.

BG 28.64

In this way both swans live together in the heart. When the one swan is instructed by the other, he is situated in his constitutional position. This means he regains his original Kṛṣṇa consciousness, which was lost because of his material attraction.

Essence. Two swans in the heart; when one (Supersoul) instructs the other (jīva), the jīva returns to its constitutional position — Kṛṣṇa consciousness. Instruction from within is the path back; the conversation is internal.

BG 28.65

My dear King Prācīnabarhi, the Supreme Personality of Godhead, the cause of all causes, is celebrated to be known indirectly. Thus I have described the story of Puraṣjana to you. Actually it is an instruction for self-realization.

Essence. Supreme Lord is celebrated to be known indirectly — through parable. The whole Purañjana story is an instruction for self-realization. Some teachings only work as story; resist the urge for direct doctrine.
