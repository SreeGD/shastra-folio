Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 11.7.74

य: प्राप्य मानुषं लोकं मुक्तिद्वारमपावृतम् । गृहेषु खगवत् सक्तस्तमारूढच्युतं विदु: ॥ ७४ ॥

The doors of liberation are opened wide to one who has achieved human life. But if a human being simply devotes himself to family life like the foolish bird in this story, then he is to be considered as one who has climbed to a high place only to trip and fall down.
Purport

Thus end the purports of the humble servants of His Divine Grace A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda to the Eleventh Canto, Seventh Chapter, of the Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam, entitled “Lord Kṛṣṇa Instructs Uddhava.”

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
The doors of liberation are opened wide to one who has achieved human life. But if a human being simply devotes himself to family life like the foolish bird in this story, then he is considered to be like a person who, having climbed to a high place, falls down. Thus ends the commentary on Seventh Chapter of the Eleventh Canto of the Bhāgavatam for the pleasure of the devotees, in accordance with the previous ācāryas. Chapter Eight The Story of Piṅgalā 11.8: The Story of Piṅgalā 8. The Story of Piṅgalā 11.8 Summary Lord Kṛṣṇa told Uddhava how the avadhūta brāhmaṇa explained to Mahārāja Yadu the instructions he had received from nine of his gurus, beginning with the python. The instruction the avadhūta brāhmaṇa received from the python is that an intelligent person should cultivate a mentality of detachment and should maintain his body by accepting whatever comes of its own accord or is easily obtained. In this way, he should remain always engaged in the worship of the Supreme Personality of Godhead. Even if no food is available, the person who wants to engage fully in the Lord's worship should not beg; rather, he should understand this to be the arrangement of providence, thinking, "Whatever enjoyment is destined for me will automatically come, and thus I should not uselessly waste the remaining duration of my life in worrying about such things." If he does not get any food, he should simply remain lying like the python and patiently fix his mind in meditation upon the Supreme Lord. The instruction the avadhūta brāhmaṇa received from the ocean is that the mind of the sage who is devoted to the Personality of Godhead appears very clear and grave, just like the still ocean waters. The ocean does not overflow during the rainy season, when all the flooded rivers discharge their waters into it, nor does it dry up during the hot season, when the rivers fail to supply it. Similarly, the sage does not become elated when he achieves desirable things, nor does he become distressed in their absence. The instruction of the moth is that just as he becomes enticed by the fire and gives up his life, the fool who cannot control his senses becomes enchanted by the forms of women decorated with gold ornaments and fine clothing. Chasing after these embodiments of the divine illusory energy of the Lord, he loses his life untimely and falls down into the most horrible hell. There are two kinds of bees, the bumblebee and the honeybee. The instruction learned from the bumblebee is that a sage should collect only small amounts of food from many different households and thus day after day practice the occupation of mādhukarī for maintaining his existence. A sage should also collect the essential truths from all scriptures, be they great or insignificant. The instruction received from the second insect, the honeybee, is that a mendicant sannyāsī should not save the food he begs for the sake of having it later that night or the next day, because if he does so, then just like the greedy honeybee he will be destroyed along with his hoard. From the elephant the avadhūta brāhmaṇa received the following instruction. Male elephants are tricked by hunters into moving toward captive female elephants, whereupon they fall into the hunters' ditch and are captured. Similarly, the man who becomes attached to the form of woman falls down into the deep well of material life and is destroyed. The instruction received from the honey thief is that just as he steals the honey collected with great effort by the honeybee, a person in the renounced order of life has the privilege of enjoying before anyone else the food and other valuable things purchased by the hard-earned money of the householders. The instruction from the deer is that just as he becomes confused upon hearing the song of the hunter's flute and loses his life, so also does any person who becomes attracted to mundane music and song uselessly waste his life. The instruction learned from the fish is that because he comes under the sway of attachment to the sense of taste, he is caught on the baited fishhook and must die. Similarly, an unintelligent person who is victimized by his insatiable tongue will also end up losing his life. There was once a prostitute named Piṅgalā in the city of Videha, and from her the avadhūta learned another lesson. One day she dressed herself in very attractive clothing and ornaments and was waiting from sunset until midnight for a customer. She waited in great anticipation, but as the time passed her mind became very uneasy. No man came to see her, and in disgust she finally became renounced, giving up her hankering for the arrival of a suitor. Thereafter she engaged herself in thinking only of the Supreme Lord, Hari, and her mind achieved the supreme platform of peace. The instruction received from her is that hopes for sense gratification are the root cause of all suffering. Therefore, only one who has given up such hankering can fix himself in meditation upon the Personality of Godhead and achieve transcendental peace.
Personal Guidance
Human birth flings the door of mukti wide open; clinging to gṛha like the trapped bird is to climb high only to fall.
Practical Applications
  • Name one household attachment today that you treat as ultimate, and consciously re-rank it beneath your sādhana.
  • Spend the first waking hour in chanting and hearing before the family's demands claim you, so the mukti-dvāra stays in view.
  • Refuse the inner voice that says "after the children, after retirement, then bhakti" — begin the surrendered act now.
Reflection Questions
  • Which specific gṛha-bond am I treating as the goal of human life rather than as a field for serving Kṛṣṇa?
  • Having climbed the rare height of mānuṣa-loka, where am I quietly preparing my own fall?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Notice when peer belonging — the friend group, the relationship, the online circle — starts functioning as your "gṛha"; rank it honestly beneath your sādhana.
  • Use the freedom of this stage, before family responsibilities arrive, to fix daily chanting and hearing as non-negotiable habits.
  • Refuse the script that says spiritual life begins after career and marriage are settled; the mukti-dvāra is open already, in your dorm room or first apartment.
householder
  • Re-examine one family decision this week — a purchase, a move, a child's schedule — and ask whether it serves the soul's journey or only thickens the nest.
  • Schedule sādhana into the family calendar with the same seriousness as work and school, so spouse and children see the door, not just the cage.
  • Speak with your spouse plainly about saktaḥ versus service, so household life becomes shared bhakti rather than mutual entanglement.
senior
  • Begin loosening grip on house, possessions, and roles now — give away, hand over, simplify — before the body forces what the will should have chosen.
  • Mentor a younger householder by sharing where you yourself climbed high and slipped, so they recognize the bird-pattern earlier than you did.
  • Treat each remaining day as the final stretch of the open door, intensifying chanting, hearing, and remembrance of Kṛṣṇa over managing legacy.
Common Pitfalls
  • Mistaking renunciation of family duties for the verse's intent, when the warning is against saktaḥ — attachment, not the āśrama itself.
  • Postponing serious sādhana until household conditions feel "settled," forgetting that human life is itself the open door, not its arrangements.
  • Reading the foolish bird as someone else — a worldly neighbor — instead of recognizing the same saktaḥ pattern in one's own home.
Discussion Prompts
  • Share a concrete gṛha-attachment you have rationalized as duty; let the group test whether it is sva-dharma or saktaḥ.
  • Where do householders in this room disagree about what "climbing high" looks like — career, family stability, or sādhana — and why?
  • If human life is the only door to mukti, what changes today in how you spend the next twenty-four hours, and what would you have to stop?
Role Play

A student tells a senior devotee: "I have a good job, a loving spouse, healthy children, and time for some chanting on weekends — surely this is a balanced life Kṛṣṇa is pleased with." The senior devotee must respond in light of this verse, neither dismissing the householder āśrama nor letting the student stay comfortable in saktaḥ.

Objections & Responses
  • This verse sounds anti-family. Are householders second-class in Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavism, doomed to "fall" simply for loving their spouse and children?
    The verse does not condemn gṛhastha-āśrama — Caitanya Mahāprabhu Himself blessed householders and their service. It condemns saktaḥ, the bird-like clinging that treats home as the goal rather than the field. A householder who serves Kṛṣṇa through family duties has not fallen; one who forgets the mukti-dvāra while inside the house has.
  • Modern psychology says strong family attachment is healthy and necessary for well-being. Why call it a fall?
    Material well-being is not denied; the verse speaks of a rarer opportunity that well-being alone cannot purchase. Human birth uniquely opens the door beyond birth and death. To use this one chance only for emotional comfort inside the cage — however warm the cage — is the fall the verse names, not because attachment feels bad but because the door was open and we did not walk through.
