Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 11.6.31

इदानीं नाश आरब्ध: कुलस्य द्विजशापज: । यास्यामि भवनं ब्रह्मन्नेतदन्ते तवानघ ॥ ३१ ॥

Now due to the brāhmaṇas’ curse, the annihilation of My family has already begun. O sinless Brahmā, when this annihilation is finished and I am enroute to Vaikuṇṭha, I will pay a small visit to your abode.
Purport

The members of the Yadu dynasty are eternal servants of the Lord; therefore Śrīla Jīva Gosvāmī has explained the word nāśaḥ, or “destruction,” as nigūḍhāyāṁ dvārakāyāṁ praveśanam ity arthaḥ: the members of the Yadu dynasty entered into the hidden or confidential Dvārakā in the spiritual world, which is not manifested here on the earth. In other words, Dvārakā, the Lord’s abode, is manifest on the earth, and when the earthly Dvārakā is apparently removed, the eternal Dvārakā in the spiritual world remains as it is. Since the members of the Yadu dynasty are eternal associates of the Lord, there is no question of their destruction. Only our conditioned vision of their manifestation was destroyed. This is the meaning of the word nāśaḥ.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
Now due to the brāhmaṇa’s curse, the disappearance of my family has already begun. O sinless Brahmā, when my family disappears. I will pay a visit to your abode. Nāśaḥ means “entering into invisible Dvārakā.” After they enter Dvārakā, I will go to your abode in the form of the son of Vikuṇṭha. It actually means the planet called Vaikuṇṭha above Brahmaloka. [Note: The avatāra Vaikuṇḥta, son of Vikuṇṭhā, created a planet called Vaikuṇḥta which is situated above Brahmaloka. See Laghu-bhāgavatāmṛta 1.4.12-13. When Kṛṣna appears all the avatāras appear within him, and when he disappears they all return to their abodes. Thus Vaikuṇṭha will return to the planet Vaikuṇṭha above Brahmaloka.] This is the explanation of Jīva Gosvāmī.
Personal Guidance
What looks like the annihilation of Kṛṣṇa's dynasty is really their entry into eternal Dvārakā — His associates are never destroyed, only veiled from our conditioned sight.
Practical Applications
  • When you read of the Yadus' end, refuse the materialist reading — remind yourself the destruction was only of our visible access, not of them.
  • Today, name one loss in your life (a person, a phase, a place) and ask whether what ended was the thing itself or only your conditioned view of it.
  • Before sleep, offer obeisances to the Lord's eternal associates and pray for the vision to see continuity where the senses report disappearance.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my life am I treating an apparent ending as if it were a real annihilation, the way an outsider would read the Yadu-nāśa?
  • What would change in how I grieve, or how I read scripture, if I trusted that the Lord's associates and pastimes are never actually destroyed?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • When a friend group dissolves after school or college, resist the story that 'it's all gone' — ask what was eternal in those bonds and what was only your conditioned view of them.
  • Next time a social media account, a phase of identity, or a public version of yourself ends, sit with the difference between losing the thing and losing your access to it.
  • Pick one verse this week that disturbs you (a curse, a death, a defeat) and read Prabhupāda's purport before deciding what the verse 'really' says.
householder
  • When you watch a parent age, a child leave home, or a career chapter close, refuse the materialist verdict that the relationship is being destroyed — pray to see what is eternal in it.
  • In family conflicts that feel like the end of something, hold the Yadu-nāśa in mind: the Lord uses apparent ruptures to relocate what is real, not to abolish it.
  • Teach your children the Jīva Gosvāmī reading of nāśaḥ the next time a pet dies or grandparents pass — give them, early, the vocabulary of concealment rather than annihilation.
senior
  • As bodily powers and social roles fall away, rehearse this verse: what is being destroyed is the conditioned manifestation, not the soul or its eternal relationships.
  • When peers die, mentor the younger ones who are watching you — show them what it looks like to grieve without believing in annihilation.
  • Make peace, one by one, with the people and places you will soon leave behind, trusting that the Lord conceals His associates rather than ending them — and that the same logic applies to your own departure.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading the destruction of the Yadu dynasty literally and concluding that Kṛṣṇa's associates share the fate of conditioned souls.
  • Using the esoteric meaning of nāśaḥ as spiritual bypass — denying real grief in one's own life by repeating 'nothing is ever destroyed.'
  • Treating the brāhmaṇas' curse as a flaw in the līlā rather than as the Lord's chosen instrument for withdrawing His earthly manifestation.
Discussion Prompts
  • Jīva Gosvāmī reads nāśaḥ as entry into the hidden Dvārakā — share with your partner one passage of scripture where you have been reading destruction where you should have been reading concealment.
  • Kṛṣṇa tells Brahmā He will visit his abode en route to Vaikuṇṭha — what does it say about the Lord's relationships that He announces His departure to a devotee in advance?
  • Disagree productively: is it more dangerous for a student to read the Yadu-nāśa too literally, or to leap to the esoteric reading before sitting with the apparent one?
Role Play

One student plays a serious newcomer who has just read SB 11.6.31 and is shaken — 'If Kṛṣṇa's own family can be destroyed by a curse, what hope is there for us?' The other plays a senior practitioner who must lead them, without dismissing the grief, into Jīva Gosvāmī's reading of nāśaḥ as entry into the hidden Dvārakā.

Objections & Responses
  • This sounds like a later commentator rescuing an embarrassing episode — the plain text says destruction, and Jīva Gosvāmī invents a spiritual reading to save the doctrine.
    The plain text also says Kṛṣṇa is the supreme Personality of Godhead and the Yadus are His eternal associates; if we take that plainly too, then nāśaḥ cannot mean ontological destruction without contradiction. Jīva Gosvāmī is not rescuing the verse from its meaning, he is holding two of its plain claims together — that is what a careful reader of scripture does.
  • If the destruction is only apparent, then the brāhmaṇas' curse accomplished nothing real, and the whole episode becomes theater.
    The curse accomplished exactly what the Lord wished — the withdrawal of His earthly manifestation at the appointed time. It is not theater; it is līlā, in which the Lord uses His own devotees and their words to orchestrate His coming and going. What looks like nothing real to the outside eye is the very mechanism by which He returns to Vaikuṇṭha.
