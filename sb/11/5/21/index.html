Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 11.5.21

कृते शुक्लश्चतुर्बाहुर्जटिलो वल्कलाम्बर: । कृष्णाजिनोपवीताक्षान् बिभ्रद् दण्डकमण्डलू ॥ २१ ॥

In Satya-yuga the Lord is white and four-armed, has matted locks and wears a garment of tree bark. He carries a black deerskin, a sacred thread, prayer beads and the rod and waterpot of a brahmacārī.
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
In Satya-yuga the Lord is white and four-armed, has matted locks and wears a garment of tree bark. He carries a black deerskin, a sacred thread, prayer beads and a rod and water pot. He is white in color and is called Śukla. He wears a dear skin and sacred thread.
Personal Guidance
In Satya-yuga the Lord appears white, four-armed, in bark and matted locks with the brahmacārī's beads and rod — pure austerity made personal.
Practical Applications
  • Pick one small austerity today — skip a snack, a screen hour, a comfort — and offer it as worship of the Satya-yuga form.
  • Touch your prayer beads first thing on waking and let them, not the phone, set the tone of the day.
  • Refuse to dress up an indulgence as a need; name it plainly and let one indulgence go untaken before sundown.
Reflection Questions
  • Which outer simplicity — in clothes, food, speech, possessions — is Kṛṣṇa asking of me that I keep postponing as impractical?
  • Where am I worshiping the Lord's sweetness while quietly refusing the austerity that His Satya-yuga form embodies?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Pick one piece of clothing or one app that you keep mainly to be seen, and go a week without it as a private offering.
  • Before your next exam or interview, sit with your beads for ten minutes instead of doom-scrolling and notice what changes in your nerves.
  • When friends mock simplicity as 'cringe,' don't argue — just keep the practice quietly for thirty days and watch who is steadier.
householder
  • Choose one household expense this month — a subscription, a takeout habit, an upgrade — and redirect it to seva or to a needed family austerity.
  • Eat one meal a week that is deliberately plain, and tell your children why the Lord Himself is honored as one who lives simply.
  • Schedule a fixed daily slot for japa and sādhana the way you schedule meetings, and refuse to let work or family bargain it down.
senior
  • Begin giving away possessions now, while you can still choose recipients — let each item leave with a blessing rather than a will.
  • Wear simpler clothes, eat simpler food, and let younger family see what dhīra-bhāva looks like when the body is visibly aging.
  • Mentor one younger practitioner in chanting on beads, passing on the brahmacārī tools the Satya-yuga form holds — discipline, prayer, restraint.
Common Pitfalls
  • Treating outer austerity as the whole of bhakti and growing proud of bark-cloth and beads while the heart stays untouched.
  • Dismissing the Satya-yuga form as 'just for that age' and using Kali-yuga as an excuse to refuse every renunciation now available.
  • Slipping into impersonalism — admiring 'purity' or 'simplicity' in the abstract instead of worshiping the specific personal Lord described here.
Discussion Prompts
  • The Lord chooses to appear with deerskin, rod, and waterpot — what is He teaching by what He carries, and which item would you find hardest to accept in your own life?
  • Share one austerity you have tried and abandoned: was it bodily impossible, or was the ahaṅkāra unwilling? How would you tell the difference?
  • If each yuga gets the form it can bear, what does it say about us that our age receives the holy name rather than this austere four-armed form?
Role Play

Two students room together. One has begun rising early, keeping simple dress, and chanting on beads; the other accuses them of 'larping as a renunciant' and embarrassing the household. Role-play the conversation: how does the practitioner answer without pride, without retreat, and without flattening the Satya-yuga ideal into mere lifestyle?

Objections & Responses
  • This is a primitive ascetic image — bark clothes and matted hair belong to a vanished age, not to a working adult in the modern world.
    The form is not a costume to copy but a revelation of what Kṛṣṇa Himself honors. We do not wear bark; we let the form teach us that He is pleased by voluntary simplicity, and we apply that in whatever āśrama we actually live in.
  • Why does the same Lord appear differently in different yugas? Doesn't that make Him just a symbol shaped by human projection?
    He is one Person who reciprocates with each age according to what its people can receive. The varying forms are His mercy on changing conditions, not our mythmaking — and that personal reciprocation is itself the point worth surrendering to.
