Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 11.8.7

द‍ृष्ट्वा स्त्रियं देवमायां तद्भ‍ावैरजितेन्द्रिय: । प्रलोभित: पतत्यन्धे तमस्यग्नौ पतङ्गवत् ॥ ७ ॥

One who has failed to control his senses immediately feels attraction upon seeing a woman’s form, which is created by the illusory energy of the Supreme Lord. Indeed, when the woman speaks with enticing words, smiles coquettishly and moves her body sensuously, his mind is immediately captured, and thus he falls blindly into the darkness of material existence, just as the moth maddened by the fire rushes blindly into its flames.
Purport

Śrīla Śrīdhara Svāmī explains in this regard that just as a moth is killed by its attraction to the form of fire, similarly a bee can easily be killed by exploiting its attraction to the fragrance of flowers. Furthermore, hunters may trap and kill an elephant by exploiting its sensuous desire to touch a captive female elephant and may also kill a deer by attracting it with the sounds of their horns; and a fish is killed by its desire to taste the bait on a hook. Thus, one who desires to learn detachment from material illusion should accept these five helpless creatures as guru and learn the needful. Certainly one who is lusty to enjoy the illusory form of woman will be quickly buried in material illusion. Of the five types of fatal attraction involving material sense objects, the lesson of rūpa, or form, is illustrated in this verse.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
A man, lured by his uncontrolled senses, seeing a women created by the Lord’s māyā, falls into the greatest darkness, just as a moth falls into fire. The moth teaches that attachment to form is the cause of destruction. This is explained in two verses.

Personal Guidance
Uncontrolled senses turn the mere sight of an alluring form into a fire — like the moth, the lusty mind rushes blindly into the darkness of material existence.
Practical Applications
  • Notice the first second of attraction today — before words form, redirect the gaze and chant Kṛṣṇa's name as the cut-off.
  • Refuse to feed the loop: cull one specific input (a feed, a show, a chat) that you know functions as the moth's flame for you.
  • Speak to one trusted devotee this week about a viṣaya you struggle with, exposing it to light rather than letting it work in private.
Reflection Questions
  • Which specific 'fire' do I keep circling — knowing it burns me — and what story do I tell myself to justify one more pass?
  • Where am I trusting my own ajita-indriya senses to handle exposure that the verse plainly says will capture me?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Audit your phone tonight — identify the one app or account that functions as your personal flame, and either delete it or hand the password to a friend for a fixed period.
  • Before a party, class trip, or date, fix in advance one line you will not cross and one devotee you will text if the situation tilts — pre-commit rather than negotiate in the moment.
  • Stop calling it 'just curiosity' when you re-open the same tab or DM thread; name it as a moth-circle and bring it up the next time you speak with a senior devotee.
householder
  • Look honestly at the 'forms' your household chases — a lifestyle, a status, a body image — and name one specific way the family is currently flying at that flame.
  • Protect the marriage by guarding the eyes and ears in workplaces and screens; install filters, end private one-on-ones that have started to feel charged, and tell your spouse what you did and why.
  • Build the evening into a sense-cooling routine — kīrtana, reading, lights down, no late-night solo scrolling — so that the day does not end with the senses ungoverned.
senior
  • Do not assume age has burned out the moth's instinct — examine which subtler viṣayas (respect, nostalgia, food, comfort) now play the role lust once did, and confess this honestly to Kṛṣṇa.
  • When mentoring younger devotees, share your actual scorch marks from the flame, not a sanitized version, so they learn the verse from a witness rather than a theorist.
  • Use the loosening of bodily life as the long-promised chance to withdraw the senses from their objects — fewer screens, simpler food, more japa — and let mortality finally do the work of vairāgya.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading the verse as anti-woman rather than as a diagnosis of one's own uncontrolled senses, missing that it applies equally to every form of viṣaya.
  • Treating disgust or moralistic suppression as vairāgya, when the verse calls for sober recognition of māyā and refuge in Kṛṣṇa, not white-knuckled aversion.
  • Assuming intellectual understanding of the moth-and-flame image is itself protection — the verse is precisely about how knowing does not stop the falling.
Discussion Prompts
  • Where in modern life does the 'enticing form' show up for each of us — and do we actually agree on which forms are dangerous, or do we excuse our own?
  • Prabhupāda gives five creatures — moth, bee, elephant, deer, fish — as gurus of detachment; which one most exposes your particular weakness, and why that one?
  • Is the problem the form itself, the senses, or the mind that gets captured — and how does locating the problem change what we actually do tomorrow morning?
Role Play

Two students share a flat. One has just deleted a social media app after recognizing it as their moth-flame; the other says that is extreme, that mature devotees can engage anything without being captured, and offers to show them 'just one funny clip.' Play the conversation — first as it would actually go, then again with the first student responding from the standing of this verse without becoming preachy.

Objections & Responses
  • This verse sounds repressive and unfair to women — it blames the form rather than the man's own choices.
    Read again: the verse names ajita-indriyaḥ, the one who has failed to control his senses, as the falling party. The form is described as deva-māyā, the Lord's illusory energy at work — the diagnosis is of the perceiver's condition, not a verdict on women. The same logic applies to every sense object; rūpa is just the example given here, with the other four creatures covering sound, touch, taste, and smell.
  • Modern psychology says suppression backfires; healthy integration of desire is the mature path, not fearing it like a moth.
    The verse is not prescribing suppression but sober recognition that the senses, untrained, are not neutral instruments — they are already pulled. Bhakti is not denial of desire but its redirection to Kṛṣṇa through sādhana; the moth analogy is simply honest about what happens when an untrained jīva insists it can fly close to the flame and integrate it.
