Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 11.8.11

सायन्तनं श्वस्तनं वा न सङ्गृह्णीत भिक्षितम् । पाणिपात्रोदरामत्रो मक्षिकेव न सङ्ग्रही ॥ ११ ॥

A saintly person should not think, “This food I will keep to eat tonight and this other food I can save for tomorrow.” In other words, a saintly person should not store foodstuffs acquired by begging. Rather, he should use his own hands as his plate and eat whatever fits on them. His only storage container should be his belly, and whatever conveniently fits into his belly should be his stock of food. Thus one should not imitate the greedy honeybee who eagerly collects more and more honey.
Purport

There are two types of honeybees: those who collect nectar from the flowers and those who actually manufacture honey in the beehive. This verse refers to the second class. The greedy honeybee eventually collects so much honey that he is trapped in his hive; and in the same way, a materialistic person becomes trapped under the burden of unnecessary material accumulation. One who is interested in making spiritual progress should avoid such a situation; however, Śrīla Bhaktisiddhānta Sarasvatī Ṭhākura points out that for the purpose of spreading Kṛṣṇa consciousness one may accumulate an unlimited amount of material opulence. This is called yukta-vairāgya, or using everything in the service of Kṛṣṇa. A saintly person who is unable to work in Lord Caitanya’s mission should practice austerities and collect only what he can hold in his hands and belly. However, one who has given his life to Kṛṣṇa may collect unlimitedly on Lord Kṛṣṇa’s behalf. In fact, without acquiring material opulence, how is it possible to spread the Kṛṣṇa consciousness movement all over the world? But if one tries to personally enjoy the funds or facilities acquired for the missionary activities of the Kṛṣṇa consciousness movement, he commits the greatest offense. Therefore, even in the name of Lord Kṛṣṇa, one should collect only what he can immediately engage in practical devotional service; otherwise one will fall down to the platform of ordinary greediness.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
The sage should not collect food, storing it for the evening or the next day, but rather should use his hands as his plate and his belly as his storage vessel. He should not collect and store food as the bee collects honey and stores it. He should not collect food thinking, “I will eat this tonight or tomorrow.” What to speak of thinking of food from the previous day, the previous month or previous full moon. One should not make plans for food one sees, and should also not accept invitations for the evening or next day, according to some authorities. With what vessel should one beg from house to house? One should use one’s hands as a vessel. Having obtained food where should one place it? One should put it in the belly. He has his belly as his storage vessel (amatraḥ).

Personal Guidance
Take only what fits in your hand and belly today; the greedy honeybee, hoarding more than it can use, gets trapped in its own hive.
Practical Applications
  • Audit one category of accumulation today — clothes, gadgets, pantry, savings beyond need — and give away or simplify what exceeds present use.
  • Before any new purchase or stockpiling, ask aloud: is this for Kṛṣṇa's service (yukta-vairāgya) or for my private enjoyment?
  • Eat one meal today with the bhikṣu's attitude — receive only what is given, store nothing for tomorrow, thank Kṛṣṇa it was enough.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my life am I manufacturing honey I will never eat — accumulating for a future enjoyer who never quite arrives?
  • If I had to justify each thing I store as immediately engageable in devotional service, what would I have to release?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Open your phone's storage, photo library, or saved-items folder and delete what you have been hoarding 'just in case' — notice the honeybee impulse in digital form.
  • Before the next sale, drop, or limited edition, wait twenty-four hours and ask whether it serves your sādhana or only your peer image.
  • Try one day this week eating only what is offered or already in the kitchen — no ordering in, no stocking up — and watch what arises when the hoarding option is removed.
householder
  • Sit with your spouse and review one savings or shopping category honestly — what portion serves the family's dharma and Kṛṣṇa's service, what portion is honey you will never eat?
  • When tempted to upgrade house, car, or lifestyle beyond the family's actual need, name the upgrade aloud as honeybee accumulation and choose whether to proceed with eyes open.
  • Teach your children the difference between providing and hoarding by letting them see you give away surplus this week — clothes outgrown, books finished, food extra.
senior
  • Begin actively distributing now what you had planned to leave in a will — books, tools, ornaments, savings — so the giving is conscious, not posthumous.
  • Identify one attachment you have quietly grown around in late life (a room, a routine, a reserve fund) and loosen your grip on it this month as practice for the final loosening.
  • Mentor a younger devotee on the honeybee trap you have personally fallen into and climbed out of — your specific story, not a general warning.
Common Pitfalls
  • Using yukta-vairāgya as a cover story — collecting opulence supposedly for Kṛṣṇa while quietly enjoying it personally, which the purport calls the greatest offense.
  • Imitating the literal renunciation of a bhikṣu while still a householder, neglecting dependents who legitimately rely on your provision.
  • Confusing minimalism as a lifestyle aesthetic with the bhikṣu's actual dependence on Kṛṣṇa — keeping the look of austerity while the heart still hoards.
Discussion Prompts
  • Where is the honest line for you between prudent provision for your āśrama and the honeybee's trapped hoarding — and how did you arrive at it?
  • Prabhupāda says one engaged in Lord Caitanya's mission may collect unlimitedly; one who cannot must take only handful-and-belly. Which side are you actually on, and how would you know if you were deceiving yourself?
  • Share one possession or savings bucket you feel defensive about discussing — what does that defensiveness reveal?
Role Play

One student plays a devotee who has been raising funds for a temple project and has begun keeping a personal reserve 'for emergencies'; the other plays a senior godbrother who has noticed. Run the conversation where the godbrother gently raises the honeybee verse and the fundraiser must respond honestly — not theatrically renounce, not defensively justify.

Objections & Responses
  • This is irresponsible in a modern economy — without savings, retirement accounts, and insurance, you become a burden on family or the state.
    The verse addresses the saintly mendicant, not the householder, and Prabhupāda explicitly preserves yukta-vairāgya — using everything in Kṛṣṇa's service. A householder provides responsibly for dependents and for preaching; what the verse forbids in every āśrama is accumulation for private enjoyment, the inner posture of the honeybee storing honey it cannot eat.
  • Telling preachers they can collect unlimitedly while ordinary devotees must live hand-to-mouth sounds like a loophole for institutional greed.
    Prabhupāda himself anticipated this and called personal enjoyment of mission funds the greatest offense. The test is not the size of the collection but whether each rupee can be immediately engaged in practical devotional service; the moment surplus is held back for private use, the same verse condemns the preacher as a honeybee.
