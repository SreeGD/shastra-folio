Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 11.2.30

अत आत्यन्तिकं क्षेमं पृच्छामो भवतोऽनघा: । संसारेऽस्मिन् क्षणार्धोऽपि सत्सङ्ग: शेवधिर्नृणाम् ॥ ३० ॥

Therefore, O completely sinless ones, I ask you to kindly tell me what the supreme good is. After all, even half a moment’s association with pure devotees within this world of birth and death is a priceless treasure for any man.
Purport

The word śevadhiḥ, or “a great treasure,” is significant in this verse. Just as an ordinary man is overjoyed to discover an unexpected treasure, one who is actually intelligent is overjoyed to gain the association of a pure devotee of the Lord, by which one’s life can easily be perfected. According to Śrīla Jīva Gosvāmī the words ātyantikaṁ kṣemam, or “the supreme good,” indicate that situation in which one cannot be touched by even the slightest fear. Now we are entangled in the cycle of birth, old age, disease and death ( saṁsāre ). Because our entire situation can be devastated in a single moment, we are constantly in fear. But the pure devotees of the Lord can teach us the practical way to free ourselves from material existence and thus to abolish all types of fear.

According to Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura, normal etiquette would dictate that a host immediately ask a guest who has arrived about his well-being. But such an inquiry is unsuitable to place before self-satisfied devotees of the Lord, who are themselves the bestowers of all well-being. According to Śrīla Viśvanātha, the King knew that it would be useless to ask the sages about their business affairs, since the only business of the pure devotees of the Lord is the achievement of the supreme goal of life. According to Bhagavad-gītā, the goal of life is to free oneself from the cycle of birth and death and reinstate oneself as an eternal servant of God on the platform of spiritual bliss. Pure devotees of the Lord do not waste their time with ordinary mundane affairs. Sometimes foolish relatives of a Vaiṣṇava preacher lament that such a transcendental preacher has not used his life for material business and that so much money has therefore been lost by the practice of spiritual life. Such ignorant persons cannot imagine the unlimited prosperity available on the spiritual platform to those who have surrendered heart and soul to the mission of the Lord. King Nimi was himself a learned Vaiṣṇava, and therefore he did not foolishly ask the sages about ordinary worldly affairs. He immediately inquired about ātyantikaṁ kṣemam, the highest, most perfect goal of life.

According to Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura, the word anaghāḥ, “O sinless ones,” has two meanings. Anaghāḥ indicates that the nine Yogendras were themselves completely free of sins. It also indicates that merely by the great fortune of seeing them and hearing from them submissively, an ordinary, sinful man could also be freed of his sins and achieve everything he desired.

One might object that since the great sages had just arrived, the King should not have been so impatient as to inquire from them about the perfection of life. Perhaps the King should have waited until the sages themselves invited his inquiry. Such a hypothetical objection is answered by the words kṣaṇārdho ’pi. Even a single moment’s association with pure devotees, or even half a moment’s association, is sufficient to give one the perfection of life. An ordinary person who is offered a great treasure will immediately desire to claim such a treasure. Similarly, King Nimi was thinking, “Why should I consider myself fortunate enough to have such great sages stay here for a long time? Because I am an ordinary person, undoubtedly you will soon be leaving. Therefore, let me immediately take advantage of your holy association.”

According to Śrīla Bhaktisiddhānta Sarasvatī, in this world there are different varieties of mercy. But ordinary mercy cannot bring about the cessation of all unhappiness. In other words, there are many humanitarians, altruists and social reformers who certainly work for the betterment of humanity. Such persons are universally considered merciful. But in spite of their mercy, humanity continues to suffer in the grip of birth, old age, disease and death. I may distribute free food to the needy, but even after eating my merciful gift, the recipient will again become hungry, or he will suffer in some other way. In other words, by mere humanitarianism or altruism, people do not actually become free from unhappiness. Their unhappiness is merely postponed or altered. King Nimi was joyful upon seeing the nava-yogendras because he knew that they were eternally perfect associates of the Supreme Lord. Therefore he thought, “You are not prone to sinful activities like unfortunate mundane people such as me. Thus the words you speak carry no deception or exploitation.”

The materially conditioned souls spend their days and nights discussing various topics of sense gratification. They never find time to hear about transcendental knowledge. But if even only briefly or accidentally they hear hari-kathā, topics of Kṛṣṇa, in the association of pure devotees of the Lord, their propensity to suffer in material existence will slacken. When one sees liberated persons, hears about Kṛṣṇa from them, remembers their saintly behavior and so on, one’s tendency to bind himself in the illusion of sense gratification is diminished, and he becomes eager to serve the Supreme Lord.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
O sinless ones! Therefore, I ask you to kindly tell me what is most beneficial. Even half a moment's association with pure devotees within this world of birth and death is a treasure for any man. The meaning is this. When a guest comes one must ask about his welfare. But to inquire to ātmārāmas who are the very form of auspiciousness, this is inappropriate by its very nature. To ask about their health is a useless question. Therefore one should ask about spiritual welfare only. O sinless ones! Sins cannot exist in your presence. Just by bestowing your presence, you have destroyed all my sins. But there is a goal for which we should strive. I ask about that. “This may be asked later. What is the rush?” Half a moment of the treasure of your association is valuable. If suddenly one attains this treasure, why should one delay in asking about the desired goal with enthusiasm? I have special good fortune because you are staying here for a long time.

Personal Guidance
Even half a moment's association with pure devotees is a priceless treasure (śevadhiḥ) — seize it now to learn the supreme good (ātyantikaṁ kṣemam).
Practical Applications
  • Treat any contact with a pure devotee today — in person, in recorded class, in their books — as the unexpected treasure it is, and drop other plans to hear from them.
  • When you next meet a sādhu or senior Vaiṣṇava, refuse small-talk about business or weather and immediately ask one direct question about the supreme good.
  • Block fifteen minutes today for hari-kathā in the company of devotees — read aloud from Prabhupāda's purport with at least one other person rather than alone.
Reflection Questions
  • Which recent moments of sat-saṅga did I treat as ordinary social time instead of as the śevadhiḥ Nimi recognized — and what did I spend that half-moment on instead?
  • Where in my life am I still asking pure devotees for ordinary mundane help, when the only thing I should be asking them about is ātyantikaṁ kṣemam?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • When a visiting devotee comes to your campus or temple, skip the next round of social media scrolling and sit in the front — kṣaṇārdho 'pi means even one Q-and-A session can reset the next decade of your life.
  • Audit your friend group honestly: which conversations leave you eager to chant and serve Kṛṣṇa, and which leave you eager for more sense gratification — and shift one weekly hangout toward the first kind this week.
  • Stop using elders and senior devotees as career coaches; this week, ask one of them instead the question Nimi asked — what is the supreme good for someone in my situation?
householder
  • Plan the family calendar around sādhu-saṅga the way you plan it around school and work — when a devotee is in town, that takes priority over a client dinner, and your children see what you treat as treasure.
  • When relatives lament that a Vaiṣṇava in the family 'wasted' their career on devotional service, do not defend apologetically — calmly explain, as the purport does, the unlimited prosperity available on the spiritual platform.
  • In your next conversation with a senior Vaiṣṇava, refuse to fill it with business or parenting troubleshooting; bring one direct question about ātyantikaṁ kṣemam for your stage of life and listen submissively.
senior
  • Use your remaining years deliberately as Nimi used his half-moment: schedule regular hari-kathā with devotees rather than waiting for it to happen, because the body's window is visibly closing.
  • When younger family members come to you for worldly advice, gently route the deeper questions toward the company of pure devotees and their books — be the elder who points past himself, not the one who hoards counsel.
  • Inventory which attachments — property, reputation, even your own opinions — are still keeping you from sitting humbly at the feet of a devotee younger than you, and drop one of them this week.
Common Pitfalls
  • Mistaking general religious or humanitarian company for sat-saṅga — Bhaktisiddhānta warns that ordinary mercy only postpones suffering, it does not end birth, old age, disease and death.
  • Postponing the supreme inquiry because the devotee 'will be here a while' — Nimi's whole point is that even half a moment cannot be assumed, so the question must be asked now.
  • Approaching devotees as advisors on career, money or family management, then leaving without ever submissively inquiring about the goal of life.
Discussion Prompts
  • Share one concrete encounter with a devotee in the last month — did you treat it as kṣaṇārdho 'pi sat-saṅgaḥ, or as ordinary company? What would have changed if you had?
  • Bhaktisiddhānta says altruism only postpones unhappiness. Where do you still privately believe that humanitarian work, not sat-saṅga, is the real solution to suffering?
  • Nimi calls the sages anaghāḥ — sinless — and treats their words as free of deception. Whom in your life do you actually trust at that level, and what does it cost you that the list is so short?
Role Play

Two students pair up. One plays a busy householder who has just learned that a senior Vaiṣṇava sannyāsī is passing through town for only one afternoon between trains; the other plays a friend urging them to skip a long-planned work meeting to go hear him. Run the conversation until the householder either reschedules the meeting or articulates clearly why they will not — then swap roles and debrief which arguments actually moved each of you.

Objections & Responses
  • Calling these sages 'sinless' and their words deception-free sounds like blind faith — surely I should evaluate any teacher critically before surrendering my time and attention to them.
    Nimi is not asking for blind faith in anyone wearing robes; he is recognizing a specific class of person — eternally perfect associates of the Lord whose only business is the supreme goal. Critical evaluation is exactly how you identify such a devotee: do they discuss hari-kathā or mundane affairs, do they point you to Kṛṣṇa or to themselves, do their lives match Prabhupāda's description in this purport? Once that test is passed, withholding submission is not discernment, it is the lost-treasure foolishness Viśvanātha describes.
  • Feeding the hungry and reforming society are obviously good — it feels arrogant to say pure devotees are doing something higher than humanitarians who actually relieve suffering.
    The purport does not deny that humanitarians do good; Bhaktisiddhānta calls them merciful. The point is empirical: after the meal the recipient is hungry again, after the reform the body still ages, sickens and dies. Saṁsāra is not solved by rearranging its furniture. A pure devotee adds a second kind of mercy on top of the first — the practical means to end the cycle itself — and refusing that higher mercy in the name of the lower is what is actually arrogant.
