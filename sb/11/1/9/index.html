Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 11.1.9

यन्निमित्त: स वै शापो याद‍ृशो द्विजसत्तम । कथमेकात्मनां भेद एतत् सर्वं वदस्व मे ॥ ९ ॥

King Parīkṣit continued inquiring: What was the motive for this curse? What did it consist of, O purest of the twice-born? And how could such a disagreement have arisen among the Yadus, who all shared the same goal of life? Please tell me all these things.
Purport

Ekātmanām means that the Yadus all shared the same opinion, namely that Kṛṣṇa, the Supreme Personality of Godhead, was the goal of their life. Therefore, Parīkṣit Mahārāja could not see any obvious reason for such a destructive quarrel among the members of the Yadu dynasty, and he was anxious to know the actual cause.

— A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda
Gauḍīya Ācāryas
Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura Gauḍīya
Translation
O purest of the twice-born! What was the curse and what was the motive for this curse? And how could such a disagreement have arisen among the Yadus, who were all devotees of Kṛṣṇa? Please tell me all these things. How could there be a quarrel causing their destruction among those with one mind?

    His Holiness Bhanu Swami· English
Personal Guidance
Even those united in Kṛṣṇa as life's goal can fall into ruinous quarrel — guard the fellowship of devotees with vigilance.
Practical Applications
  • Identify one festering disagreement with a fellow devotee today and take the first humble step to mend it before it hardens into a curse.
  • Notice when your speech toward a Vaiṣṇava turns sharp or dismissive, and silently beg Kṛṣṇa's forgiveness before continuing.
  • Refuse to assume that shared devotion automatically protects a community — actively serve the harmony of your saṅga through concrete acts of respect.
Reflection Questions
  • Where in my own circle — family, saṅga, or workplace — do we claim ekātmatā around Kṛṣṇa yet quietly carry the seeds of bheda through pride, faction, or unresolved offense?
  • What hidden nimitta (motive, grudge, or subtle ahaṅkāra) am I nursing that could, if left unchecked, ripen into a śāpa-like rupture even among those who share my spiritual goal?
For Each Age Group
youth
  • Identify one friend group or class WhatsApp chat where everyone claims the same values yet quietly competes; this week, name (to yourself, in writing) the real viṣaya — marks, attention, status — driving the friction.
  • Before posting or replying online today, ask Parīkṣit's question of yourself: yan-nimittaḥ — what is actually motivating this? If the honest answer is ahaṅkāra, do not send it.
  • Pick one classmate or roommate you've drifted from over something 'principled.' Approach them once this week and ask, in your own words, katham ekātmanāṁ bhedaḥ — how did we, who wanted the same thing, end up here?
householder
  • In the next family disagreement over money, schooling, or in-laws, pause and ask aloud the three Parīkṣit questions — motive, content, how it grew — before defending your position.
  • With your spouse, name one shared goal (raising the children in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, financial stability) and one concrete bheda that has crept in around it; schedule a single evening this week to address only that gap.
  • At work, identify a colleague with whom you share an explicit goal but a hidden rivalry. Treat the rivalry itself as the śāpa-mechanism in miniature; refuse to feed it through gossip or one-upmanship for one full week.
senior
  • Look over the long arc of your family or community: where did groups that began ekātmanām fracture? Write down what you now see as the real yan-nimittaḥ — not to assign blame, but to warn the next generation honestly.
  • Choose one relationship — a sibling, an old friend, a former co-worker — where a rupture remains. Before the body fails, take Parīkṣit's posture: go and ask, not 'who was right,' but 'how did we, sharing so much, come to this?'
  • Mentor one younger devotee or family member by telling them, without invented pastimes, how ahaṅkāra and viṣaya quietly eroded a unity you once trusted — so that they recognize the early signs in their own saṅga.
Common Pitfalls
  • Assuming that sharing the same external goal — Kṛṣṇa as the supreme — automatically immunizes a community from quarrel, and therefore ignoring the small frictions that actually compound into bheda.
  • Treating Parīkṣit's inquiry as mere historical curiosity rather than as a mirror: rushing to learn 'what happened to the Yadus' while refusing to examine what nimitta is alive in one's own dealings today.
  • Spiritualizing conflict by labeling every disagreement among devotees as 'Kṛṣṇa's arrangement,' and thereby evading the personal accountability — for speech, motive, and offense — that the verse's question presses us to face.
Discussion Prompts
  • The Yadus all agreed that Kṛṣṇa was the goal of life (ekātmanām), yet they destroyed one another. Share a time when you and people you respected agreed on the highest principle but still fell into bitter conflict. What was actually fueling the fight underneath the shared ideology?
  • Parīkṣit asks three precise questions — motive, content, and how unity broke. In your own sādhana-saṅga or family, which of those three do you most avoid investigating when things go wrong, and why?
  • Prabhupāda says the cause was not obvious to Parīkṣit. Where in your own life have you accepted an 'obvious' explanation for a rupture (with a parent, friend, co-devotee) when the real cause was unseen? What would it cost you to ask, as Parīkṣit does, 'tell me all these things'?
Role Play

Two students play co-leaders of a small temple program who both sincerely hold Kṛṣṇa as the goal of life, yet have stopped speaking after a dispute over how to run a festival. A third student plays a senior Vaiṣṇava whom they have approached separately. The senior must, in the manner of Parīkṣit's inquiry, refuse the surface story from each side and press for yan-nimittaḥ — the actual motive — and katham ekātmanāṁ bhedaḥ — how two who share the same goal came to this. The exercise ends only when each co-leader can name the ahaṅkāra or viṣaya underneath their 'principled' position.

Objections & Responses
  • If even the Yadus — Kṛṣṇa's own associates, all sharing the same goal — could be cursed into mutual destruction, what hope is there for ordinary devotees? This sounds like a story that should discourage rather than inspire.
    Parīkṣit asks this very question because he sees the paradox. The Gauḍīya reading is the opposite of discouragement: precisely because the Yadus' fall was not random but caused (yan-nimittaḥ), it is instructive. The Lord's līlā exposes that proximity, lineage, and even correct philosophical conviction do not by themselves protect against ahaṅkāra and brāhmaṇa-aparādha. The lesson is sobering, not despairing — it sends us to take shelter of Kṛṣṇa personally, in humility, rather than resting on our group identity.
  • This is just an ancient family feud being dressed up as theology. Calling it a 'curse' is a pre-modern way of avoiding the ordinary sociology of clans turning on each other.
    A Gauḍīya teacher does not deny the sociology — Parīkṣit himself asks what the curse 'consisted of,' meaning he wants the concrete mechanism. But the verse insists on a deeper causal layer: a śāpa from qualified brāhmaṇas, accepted by Kṛṣṇa Himself, working through the ordinary passions of the Yadus. Reducing it to 'just' clan dynamics is not more rigorous; it is less, because it refuses to ask Parīkṣit's question — how does the same goal of life fracture into bheda? That is a spiritual diagnosis, and the sociology is its instrument, not its substitute.
