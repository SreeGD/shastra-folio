BG 6.1

Sūta Gosvāmī said: After hearing all that was narrated to him by the self-realized and equipoised Śukadeva, the son of Vyāsadeva, Mahārāja Parīkṣit humbly approached his lotus feet. Bowing his head down upon the sage’s feet, the King, who had lived his entire life under the protection of Lord Viṣṇu, folded his hands in supplication and spoke as follows.

Essence. Having heard the entire Bhagavatam from the equipoised Sukadeva, Pariksit feels gratitude take over his being. The verse models what receiving deep teaching should produce in us — not pride of knowing but soft gratitude and humility.

BG 6.2

Mahārāja Parīkṣit said: I have now achieved the purpose of my life, because a great and merciful soul like you has shown such kindness to me. You have personally spoken to me this narration of the Supreme Personality of Godhead, Hari, who is without beginning or end.

Essence. Pariksit declares the purpose of his life fulfilled because a kind soul revealed Kṛṣṇa to him. He doesn't say his kingdom was the fulfillment; the kingdom was just the setting. Our circumstances are setting; encountering the Lord is the plot.

BG 6.3

I do not consider it at all amazing that great souls such as yourself, whose minds are always absorbed in the infallible Personality of Godhead, show mercy to the foolish conditioned souls, tormented as we are by the problems of material life.

Essence. Pariksit says he is not amazed that great souls absorbed in the Lord still show mercy to the fallen — that is simply their nature. Saints reach down because they are full, not because they need anything. We can become small currents of that same mercy.

BG 6.4

I have heard from you this Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam, which is the perfect summary of all the Purāṇas and which perfectly describes the Supreme Lord, Uttamaḥśloka.

Essence. Pariksit has now heard the perfect summary of all the Puranas describing Uttamaḥśloka. Receiving Bhagavatam at the right time can complete a life. Even one good hearing, fully received, is enough to reorient everything.

BG 6.5

My lord, I now have no fear of Takṣaka or any other living being, or even of repeated deaths, because I have absorbed myself in that purely spiritual Absolute Truth, which you have revealed and which destroys all fear.

Essence. Pariksit no longer fears Taksaka, other beings, or repeated deaths — he has absorbed himself in the purely spiritual Brahman. Fear shrinks naturally when something larger fills the inner space; we don't have to fight it directly.

BG 6.6

O brāhmaṇa, please give me permission to resign my speech and the functions of all my senses unto Lord Adhokṣaja. Allow me to absorb my mind, purified of lusty desires, within Him and to thus give up my life.

Essence. Pariksit asks permission to withdraw his speech, senses, and mind into Lord Adhokṣaja. He doesn't simply stop using them; he offers them. Spiritual death is not absence of activity but redirection of every faculty to the Lord.

BG 6.7

You have revealed to me that which is most auspicious, the supreme personal feature of the Lord. I am now fixed in knowledge and self-realization, and my ignorance has been eradicated.

Essence. Pariksit thanks Sukadeva for revealing the supreme personal feature of the Lord, leaving him fixed in knowledge and self-realization. The personal Lord, not impersonal vagueness, is what finally settles the heart.

BG 6.8

Sūta Gosvāmī said: Thus requested, the saintly son of Śrīla Vyāsadeva gave his permission to King Parīkṣit. Then, after being worshiped by the King and all the sages present, Śukadeva departed from that place.

Essence. Sukadeva grants permission and, worshiped by Pariksit and the sages, takes leave. There is a quiet dignity in spiritual goodbyes between teacher and student — neither clinging nor coldness, just permission for the next stage.

BG 6.9-10

Mahārāja Parīkṣit then sat down on the bank of the Ganges, upon a seat made of darbha grass with the tips of its stalks facing east, and turned himself toward the north. Having attained the perfection of yoga, he experienced full self-realization and was free of material attachment and doubt. The saintly King settled his mind within his spiritual self by pure intelligence and proceeded to meditate upon the Supreme Absolute Truth. His life air ceased to move, and he became as stationary as a tree.

Essence. Pariksit sits on darbha grass facing east, turns himself toward Brahman, and waits. He does not protest his fate or scramble for protection. There is a courage in receiving death consciously, in chosen posture, that bhakti makes possible.

BG 6.11

O learned brāhmaṇas, the snake-bird Takṣaka, who had been sent by the angry son of a brāhmaṇa, was going toward the King to kill him when he saw Kaśyapa Muni on the path.

Essence. Taksaka, sent by the brahmana boy's curse, meets Kaśyapa on the road — a healer who could counteract the poison. Even our enemies are choreographed within a larger script. Nothing approaches us by pure accident.

BG 6.12

Takṣaka flattered Kaśyapa by presenting him with valuable offerings and thereby stopped the sage, who was expert in counteracting poison, from protecting Mahārāja Parīkṣit. Then the snake-bird, who could assume any form he wished, disguised himself as a brāhmaṇa, approached the King and bit him.

Essence. Taksaka flatters Kasyapa with valuable offerings, stopping him from protecting Pariksit. Greed turns even powerful potential help into silence. The verse warns: any of us can be bought when we want something more than we want dharma.

BG 6.13

While living beings all over the universe looked on, the body of the great self-realized saint among kings was immediately burned to ashes by the fire of the snake’s poison.

Essence. While beings throughout the universe look on, Pariksit's body is burned to ashes by Taksaka's fire. The saint dies a death the world witnesses; the loss is universal, the soul's gain is total. A devotee's exit is a teaching to all who watch.

BG 6.14

There arose a terrible cry of lamentation in all directions on the earth and in the heavens, and all the demigods, demons, human beings and other creatures were astonished.

Essence. Lamentation rises throughout the worlds at the loss of Pariksit. Genuine saints leave a hole in the world that creation itself feels. Our mourning, properly held, can become honoring rather than only grief.

BG 6.15

Kettledrums sounded in the regions of the demigods, and the celestial Gandharvas and Apsarās sang. The demigods showered flowers and spoke words of praise.

Essence. Even as some lament, the demigods praise Pariksit and shower flowers from above. From an earthly view his death is loss; from a higher view it is a coronation. The same event reads differently depending on the altitude of vision.

BG 6.16

Hearing that his father had been fatally bitten by the snake-bird, Mahārāja Janamejaya became extremely angry and had brāhmaṇas perform a mighty sacrifice in which he offered all the snakes in the world into the sacrificial fire.

Essence. Janamejaya, hearing of his father's death, organizes a massive snake sacrifice in rage. Grief without wisdom turns into harm-seeking. Bhagavatam quietly observes how unprocessed loss can drive us into chains of further violence.

BG 6.17

When Takṣaka saw even the most powerful serpents being burned in the blazing fire of that snake sacrifice, he was overwhelmed with fear and approached Lord Indra for shelter.

Essence. Seeing serpents burn in the fire, Taksaka panics and runs to Indra for shelter. Even powerful beings, when their wrongdoing catches up, hide behind higher powers. Refuge sought from selfish motives still finds someone.

BG 6.18

When King Janamejaya did not see Takṣaka entering his sacrificial fire, he said to the brāhmaṇas : Why is not Takṣaka, the lowest of all serpents, burning in this fire?

Essence. Janamejaya demands to know why Taksaka has not fallen into the fire. Anger keeps escalating its demands; it always wants more thoroughness. The verse shows the open-ended hunger of revenge.

BG 6.19

The brāhmaṇas replied: O best of kings, the snake Takṣaka has not fallen into the fire because he is being protected by Indra, whom he has approached for shelter. Indra is holding him back from the fire.

Essence. The brahmanas reply that Taksaka is being protected by Indra, his refuge. Bhagavatam matter-of-factly shows the political-spiritual networks of power. Reality is not flat; everyone has a patron somewhere.

BG 6.20

The intelligent King Janamejaya, hearing these words, replied to the priests: Then, my dear brāhmaṇas, why not make Takṣaka fall into the fire, along with his protector, Indra?

Essence. Janamejaya then asks why not pull Indra in along with Taksaka. Revenge expands its target list; once you accept its logic, no one is too high to attack. Bhagavatam is sober about how anger devours hierarchy itself.

BG 6.21

Hearing this, the priests then chanted this mantra for offering Takṣaka together with Indra as an oblation into the sacrificial fire: O Takṣaka, fall immediately into this fire, together with Indra and his entire host of demigods!

Essence. The priests chant the dangerous mantra that begins pulling Indra and Taksaka together toward the fire. Words backed by ritual power can do real harm; we should be careful what we authorize, even verbally, in moments of anger.

BG 6.22

When Lord Indra, along with his airplane and Takṣaka, was suddenly thrown from his position by these insulting words of the brāhmaṇas, he became very disturbed.

Essence. Indra, suddenly thrown from his position by the brahmanas' insulting words, becomes very anxious. Even the king of heaven is not safely above public disturbance. Position never fully protects from indignity once the wheel turns.

BG 6.23

Bṛhaspati, the son of Aṅgirā Muni, seeing Indra falling from the sky in his airplane along with Takṣaka, approached King Janamejaya and spoke to him as follows.

Essence. Bṛhaspati, seeing Indra falling, approaches Janamejaya to advise restraint. Wise mediators intervene before things become irretrievable. We can ourselves be that quiet advisor for someone heading too far.

BG 6.24

O King among men, it is not fitting that this king of snakes meet death at your hands, for he has drunk the nectar of the immortal demigods. Consequently he is not subject to the ordinary symptoms of old age and death.

Essence. Bṛhaspati tells the king Taksaka has drunk amṛta and cannot be killed by him. Some battles cannot be won by force of will alone; the wise see this and stop pushing. Knowing when to stop is half of wisdom.

BG 6.25

The life and death of an embodied soul and his destination in the next life are all caused by himself through his own activity. Therefore, O King, no other agent is actually responsible for creating one’s happiness and distress.

Essence. The life, death, and next destination of an embodied soul are caused by himself through his own activity. No one can really kill another against the script of their karma. The locus of responsibility moves inside, where it belongs.

BG 6.26

When a conditioned soul is killed by snakes, thieves, fire, lightning, hunger, disease or anything else, he is experiencing the reaction to his own past work.

Essence. When a conditioned soul is killed by snakes, thieves, fire, lightning, hunger or disease, he is experiencing the reaction of his own past karma. The world is not random violence; it is the body's encounter with its own seed-bed. Knowing this softens both rage and victimhood.

BG 6.27

Therefore, my dear King, please stop this sacrificial performance, which was initiated with the intent of doing harm to others. Many innocent snakes have already been burned to death. Indeed, all persons must suffer the unforeseen consequences of their past activities.

Essence. Bṛhaspati urges Janamejaya to stop the sacrifice meant to harm others. Many innocent snakes have already died for someone else's revenge. We are warned: large rituals of vengeance always end up consuming the innocent.

BG 6.28

Sūta Gosvāmī continued: Advised in this manner, Mahārāja Janamejaya replied, “So be it.” Honoring the words of the great sage, he desisted from performing the snake sacrifice and worshiped Bṛhaspati, the most eloquent of sages.

Essence. Janamejaya replies, 'So be it,' and honors the sage's words. To stop mid-course, after a public commitment, takes humility. Wise people prize being right less than being good.

BG 6.29

This is indeed the Supreme Lord Viṣṇu’s illusory energy, which is unstoppable and difficult to perceive. Although the individual spirit souls are part and parcel of the Lord, through the influence of this illusory energy they are bewildered by their identification with various material bodies.

Essence. Suta now teaches: the Lord's illusory energy is unstoppable and hard to perceive; though we are parts of Him, we identify with the bodies she creates. Maya is sophisticated; she binds us by pretending she's not there at all.

BG 6.30-31

But there exists a supreme reality, in which the illusory energy cannot fearlessly dominate, thinking, “I can control this person because he is deceitful.” In that highest reality there are no illusory argumentative philosophies. Rather, there the true students of spiritual science constantly engage in authorized spiritual investigation. In that supreme reality there is no manifestation of the material mind, which functions in terms of alternating decision and doubt. Created material products, their subtle causes and the goals of enjoyment attained by their utilization do not exist there. Furthermore, in that supreme reality there is no conditioned spirit, covered by false ego and the three modes of nature. That reality excludes everything limited or limiting. One who is wise should therefore stop the waves of material life and enjoy within that Supreme Truth.

Essence. But there is a supreme reality maya cannot fearlessly dominate. Surrender to that reality breaks her presumption. We don't beat maya; we let Someone bigger stand between us and her.

BG 6.32

Those who desire to give up all that is not essentially real move systematically, by negative discrimination of the extraneous, to the supreme position of Lord Viṣṇu. Giving up petty materialism, they offer their love exclusively to the Absolute Truth within their hearts and embrace that highest truth in fixed meditation.

Essence. Those who want to give up everything inessential systematically discriminate, dropping the extraneous, until they reach the supreme position. The path is not just acquisition of truth but careful, ongoing release of what is not it.

BG 6.33

Such devotees come to understand the supreme transcendental situation of the Personality of Godhead, Lord Viṣṇu, because they are no longer polluted by the concepts of “I” and “my,” which are based on body and home.

Essence. Such devotees come to understand the Lord's supreme transcendental situation because their hearts are no longer polluted. Vision improves by purification, not by extra effort to see. Cleaning, not staring harder, is the spiritual technique.

BG 6.34

One should tolerate all insults and never fail to show proper respect to any person. Avoiding identification with the material body, one should not create enmity with anyone.

Essence. Tolerate insults, respect others, do not identify with the body, do not create suffering for any living being. Bhagavatam compresses a full ethical curriculum into one verse. Each clause is the work of a lifetime.

BG 6.35

I offer my obeisances to the Supreme Personality of Godhead, the invincible Lord Śrī Kṛṣṇa. Simply by meditating upon His lotus feet I have been able to study and appreciate this great literature.

Essence. Suta closes with obeisances to invincible Kṛṣṇa, by meditation on whose lotus feet he has been able to recall this entire history. Devotion, not memory alone, retrieves spiritual teaching. The Lord supplies the very capacity to remember Him.

BG 6.36

Śaunaka Ṛṣi said: O gentle Sūta, please narrate to us how Paila and the other greatly intelligent disciples of Śrīla Vyāsadeva, who are known as the standard authorities of Vedic wisdom, spoke and edited the Vedas.

Essence. Saunaka now asks about the Veda's branches and how Vyasa's disciples carried them. Bhagavatam pauses to honor the careful work of transmission. Spiritual culture survives because someone stayed precise about the chain.

BG 6.37

Sūta Gosvāmī said: O brāhmaṇa, first the subtle vibration of transcendental sound appeared from the sky of the heart of the most elevated Lord Brahmā, whose mind was perfectly fixed in spiritual realization. One can perceive this subtle vibration when one stops all external hearing.

Essence. Suta describes how subtle transcendental sound first appeared from the sky of Brahma's heart. The Vedas began as an inner vibration before becoming external syllables. All real teaching has this two-layered nature.

BG 6.38

By worship of this subtle form of the Vedas, O brāhmaṇa, mystic sages cleanse their hearts of all contamination caused by impurity of substance, activity and doer, and thus they attain freedom from repeated birth and death.

Essence. By worship of this subtle form of the Vedas, mystic sages cleanse their hearts of contamination caused by substance, activity, and the doer's mistaken self-conception. Vedic study is a purification, not a collection of beliefs.

BG 6.39

From that transcendental subtle vibration arose the oṁkāra composed of three sounds. The oṁkāra has unseen potencies and manifests automatically within a purified heart. It is the representation of the Absolute Truth in all three of His phases — the Supreme Personality, the Supreme Soul and the supreme impersonal truth.

Essence. From the subtle vibration arose oṁkara, with unseen potencies, manifesting in the realized self. Sound itself is a portal — not metaphorically, but functionally. The mantras we chant work on us at this level.

BG 6.40-41

This oṁkāra, ultimately nonmaterial and imperceptible, is heard by the Supersoul without His possessing material ears or any other material senses. The entire expanse of Vedic sound is elaborated from oṁkāra, which appears from the soul, within the sky of the heart. It is the direct designation of the self-originating Absolute Truth, the Supersoul, and is the secret essence and eternal seed of all Vedic hymns.

Essence. Oṁkara, nonmaterial and imperceptible, is heard by the Supersoul without material ears. The Lord receives our mantras at a level prior to the senses. Our chanting reaches Him even when we ourselves are barely awake to it.

BG 6.42

Oṁkāra exhibited the three original sounds of the alphabet — A, U and M. These three, O most eminent descendant of Bhṛgu, sustain all the different threefold aspects of material existence, including the three modes of nature, the names of the Ṛg, Yajur and Sāma Vedas, the goals known as the Bhūr, Bhuvar and Svar planetary systems, and the three functional platforms called waking consciousness, sleep and deep sleep.

Essence. Oṁkara contains A, U and M — the three sounds sustaining all things that have a name. The simplest sounds organize the greatest realities. Spiritual life often hides in the smallest, most repeatable units.

BG 6.43

From that oṁkāra Lord Brahmā created all the sounds of the alphabet — the vowels, consonants, semivowels, sibilants and others — distinguished by such features as long and short measure.

Essence. From oṁkara, Brahma created vowels, consonants, semivowels, and sibilants — the whole grammar of human language. All speech traces back to a single sacred breath. Treating speech as ordinary is a kind of forgetting.

BG 6.44

All-powerful Brahmā made use of this collection of sounds to produce from his four faces the four Vedas, which appeared together with the sacred oṁkāra and the seven vyāhṛti invocations. His intention was to propagate the process of Vedic sacrifice according to the different functions performed by the priests of each of the four Vedas.

Essence. Brahma uses these sounds to produce the four Vedas from his four faces, together with oṁkara. The structure of revelation is patterned, organized, complete. Nothing in Vedic culture is haphazard at its source.

BG 6.45

Brahmā taught these Vedas to his sons, who were great sages among the brāhmaṇas and experts in the art of Vedic recitation. They in turn took the role of ācāryas and imparted the Vedas to their own sons.

Essence. Brahma taught the Vedas to his sons, who taught their sons. The whole vast edifice rests on relationships of teaching and trust. Without that human chain, no scripture survives — and we are now one link in it.

BG 6.46

In this way, throughout the cycles of four ages, generation after generation of disciples — all firmly fixed in their spiritual vows — have received these Vedas by disciplic succession. At the end of each Dvāpara-yuga the Vedas are edited into separate divisions by eminent sages.

Essence. Through cycles of four ages, generation after generation of disciples, firmly fixed in vows, received the Veda. The strength of a tradition is not one teacher's brilliance but the steadiness of countless ordinary disciples.

BG 6.47

Observing that people in general were diminished in their life span, strength and intelligence by the influence of time, great sages took inspiration from the Personality of Godhead sitting within their hearts and systematically divided the Vedas.

Essence. Observing that human lifespan, strength, and intelligence were diminishing under time, great sages divided the Vedas to make them accessible. Mercy is willing to simplify when the audience cannot bear the full version anymore.

BG 6.48-49

O brāhmaṇa, in the present age of Vaivasvata Manu, the leaders of the universe, led by Brahmā and Śiva, requested the Supreme Personality of Godhead, the protector of all the worlds, to save the principles of religion. O most fortunate Śaunaka, the almighty Lord, exhibiting a divine spark of a portion of His plenary portion, then appeared in the womb of Satyavatī as the son of Parāśara. In this form, named Kṛṣṇa Dvaipāyana Vyāsa, he divided the one Veda into four.

Essence. Brahma and Siva, leaders of the universe, requested Vyasa to subdivide the Vedas. Even the highest beings recognize when a service is needed and request the right person. Leadership often means knowing whom to ask.

BG 6.50

Śrīla Vyāsadeva separated the mantras of the Ṛg, Atharva, Yajur and Sāma Vedas into four divisions, just as one sorts out a mixed collection of jewels into piles. Thus he composed four distinct Vedic literatures.

Essence. Vyasa sorts the mantras of the four Vedas as one sorts a mixed pile of jewels. Each scripture finds its right place by careful classification. Spiritual organization is itself an act of devotion when done with love.

BG 6.51

The most powerful and intelligent Vyāsadeva called four of his disciples, O brāhmaṇa, and entrusted to each of them one of these four saṁhitās.

Essence. Vyasa calls four disciples and entrusts each with one saṁhita. Authority is given, then released. A real teacher hands real responsibility — not just tasks — to those he has trained.

BG 6.52-53

Śrīla Vyāsadeva taught the first saṁhitā, the Ṛg Veda, to Paila and gave this collection the name Bahvṛca. To the sage Vaiśampāyana he spoke the collection of Yajur mantras named Nigada. He taught the Sāma Veda mantras, designated as the Chandoga-saṁhitā, to Jaimini, and he spoke the Atharva Veda to his dear disciple Sumantu.

Essence. Each Veda goes to a specific disciple. The naming and assigning create a structure that lasts millennia. Specific personal commission has more durable power than vague communal invitation.

BG 6.54-56

After dividing his saṁhitā into two parts, the wise Paila spoke it to Indrapramiti and Bāṣkala. Bāṣkala further divided his collection into four parts, O Bhārgava, and instructed them to his disciples Bodhya, Yājṣavalkya, Parāśara and Agnimitra. Indrapramiti, the self-controlled sage, taught his saṁhitā to the learned mystic Māṇḍūkeya, whose disciple Devamitra later passed down the divisions of the Ṛg Veda to Saubhari and others.

Essence. Paila divides his saṁhita and gives the parts to students; the chain branches and proliferates. One person's faithful handling becomes the seed of many lineages. Small fidelities have unforeseeable reach.

BG 6.57

The son of Māṇḍūkeya, named Śākalya, divided his own collection into five, entrusting one subdivision each to Vātsya, Mudgala, Śālīya, Gokhalya and Śiśira.

Essence. Sakalya divides his collection into five and entrusts each part to a disciple. Even bookish details receive devotional attention here. To Vyasa's school, classification was a form of worship.

BG 6.58

The sage Jātūkarṇya was also a disciple of Śākalya, and after dividing the saṁhitā he received from Śākalya into three parts, he added a fourth section, a Vedic glossary. He taught one of these parts to each of four disciples — Balāka, the second Paila, Jābāla and Viraja.

Essence. Jatukarnya divides his received saṁhita into three and adds a fourth section. Faithful transmission still leaves room for creative addition. Honoring tradition does not exclude bringing one's own contribution.

BG 6.59

Bāṣkali assembled the Vālakhilya-saṁhitā, a collection from all the branches of the Ṛg Veda. This collection was received by Vālāyani, Bhajya and Kāśāra.

Essence. Baskali assembles the Valakhilya-saṁhita from many branches and passes it on. Some servants of tradition are anthologizers — gathering the scattered for those who come later. Curatorial service is real service.

BG 6.60

Thus these various saṁhitās of the Ṛg Veda were maintained through disciplic succession by these saintly brāhmaṇas. Simply by hearing of this distribution of the Vedic hymns, one will be freed from all sins.

Essence. These saṁhitas of the Ṛg Veda are kept alive through disciplic succession by saintly brahmanas. Just hearing of this distribution is itself purifying. Even reading lineage lists is a contact with grace.

BG 6.61

The disciples of Vaiśampāyana became authorities in the Atharva Veda. They were known as the Carakas because they executed strict vows to free their guru from his sin of killing a brāhmaṇa.

Essence. Vaisampayana's disciples become Carakas, vowing to free their guru of a karmic burden. Real disciples take on their teacher's debts as their own — that is the depth of the bond.

BG 6.62

Once Yājṣavalkya, one of the disciples of Vaiśampāyana, said: O master, how much benefit will be derived from the feeble endeavors of these weak disciples of yours? I will personally perform some outstanding penance.

Essence. Yajnavalkya questions the weak efforts of his fellow disciples — a moment of pride disguised as concern. The Bhagavatam captures how easily intelligence becomes superiority. Sharp minds need sharp humility.

BG 6.63

Addressed thus, the spiritual master Vaiśampāyana became angry and said: Go away from here! Enough of you, O disciple who insults brāhmaṇas! Furthermore, you must immediately give back everything I have taught you.

Essence. Vaisampayana, angered, expels Yajnavalkya for insulting brahmanas. Disrespect to the community of sages has weight even from a star pupil. We can be talented and wrong at the same time.

BG 6.64-65

Yājṣavalkya, the son of Devarāta, then vomited the mantras of the Yajur Veda and went away from there. The assembled disciples, looking greedily upon these yajur hymns, assumed the form of partridges and picked them all up. These divisions of the Yajur Veda therefore became known as the most beautiful Taittirīya-saṁhitā, the hymns collected by partridges [tittirāḥ].

Essence. Yajnavalkya, expelled, vomits the Yajur mantras; the other disciples eagerly take them up. What one losing personality releases can become food for many. Even painful losses can feed those who come after.

BG 6.66

My dear brāhmaṇa Śaunaka, Yājṣavalkya then desired to find out new yajur-mantras unknown to even his spiritual master. With this in mind he offered attentive worship to the powerful lord of the sun.

Essence. Yajnavalkya now desires new yajur-mantras unknown even to his guru, and offers worship to the sun-god. Falling, he humbly seeks a higher source. Mistakes, repented, can become the doorway to fresh revelation.

BG 6.67

Śrī Yājṣavalkya said: I offer my respectful obeisances to the Supreme Personality of Godhead appearing as the sun. You are present as the controller of the four kinds of living entities, beginning from Brahmā and extending down to the blades of grass. Just as the sky is present both inside and outside every living being, you exist both within the hearts of all as the Supersoul and externally in the form of time. Just as the sky cannot be covered by the clouds present within it, you are never covered by any false material designation. By the flow of years, which are made up of the tiny fragments of time called kṣaṇas, lavas and nimeṣas, you alone maintain this world, drying up the waters and giving them back as rain.

Essence. Yajnavalkya offers obeisance to the Supreme Lord appearing as the sun, the controller of the four kinds of beings. Worship of any deity, properly understood, reaches the Lord behind that form. Diversity of approach, unity of arrival.

BG 6.68

O glowing one, O powerful lord of the sun, you are the chief of all the demigods. I meditate with careful attention on your fiery globe, because for those who offer prayers to you three times daily according to the Vedic method passed down through authorized disciplic succession, you burn away all sinful activities, all consequent suffering and even the original seed of desire.

Essence. Yajnavalkya meditates with careful attention on the sun's fiery globe — the chief of demigods. Choosing one form for fixed meditation can quiet the wandering mind. Specificity supports concentration.

BG 6.69

You are personally present as the indwelling lord in the hearts of all moving and nonmoving beings, who depend completely on your shelter. Indeed, you animate their material minds, senses and vital airs to act.

Essence. The sun-god is the indwelling lord of all moving and nonmoving beings, present in every heart. We are never alone, never unobserved by love. That recognition can reshape an ordinary day.

BG 6.70

The world has been seized and swallowed by the python of darkness in its horrible mouth and has become unconscious, as if dead. But mercifully glancing upon the sleeping people of the world, you raise them up with the gift of sight. Thus you are most magnanimous. At the three sacred junctures of each day, you engage the pious in the path of ultimate good, inducing them to perform religious duties that situate them in their spiritual position.

Essence. The world swallowed by the python of darkness becomes unconscious; only the Lord's merciful glance can revive it. Dawn is itself a daily mercy. The Lord's gaze is what keeps anyone awake at all.

BG 6.71

Just like an earthly king, you travel about everywhere spreading fear among the unholy as the powerful deities of the directions offer you in their folded palms lotus flowers and other respectful presentations.

Essence. Like an earthly king, the sun travels everywhere spreading awe among the unholy as the direction-deities offer it lotus tributes. Beauty and authority can travel together; rule does not need to be ugly.

BG 6.72

Therefore, my lord, I am prayerfully approaching your lotus feet, which are honored by the spiritual masters of the three worlds, because I hope to receive from you mantras of the Yajur Veda unknown to anyone else.

Essence. Yajnavalkya prayerfully approaches the sun's lotus feet, honored by spiritual masters, hoping to receive yajur-mantras unknown to others. He uses his prayer not for power but for service to scripture. The right ask matters.

BG 6.73

Sūta Gosvāmī said: Satisfied by such glorification, the powerful sun-god assumed the form of a horse and presented to the sage Yājṣavalkya yajur-mantras previously unknown in human society.

Essence. Satisfied, the sun-god takes the form of a horse and gives Yajnavalkya yajur-mantras. Revelation can come in unexpected shapes when one is sincere. We should not pre-judge how grace will arrive.

BG 6.74

From these countless hundreds of mantras of the Yajur Veda, the powerful sage compiled fifteen new branches of Vedic literature. These became known as the Vājasaneyi-saṁhitā because they were produced from the hairs of the horse’s mane, and they were accepted in disciplic succession by the followers of Kāṇva, Mādhyandina and other ṛṣis.

Essence. From the hundreds of mantras received, Yajnavalkya compiles fifteen new branches — the Vajasaneyi school. Personal disgrace is converted into permanent contribution. The Lord lets a falling become a foundation.

BG 6.75

Jaimini Ṛṣi, the authority of the Sāma Veda, had a son named Sumantu, and the son of Sumantu was Sutvān. The sage Jaimini spoke to each of them a different part of the Sāma-veda-saṁhitā.

Essence. Jaimini speaks different parts of the Sama Veda to his son Sumantu, his grandson Sutvan, and others. Spiritual transmission can travel through bloodline when bloodline is also sadhana. Family can be a school of devotion.

BG 6.76-77

Sukarmā, another disciple of Jaimini, was a great scholar. He divided the mighty tree of the Sāma Veda into one thousand saṁhitās. Then, O brāhmaṇa, three disciples of Sukarmā — Hiraṇyanābha, the son of Kuśala; Pauṣyaṣji; and Āvantya, who was very advanced in spiritual realization — took charge of the sāma-mantras.

Essence. Sukarma, another disciple, divides the Sama Veda into one thousand saṁhitas. Some servants of tradition specialize in scale; others in depth. Tradition needs both kinds.

BG 6.78

The five hundred disciples of Pauṣyaṣji and Āvantya became known as the northern singers of the Sāma Veda, and in later times some of them also became known as eastern singers.

Essence. Five hundred disciples become the northern singers of the Sama Veda. Numbers grow because patient teachers said yes to one student at a time. Mass movements have humble, individual roots.

BG 6.79

Five other disciples of Pauṣyaṣji, namely Laugākṣi, Māṅgali, Kulya, Kuśīda and Kukṣi, each received one hundred saṁhitās.

Essence. Five other disciples of Pausyanji each receive one hundred saṁhitas. Even ordinary participants in a lineage carry significant trust. We may not be the star — but we may still be a hundred saṁhitas' worth.

BG 6.80

Kṛta, the disciple of Hiraṇyanābha, spoke twenty-four saṁhitās to his own disciples, and the remaining collections were passed down by the self-realized sage Āvantya.

Essence. Krta and the disciples of Hiranyanabha continue the chain. The Bhagavatam patiently names them so that no one is lost. Heaven does not forget the unsung servants of paramparas; we should not either.
