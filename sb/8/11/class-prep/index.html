Chapter 11 · Class prep

BG 11.1

Śukadeva Gosvāmī said: Thereafter, by the supreme grace of the Supreme Personality of Godhead, Śrī Hari, all the demigods, headed by Indra and Vāyu, were brought back to life. Being enlivened, the demigods began severely beating the very same demons who had defeated them before.

Essence. By the Lord's grace, demigods were brought back to life and turned the tables on those who had defeated them. The Lord's grace does not just preserve us; it reverses our position. The same enemies who beat us yesterday can be beaten back today.

BG 11.2

When the most powerful Indra became angry and took his thunderbolt in hand to kill Mahārāja Bali, the demons began lamenting, “Alas, alas!”

Essence. When Indra raised his thunderbolt at Bali, the demons cried out 'Alas, alas!' The fall of a leader produces panic in his community. Loyalty becomes visible at the moment of his exposure.

BG 11.3

Sober and tolerant and well equipped with paraphernalia for fighting, Bali Mahārāja moved before Indra on the great battlefield. King Indra, who always carries the thunderbolt in his hand, rebuked Bali Mahārāja as follows.

Essence. Sober, tolerant, fully equipped, Bali moved before Indra and was rebuked. Even demonic kings can show the composure of warriors. Stability under threat is not exclusively a devotee's quality, though it points toward dharma.

BG 11.4

Indra said: O rascal, as a cheater sometimes binds the eyes of a child and takes away his possessions, you are trying to defeat us by displaying some mystic power, although you know that we are the masters of all such mystic powers.

Essence. Indra accused Bali of trying to defeat them by mystic juggling — as a cheater blinds a child and steals his things. Cheap tricks impress only the inexperienced. When the masters of māyā are watching, illusion does not work.

BG 11.5

Those fools and rascals who want to ascend to the upper planetary system by mystic power or mechanical means, or who endeavor to cross even the upper planets and achieve the spiritual world or liberation, I cause to be sent to the lowest region of the universe.

Essence. Those who try to ascend to higher planets by mystic power or machines, without qualification, Indra sends to the lowest region. Spiritual ascent has prerequisites. Bypassing them does not just fail — it actively reverses the direction.

BG 11.6

Today, with my thunderbolt, which has hundreds of sharp edges, I, the same powerful person, shall sever your head from your body. Although you can produce so much jugglery through illusion, you are endowed with a poor fund of knowledge. Now, try to exist on this battlefield with your relatives and friends.

Essence. Indra threatened to sever Bali's head with the hundred-edged thunderbolt — though Bali can produce jugglery, he has 'a poor fund of knowledge.' The most dangerous combination is power without understanding. Indra is warning of this in his enemy.

BG 11.7

Bali Mahārāja replied: All those present on this battlefield are certainly under the influence of eternal time, and according to their prescribed activities, they are destined to receive fame, victory, defeat and death, one after another.

Essence. Bali replied: all of us on this battlefield are under eternal time; fame, defeat, and death are time's allotments. The demon king speaks Bhagavad-gītā philosophy. Wisdom can be quoted by anyone; living by it is rarer.

BG 11.8

Seeing the movements of time, those who are cognizant of the real truth neither rejoice nor lament for different circumstances. Therefore, because you are jubilant due to your victory, you should be considered not very learned.

Essence. Those who see time clearly neither rejoice nor lament; your jubilation in victory shows you are not very learned. Bali rebukes Indra by Bhagavad-gītā standards. Sometimes opponents diagnose us correctly. Take the teaching even from the wrong source.

BG 11.9

You demigods think that your own selves are the cause of your attaining fame and victory. Because of your ignorance, saintly persons feel sorry for you. Therefore, although your words afflict the heart, we do not accept them.

Essence. You demigods think your selves are the cause of your fame; saints feel sorry for you. Bali identifies the demigods' ignorance: self-as-doer. Even good people can hold the wrong philosophy. Position does not equal correct understanding.

BG 11.10

Śukadeva Gosvāmī said: After thus rebuking Indra, King of heaven, with sharp words, Bali Mahārāja, who could subdue any other hero, drew back to his ear the arrows known as nārācas and attacked Indra with these arrows. Then he again chastised Indra with strong words.

Essence. After his philosophical rebuke, Bali still drew his nāraca arrows and attacked. Words of wisdom can coexist with continued aggression. Right speech and right action are not the same; the test is whether the words have changed the actions.

BG 11.11

Since Mahārāja Bali’s rebukes were truthful, King Indra did not at all become sorry, just as an elephant beaten by its driver’s rod does not become agitated.

Essence. Bali's rebukes were truthful, so Indra was not agitated — like an elephant beaten with the driver's rod. Strong people do not flinch at truthful criticism. Pain from a true rebuke is information, not insult.

BG 11.12

When Indra, the defeater of enemies, released his infallible thunderbolt scepter at Bali Mahārāja with a desire to kill him, Bali Mahārāja indeed fell to the ground with his airplane, like a mountain with its wings cut off.

Essence. Indra released the infallible thunderbolt and Bali fell from his airplane like a winged mountain with cut wings. Even philosophical insight does not deflect karma's results. Wisdom in speech does not exempt one from the consequences of action.

BG 11.13

When the demon Jambhāsura saw that his friend Bali had fallen, he appeared before Indra, the enemy, just to serve Bali Mahārāja with friendly behavior.

Essence. Jambhāsura saw his friend fall and appeared before Indra to serve Bali with friendly behavior. Demonic loyalty also exists. Friendship is a value that crosses moral camps. The Bhāgavatam acknowledges good qualities even in adversaries.

BG 11.14

The greatly powerful Jambhāsura, carried by a lion, approached Indra and forcefully struck him on the shoulder with his club. He also struck Indra’s elephant.

Essence. Jambhāsura on a lion struck Indra's shoulder and his elephant. Devoted service to a fallen friend takes the form of direct attack on the friend's enemy. Loyalty unredirected by dharma can fuel ongoing escalation.

BG 11.15

Being beaten by Jambhāsura’s club, Indra’s elephant was confused and aggrieved. Thus it touched its knees to the ground and fell unconscious.

Essence. Indra's elephant fell unconscious, struck by the demon's club. Even mighty carriers get knocked down. What I rely on for transport in spiritual life is not invincible; planning for its temporary failure is wisdom.

BG 11.16

Thereafter, Mātali, Indra’s chariot driver, brought Indra’s chariot, which was drawn by one thousand horses. Indra then left his elephant and got onto the chariot.

Essence. Mātali, the charioteer, immediately brought a thousand-horse chariot, and Indra transferred. Good support staff make leadership possible. When one vehicle fails, another is ready. Behind every great fighter is a great driver.

BG 11.17

Appreciating Mātali’s service, Jambhāsura, the best of the demons, smiled. Nonetheless, he struck Mātali in the battle with a trident of blazing fire.

Essence. Jambhāsura smiled appreciating Mātali's service — then struck him with a blazing trident. Even demons can recognize excellence in others before destroying them. Recognition does not guarantee mercy. Beware admirers with weapons.

BG 11.18

Although the pain was extremely severe, Mātali tolerated it with great patience. Indra, however, became extremely angry at Jambhāsura. He struck Jambhāsura with his thunderbolt and thus severed his head from his body.

Essence. Mātali bore severe pain with patience while Indra retaliated and killed Jambhāsura. Servants of dharma often absorb the suffering while their lord delivers the final response. Patience under pain is its own kind of strength.

BG 11.19

When Nārada Ṛṣi informed Jambhāsura’s friends and relatives that Jambhāsura had been killed, the three demons named Namuci, Bala and Pāka arrived on the battlefield in great haste.

Essence. Nārada informed Jambhāsura's relatives, and Namuci, Bala, and Pāka rushed to the battlefield in fury. Even the sage's truthful news triggers further conflict. Information, dispassionately delivered, still sets actions in motion.

BG 11.20

Rebuking Indra with harsh, cruel words that were piercing to the heart, these demons showered him with arrows, just as torrents of rain wash a great mountain.

Essence. They rebuked Indra with piercing words and showered arrows like torrents of rain on a mountain. Sustained verbal and physical attack tries to break the immovable. Mountains stand precisely because they have endured many storms.

BG 11.21

Quickly handling the situation on the battlefield, the demon Bala put all of Indra’s one thousand horses into tribulation by simultaneously piercing them all with an equal number of arrows.

Essence. Bala pierced all one thousand of Indra's horses with an equal number of simultaneous arrows. Demonic skill can be technically virtuosic. Do not dismiss enemies as incompetent simply because they are wrong.

BG 11.22

Pāka, another demon, attacked both the chariot, with all its paraphernalia, and the chariot driver, Mātali, by fitting two hundred arrows to his bow and releasing them all simultaneously. This was indeed a wonderful act on the battlefield.

Essence. Pāka shot two hundred arrows at the chariot and Mātali simultaneously — a wonderful act on the battlefield. The Bhāgavatam allows even demonic feats to be called 'wonderful.' Skill is skill; we may admire technique without endorsing the user.

BG 11.23

Then Namuci, another demon, attacked Indra and injured him with fifteen very powerful golden-feathered arrows, which roared like a cloud full of water.

Essence. Namuci injured Indra with fifteen golden-feathered arrows roaring like raincloud thunder. Bright, decorated weapons still injure. The aesthetic of the arrow does not soften its impact. Beauty in violence remains violence.

BG 11.24

Other demons covered Indra, along with his chariot and chariot driver, with incessant showers of arrows, just as clouds cover the sun in the rainy season.

Essence. Other demons covered Indra and his chariot with incessant arrows, like clouds covering the sun in rainy season. Sometimes even the brightest is temporarily obscured. The sun does not stop being sun under clouds; we simply cannot see it.

BG 11.25

The demigods, being severely oppressed by their enemies and being unable to see Indra on the battlefield, were very anxious. Having no captain or leader, they began lamenting like traders in a wrecked vessel in the midst of the ocean.

Essence. With Indra invisible, the demigods felt like traders in a shipwreck — anxious, leaderless. Even upper planetary beings know the panic of lost leadership. Faith in a leader who is temporarily out of sight is one of the hardest tests.

BG 11.26

Thereafter, Indra released himself from the cage of the network of arrows. Appearing with his chariot, flag, horses and chariot driver and thus pleasing the sky, the earth and all directions, he shone effulgently like the sun at the end of night. Indra was bright and beautiful in the vision of everyone.

Essence. Indra released himself from the arrow-cage and shone like the sun at end of night, pleasing sky, earth, and all directions. Apparent absence ends with brilliant return. Sometimes the leader emerges more glorious for having been briefly hidden.

BG 11.27

When Indra, who is known as Vajra-dhara, the carrier of the thunderbolt, saw his own soldiers so oppressed by the enemies on the battlefield, he became very angry. Thus he took up his thunderbolt to kill the enemies.

Essence. Seeing his soldiers oppressed, Indra became angry and took up his thunderbolt. The leader's wrath rises not for himself but for his people. Righteous anger in defense of others is a different quality from personal indignation.

BG 11.28

O King Parīkṣit, King Indra used his thunderbolt to cut off the heads of both Bala and Pāka in the presence of all their relatives and followers. In this way he created a very fearful atmosphere on the battlefield.

Essence. Indra cut off Bala's and Pāka's heads in front of their relatives, creating a fearful atmosphere. Public, decisive consequences shape future behavior. Mercy is not the only dharmic response; appropriate severity also has its place.

BG 11.29

O King, when Namuci, another demon, saw the killing of both Bala and Pāka, he was full of grief and lamentation. Thus he angrily made a great attempt to kill Indra.

Essence. Namuci was filled with grief at seeing Bala and Pāka killed, and angrily attempted to kill Indra. Grief and anger together produce dangerous attacks. Mourning that does not metabolize becomes the next round of violence.

BG 11.30

Being angry and roaring like a lion, the demon Namuci took up a steel spear, which was bound with bells and decorated with ornaments of gold. He loudly cried, “Now you are killed!” Thus coming before Indra to kill him, Namuci released his weapon.

Essence. Roaring like a lion, Namuci took a bell-bound, gold-ornamented steel spear and cried 'Now you are killed!' Loud confidence in announcing victory is not the same as actually achieving it. Bravado precedes many a defeat.

BG 11.31

O King, when Indra, King of heaven, saw this very powerful spear falling toward the ground like a blazing meteor, he immediately cut it to pieces with his arrows. Then, being very angry, he struck Namuci’s shoulder with his thunderbolt to cut off Namuci’s head.

Essence. Indra cut the falling spear to pieces and struck Namuci's shoulder with his thunderbolt. Skillful response: first disarm, then strike. Order matters. Do not strike while the enemy's weapon is still airborne.

BG 11.32

Although King Indra hurled his thunderbolt at Namuci with great force, it could not even pierce his skin. It is very wonderful that the famed thunderbolt that had pierced the body of Vṛtrāsura could not even slightly injure the skin of Namuci’s neck.

Essence. The famed thunderbolt that pierced Vṛtra could not even injure Namuci's skin. Some adversaries have specific protections that render even our best weapons useless. Universal methods fail against particular benedictions.

BG 11.33

When Indra saw the thunderbolt return from the enemy, he was very much afraid. He began to wonder whether this had happened because of some miraculous superior power.

Essence. Seeing his thunderbolt return without effect, Indra was afraid and suspected some miraculous superior power. Even the king of heaven knows fear when his strongest tool fails. Fear is not absence of strength; it is honest recognition of mystery.

BG 11.34

Indra thought: Formerly, when many mountains flying in the sky with wings would fall to the ground and kill people, I cut their wings with this same thunderbolt.

Essence. Indra reviewed his past: with this same thunderbolt he had cut the wings of flying mountains. Memory of past power is comfort and confusion. Past competence does not guarantee present effectiveness.

BG 11.35

Vṛtrāsura was the essence of the austerities undergone by Tvaṣṭā, yet the thunderbolt killed him. Indeed, not only he but also many other stalwart heroes, whose very skin could not be injured even by all kinds of weapons, were killed by the same thunderbolt.

Essence. Vṛtrāsura — essence of Tvaṣṭā's austerities — had been killed by this thunderbolt; many invincible-skinned heroes also. Past triumphs against stronger foes amplify the puzzlement of present failure. The pattern is broken; what is going on?

BG 11.36

But now, although the same thunderbolt has been released against a less important demon, it has been ineffectual. Therefore, although it was as good as a brahmāstra, it has now become useless like an ordinary rod. I shall therefore hold it no longer.

Essence. Indra decided to put down the thunderbolt — useless now like an ordinary rod. Wisdom includes letting go of a tool when it has stopped working. Loyalty to past power becomes superstition if continued past its time.

BG 11.37

Śukadeva Gosvāmī continued: While the morose Indra was lamenting in this way, an ominous, unembodied voice said from the sky, “This demon Namuci is not to be annihilated by anything dry or moist.”

Essence. An unembodied voice declared from the sky: Namuci cannot be killed by anything dry or moist. Divine intervention provides a riddle, not a solution. Hearing the constraint is the beginning of inventing within it.

BG 11.38

The voice also said, “O Indra, because I have given this demon the benediction that he will never be killed by any weapon that is dry or moist, you have to think of another way to kill him.”

Essence. I gave him the boon — no dry or moist weapon can kill him; find another way. Even the demon's protection was given by divine power. The Lord plays both sides, and arranges puzzles that only His own creativity can solve.

BG 11.39

After hearing the ominous voice, Indra, with great attention, began to meditate on how to kill the demon. He then saw that foam would be the means, for it is neither moist nor dry.

Essence. Indra meditated attentively and saw that foam — neither dry nor moist — was the answer. Solutions to riddles emerge from focused attention, not panic. The middle between two named extremes is often the doorway.

BG 11.40

Thus Indra, King of heaven, severed Namuci’s head with a weapon of foam, which was neither dry nor moist. Then all the sages satisfied Indra, the exalted personality, by showering flowers and garlands upon him, almost covering him.

Essence. With foam-as-weapon Indra severed Namuci's head; sages showered him with flowers. Creative solution to an unfair-seeming problem brings universal praise. What looks impossible yields to one who can think between categories.

BG 11.41

Viśvāvasu and Parāvasu, the two chiefs of the Gandharvas, sang in great happiness. The kettledrums of the demigods sounded, and the Apsarās danced in jubilation.

Essence. Gandharvas sang, drums sounded, Apsarās danced in jubilation. Victory celebration is a real part of dharmic life. The sacred world makes time for joy after struggle. Not all is sober work.

BG 11.42

Vāyu, Agni, Varuṇa and other demigods began killing the demons who opposed them, just as lions kill deer in a forest.

Essence. Vāyu, Agni, Varuṇa, and others killed demons like lions killing deer. After the leader's foam-victory, the others followed quickly. Decisive action by the head clears the way for the limbs to act. Pattern-shift cascades.

BG 11.43

O King, when Lord Brahmā saw the imminent total annihilation of the demons, he sent a message with Nārada, who went before the demigods to make them stop fighting.

Essence. Seeing imminent total annihilation of the demons, Brahmā sent Nārada to make the demigods stop. Even the right side, in victory, can go too far. Restraint after success is as crucial as courage in defeat.

BG 11.44

The great sage Nārada said: All of you demigods are protected by the arms of Nārāyaṇa, the Supreme Personality of Godhead, and by His grace you have gotten the nectar. By the grace of the goddess of fortune, you are glorious in every way. Therefore, please stop this fighting.

Essence. Nārada said: you are protected by Nārāyaṇa, you have nectar, Lakṣmī favors you — stop fighting. The reminder of accumulated blessings invites the cessation of further violence. Gratitude is the antidote to extending conflict.

BG 11.45

Śrī Śukadeva Gosvāmī said: Accepting the words of Nārada, the demigods gave up their anger and stopped fighting. Being praised by their followers, they returned to their heavenly planets.

Essence. Accepting Nārada's words, the demigods gave up anger and returned to their heavenly planets, praised by followers. Receiving wise advice and stopping is itself praised. The end of conflict is a victory that earns more honor than the fight.

BG 11.46

Following the order of Nārada Muni, whatever demons remained on the battlefield took Bali Mahārāja, who was in a precarious condition, to the hill known as Astagiri.

Essence. Following Nārada's order, the remaining demons carried the precarious Bali to Astagiri hill. Even defeated parties have caretakers. The instruction to stop applies to all sides. After the battle, every casualty needs evacuation.

BG 11.47

There, on that hill, Śukrācārya brought to life all the dead demoniac soldiers who had not lost their heads, trunks and limbs. He achieved this by his own mantra, known as Saṁjīvanī.

Essence. Śukrācārya used his Saṁjīvanī mantra to revive all dead demons whose limbs were intact. Reactivation requires that the basic structure remain. Mantras work where there is something coherent to restore. Severed parts cannot be re-attached.

BG 11.48

Bali Mahārāja was very experienced in universal affairs. When he regained his senses and memory by the grace of Śukrācārya, he could understand everything that had happened. Therefore, although he had been defeated, he did not lament.

Essence. Bali regained memory and understood — yet did not lament. He knew nothing happens without the Lord's sanction. Even a demon king with this understanding accepts defeat as karma's arrangement. Acceptance is not a devotee monopoly.
