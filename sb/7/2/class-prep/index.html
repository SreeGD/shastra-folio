BG 2.1

Śrī Nārada Muni said: My dear King Yudhiṣṭhira, when Lord Viṣṇu, in the form of Varāha, the boar, killed Hiraṇyākṣa, Hiraṇyākṣa’s brother Hiraṇyakaśipu was extremely angry and began to lament.

Essence. Hiraṇyakaśipu's enmity toward Viṣṇu began with grief over his brother's death. Unresolved anger fuels the cycle of opposition to the Divine. Notice when sorrow is hardening into hatred.

BG 2.2

Filled with rage and biting his lips, Hiraṇyakaśipu gazed at the sky with eyes that blazed in anger, making the whole sky smoky. Thus he began to speak.

Essence. Filled with rage, biting his lips, Hiraṇyakaśipu makes the sky smoky with his anger. Demoniac mentality plans to destroy what it cannot dominate. The body itself betrays the inner state — fierce features mirror a fierce mind.

BG 2.3

Exhibiting his terrible teeth, fierce glance and frowning eyebrows, terrible to see, he took up his weapon, a trident, and thus began speaking to his associates, the assembled demons.

Essence. Brandishing his trident and showing his terrible teeth, Hiraṇyakaśipu rallies his demonic associates. Anger naturally seeks a tribe to amplify it. Watch which company you reach for when you are most upset.

BG 2.4-5

O Dānavas and Daityas! O Dvimūrdha, Tryakṣa, Śambara and Śatabāhu! O Hayagrīva, Namuci, Pāka and Ilvala! O Vipracitti, Puloman, Śakuna and other demons! All of you, kindly hear me attentively and then act according to my words without delay.

Essence. Hiraṇyakaśipu addresses each demon by name, organizing them for action. Even destructive movements depend on careful coordination. Whenever a group is mobilized, ask who is being named, what is being asked, and toward what end.

BG 2.6

My insignificant enemies the demigods have combined to kill my very dear and obedient well-wisher, my brother Hiraṇyākṣa. Although the Supreme Lord, Viṣṇu, is always equal to both of us — namely, the demigods and the demons — this time, being devoutly worshiped by the demigods, He has taken their side and helped them kill Hiraṇyākṣa.

Essence. Hiraṇyakaśipu accuses Viṣṇu of partiality — but the Lord helps the demigods because they follow His instructions. The 'partiality' we see in life is often our own refusal to align with what is being asked of us. God responds to sincerity, not flattery.

BG 2.7-8

The Supreme Personality of Godhead has given up His natural tendency of equality toward the demons and demigods. Although He is the Supreme Person, now, influenced by māyā, He has assumed the form of a boar to please His devotees, the demigods, just as a restless child leans toward someone. I shall therefore sever Lord Viṣṇu’s head from His trunk by my trident, and with the profuse blood from His body I shall please my brother Hiraṇyākṣa, who was so fond of sucking blood. Thus shall I too be peaceful.

Essence. Hiraṇyakaśipu imagines he can behead Viṣṇu with his trident and please his brother with blood. The demoniac mind projects its own conditioned nature onto the Supreme — fickle, killable, vengeful. We always see God through the lens of who we are.

BG 2.9

When the root of a tree is cut and the tree falls down, its branches and twigs automatically dry up. Similarly, when I have killed this diplomatic Viṣṇu, the demigods, for whom Lord Viṣṇu is the life and soul, will lose the source of their life and wither away.

Essence. Hiraṇyakaśipu reasons that if he can kill Viṣṇu, the demigods will wither like a felled tree. Demons sometimes unintentionally glorify the demigods by recognizing their full dependence on the Lord. Even enemies confirm where the real strength lies.

BG 2.10

While I am engaged in the business of killing Lord Viṣṇu, go down to the planet earth, which is flourishing due to brahminical culture and a kṣatriya government. These people engage in austerity, sacrifice, Vedic study, regulative vows, and charity. Destroy all the people thus engaged!

Essence. Hiraṇyakaśipu orders his demons to attack earth's brahminical and kṣatriya culture — places of austerity, sacrifice, Vedic study, and charity. Demonic forces always target the institutions that sustain spiritual life. Protect the structures that produce saints.

BG 2.11

The basic principle of brahminical culture is to satisfy Lord Viṣṇu, the personification of sacrificial and ritualistic ceremonies. Lord Viṣṇu is the personified reservoir of all religious principles, and He is the shelter of all the demigods, the great pitās, and the people in general. When the brāhmaṇas are killed, no one will exist to encourage the kṣatriyas to perform yajṣas, and thus the demigods, not being appeased by yajṣa, will automatically die.

Essence. Brahminical culture's goal is to satisfy Viṣṇu through yajña; when brāhmaṇas are killed, sacrifice ceases, and even the demigods die. A society without spiritual specialists eventually collapses at every level. Honor those whose lives are devoted to remembrance.

BG 2.12

Immediately go wherever there is good protection for the cows and brāhmaṇas and wherever the Vedas are studied in terms of the varṇāśrama principles. Set fire to those places and cut from the roots the trees there, which are the source of life.

Essence. Hiraṇyakaśipu targets cows, brāhmaṇas, and Vedic places — burning forests and cutting trees by the roots. Demonic civilization attacks the gentle, the contemplative, and the natural together. A culture that cannot protect cows and saints is in trouble.

BG 2.13

Thus the demons, being fond of disastrous activities, took Hiraṇyakaśipu’s instructions on their heads with great respect and offered him obeisances. According to his directions, they engaged in envious activities directed against all living beings.

Essence. The demons receive Hiraṇyakaśipu's orders with reverence and go forth envious of all beings. Modern parallels include weapons of mass destruction — discoveries celebrated as progress that ultimately harm everyone. Watch which 'advances' are really envy in disguise.

BG 2.14

The demons set fire to the cities, villages, pasturing grounds, cowpens, gardens, agricultural fields and natural forests. They burned the hermitages of the saintly persons, the important mines that produced valuable metals, the residential quarters of the agriculturalists, the mountain villages, and the villages of the cow protectors, the cowherd men. They also burned the government capitals.

Essence. The demons burn cities, villages, pastures, hermitages, gardens, fields, mines, and even government capitals — leaving nothing of culture standing. Destruction without discrimination is the signature of demonic energy. Build slowly; burn nothing in haste.

BG 2.15

Some of the demons took digging instruments and broke down the bridges, the protective walls and the gates [gopuras] of the cities. Some took axes and began cutting the important trees that produced mango, jackfruit and other sources of food. Some of the demons took firebrands and set fire to the residential quarters of the citizens.

Essence. Some demons break bridges and walls, others cut fruit-bearing trees like mango and jackfruit, others torch homes. Śāstra forbids cutting trees that feed people. A civilization is measured by what it refuses to destroy.

BG 2.16

Thus disturbed again and again by the unnatural occurrences caused by the followers of Hiraṇyakaśipu, all the people had to cease the activities of Vedic culture. Not receiving the results of yajṣa, the demigods also became disturbed. They left their residential quarters in the heavenly planets and, unobserved by the demons, began wandering on the planet earth to see the disasters.

Essence. Vedic activity ceases under demonic disturbance, yajña stops, demigods receive no offerings, and they wander earth disguised to see the damage. When sacrifice stops, the unseen world also suffers — and the wise come quietly to assess.

BG 2.17

After performing the ritualistic observances for the death of his brother, Hiraṇyakaśipu, being extremely unhappy, tried to pacify his nephews.

Essence. After the funeral rites, Hiraṇyakaśipu — still grieving — tries to comfort his nephews. Even the demonic feel familial tenderness. The capacity to console others remains even in those most hardened by the world.

BG 2.18-19

O King, Hiraṇyakaśipu was extremely angry, but since he was a great politician, he knew how to act according to the time and situation. With sweet words he began pacifying his nephews, whose names were Śakuni, Śambara, Dhṛṣṭi, Bhūtasantāpana, Vṛka, Kālanābha, Mahānābha, Hariśmaśru and Utkaca. He also consoled their mother, his sister-in-law, Ruṣābhānu, as well as his own mother, Diti. He spoke to them all as follows.

Essence. Furious yet politically shrewd, Hiraṇyakaśipu pacifies his nephews, sister-in-law, and mother with sweet words. The demonic can be eloquent and gentle when strategy demands it. Charm is not the same as character.

BG 2.20

Hiraṇyakaśipu said: My dear mother, sister-in-law and nephews, you should not lament for the death of the great hero, for a hero’s death in front of his enemy is glorious and desirable.

Essence. Hiraṇyakaśipu tells the bereaved that a hero's death facing the enemy is glorious. Worldly codes of honor reframe death to make it bearable. Notice when 'glory' is being used to anesthetize real grief.

BG 2.21

My dear mother, in a restaurant or place for drinking cold water, many travelers are brought together, and after drinking water they continue to their respective destinations. Similarly, living entities join together in a family, and later, as a result of their own actions, they are led apart to their destinations.

Essence. Like travelers meeting at a roadside rest and then parting, families gather briefly and disperse according to karma. The intimacy you treasure is real but provisional. Hold those given to you well; do not pretend the meeting is permanent.

BG 2.22

The spirit soul, the living entity, has no death, for he is eternal and inexhaustible. Being free from material contamination, he can go anywhere in the material or spiritual worlds. He is fully aware and completely different from the material body, but because of being misled by misuse of his slight independence, he is obliged to accept subtle and gross bodies created by the material energy and thus be subjected to so-called material happiness and distress. Therefore, no one should lament for the passing of the spirit soul from the body.

Essence. The soul is eternal, indestructible, and free to roam — but misuse of free will lands it in subtle and gross bodies. So-called material happiness and distress arise from this misuse. Lament not for the soul's passage; examine your own use of free will.

BG 2.23

Because of the movements of the water, the trees on the bank of a river, when reflected on the water, seem to move. Similarly, when the eyes move because of some mental derangement, the land appears to move also.

Essence. When water moves, reflected trees seem to move; when the mind is deranged, the land itself seems to shift. Much of what we perceive as 'happening to us' is mental disturbance projected outward. The soul itself does not actually go anywhere.

BG 2.24

In the same way, O my gentle mother, when the mind is agitated by the movements of the modes of material nature, the living entity, although freed from all the different phases of the subtle and gross bodies, thinks that he has changed from one condition to another.

Essence. When the modes agitate the mind, the soul — though always free — imagines it is changing states. The transitions you mourn or celebrate are happening in the modes, not in your true self. Identifying with the unchanging witness ends the drama.

BG 2.25-26

In his bewildered state, the living entity, accepting the body and mind to be the self, considers some people to be his kinsmen and others to be outsiders. Because of this misconception, he suffers. Indeed, the accumulation of such concocted material ideas is the cause of suffering and so-called happiness in the material world. The conditioned soul thus situated must take birth in different species and work in various types of consciousness, thus creating new bodies. This continued material life is called saṁsāra. Birth, death, lamentation, foolishness and anxiety are due to such material considerations. Thus we sometimes come to a proper understanding and sometimes fall again to a wrong conception of life.

Essence. Mistaking body and mind for self, the conditioned soul carves the world into kin and outsiders, and suffers accordingly. The accumulation of such notions IS saṁsāra. Loosen the false categories and the suffering loosens with them.

BG 2.27

In this regard, an example is given from an old history. This involves a discourse between Yamarāja and the friends of a dead person. Please hear it attentively.

Essence. Hiraṇyakaśipu cites an old history — a dialogue between Yamarāja and grieving relatives. The Purāṇas, though not chronological, record real events; their stories serve as mirrors for our own grief and confusion. Trust the wisdom carried in ancient narrative.

BG 2.28

In the state known as Uśīnara there was a celebrated king named Suyajṣa. When the King was killed in battle by his enemies, his kinsmen sat down around the dead body and began to lament the death of their friend.

Essence. King Suyajña, killed in battle, lies surrounded by lamenting kinsmen. Even kings die in war, and even the powerful are mourned. No status protects from the inevitable; only knowledge of the soul does.

BG 2.29-31

His golden, bejeweled armor smashed, his ornaments and garlands fallen from their places, his hair scattered and his eyes lusterless, the slain King lay on the battlefield, his entire body smeared with blood, his heart pierced by the arrows of the enemy. When he died he had wanted to show his prowess, and thus he had bitten his lips, and his teeth remained in that position. His beautiful lotuslike face was now black and covered with dust from the battlefield. His arms, with his sword and other weapons, were cut and broken. When the queens of the King of Uśīnara saw their husband lying in that position, they began crying, “O lord, now that you have been killed, we also have been killed.” Repeating these words again and again, they fell down, pounding their breasts, at the feet of the dead King.

Essence. Suyajña's queens fall on his shattered body, weeping that they too have died with him. Worldly love often defines itself by what cannot survive separation. The shock of death exposes how identified we are with another person's body.

BG 2.32

As the queens loudly cried, their tears glided down their breasts, becoming reddened by kuṅkuma powder, and fell upon the lotus feet of their husband. Their hair became disarrayed, their ornaments fell, and in a way that evoked sympathy from the hearts of others, the queens began lamenting their husband’s death.

Essence. The queens' tears mix with kuṅkuma and fall onto the dead king's feet; their hair is undone, their ornaments scattered. The aesthetic of mourning hides how little it can do for the dead. Beautiful grief is still grief in ignorance.

BG 2.33

O lord, you have now been removed by cruel providence to a state beyond our sight. You had previously sustained the livelihood of the inhabitants of Uśīnara, and thus they were happy, but your condition now is the cause of their unhappiness.

Essence. The queens cry that cruel providence has snatched their lord away. Conditioned grief always blames an external force — fate, God, circumstance. Naming providence as 'cruel' is a refusal to enter the mystery of its plan.

BG 2.34

O King, O hero, you were a very grateful husband and the most sincere friend of all of us. How shall we exist without you? O hero, wherever you are going, please direct us there so that we may follow in your footsteps and engage again in your service. Let us go along with you!

Essence. The queens beg to follow the dead king wherever he is going, ready for saha-māraṇa. Devotion to a person can be so total that the survivor sees no life apart from them. Such intensity of attachment, if redirected toward the Lord, would be liberation itself.

BG 2.35

The time was appropriate for the body to be burned, but the queens, not allowing it to be taken away, continued lamenting for the dead body, which they kept on their laps. In the meantime, the sun completed its movements for setting in the west.

Essence. The queens refuse to release the body for cremation as the sun sets. Illusion is stubborn — it keeps a corpse on its lap rather than admit the soul has gone. Holding tight to forms after their life has fled is one of grief's cruelest forms.

BG 2.36

While the queens were lamenting for the dead body of the King, their loud cries were heard even from the abode of Yamarāja. Assuming the body of a boy, Yamarāja personally approached the relatives of the dead body and advised them as follows.

Essence. Yamarāja himself, hearing the cries, comes as a small boy to instruct the grieving. Death personified arrives in the form of innocence. Sometimes the deepest teaching about loss comes from the most unexpected messenger.

BG 2.37

Śrī Yamarāja said: Alas, how amazing it is! These persons, who are older than me, have full experience that hundreds and thousands of living entities have taken birth and died. Thus they should understand that they also are apt to die, yet still they are bewildered. The conditioned soul comes from an unknown place and returns after death to that same unknown place. There is no exception to this rule, which is conducted by material nature. Knowing this, why do they uselessly lament?

Essence. The boy Yamarāja marvels that adults who have witnessed countless deaths still think themselves exempt. The soul comes from an unknown place and returns there — why lament a passage no one controls? Knowledge does not require us to like the news; it asks us to stop being surprised.

BG 2.38

It is wonderful that these elderly women do not have a higher sense of life than we do. Indeed, we are most fortunate, for although we are children and have been left to struggle in material life, unprotected by father and mother, and although we are very weak, we have not been vanquished or eaten by ferocious animals. Thus we have a firm belief that the Supreme Personality of Godhead, who has given us protection even in the womb of the mother, will protect us everywhere.

Essence. The boy points out that he and the other children survived in the womb and survived being abandoned — the Lord protects everywhere. Childlike trust in the Supreme is a higher knowledge than adult grief. Faith in the Lord's care is the antidote to existential terror.

BG 2.39

The boy addressed the women: O weak women! Only by the will of the Supreme Personality of Godhead, who is never diminished, is the entire world created, maintained and again annihilated. This is the verdict of the Vedic knowledge. This material creation, consisting of the moving and nonmoving, is exactly like His plaything. Being the Supreme Lord, He is completely competent to destroy and protect.

Essence. Creation, maintenance, and dissolution all happen by the Lord's will — the universe is His plaything. We cannot demand consistency in His protection; He acts freely because He is supreme. Surrender accepts this without bargaining.

BG 2.40

Sometimes one loses his money on a public street, where everyone can see it, and yet his money is protected by destiny and not seen by others. Thus the man who lost it gets it back. On the other hand, if the Lord does not give protection, even money maintained very securely at home is lost. If the Supreme Lord gives one protection, even though one has no protector and is in the jungle, one remains alive, whereas a person well protected at home by relatives and others sometimes dies, no one being able to protect him.

Essence. Money lost in plain sight may be returned by destiny; money locked at home may vanish. A person alone in the jungle may live; one surrounded by family may die. Protection comes from the Lord, not from arrangements. Trust the Source, not the safeguards.

BG 2.41

Every conditioned soul receives a different type of body according to his work, and when the engagement is finished the body is finished. Although the spirit soul is situated in subtle and gross material bodies in different forms of life, he is not bound by them, for he is always understood to be completely different from the manifested body.

Essence. Each conditioned soul takes a body suited to its karma; when the task is done, the body ends. The soul, distinct from gross and subtle bodies, is never bound by them. God is not responsible for your body — your past activities are.

BG 2.42

Just as a householder, although different from the identity of his house, thinks his house to be identical with him, so the conditioned soul, due to ignorance, accepts the body to be himself, although the body is actually different from the soul. This body is obtained through a combination of portions of earth, water and fire, and when the earth, water and fire are transformed in the course of time, the body is vanquished. The soul has nothing to do with this creation and dissolution of the body.

Essence. A householder identifies with his house though they are wholly different. So we identify with our body, made of earth, water, and fire, even though the soul has nothing to do with its creation or dissolution. Notice the constant slip into mistaken identity.

BG 2.43

As fire, although situated in wood, is perceived to be different from the wood, as air, although situated within the mouth and nostrils, is perceived to be separate, and as the sky, although all-pervading, never mixes with anything, so the living entity, although now encaged within the material body, of which it is the source, is separate from it.

Essence. Fire is in wood but distinct from it; air moves through nostrils but is not the body; sky pervades all yet mingles with nothing. So the living entity, though housed in the body, is separate from it. Closeness is not identity.

BG 2.44

Yamarāja continued: O lamenters, you are all fools! The person named Suyajṣa, for whom you lament, is still lying before you and has not gone anywhere. Then what is the cause for your lamentation? Previously he heard you and replied to you, but now, not finding him, you are lamenting. This is contradictory behavior, for you have never actually seen the person within the body who heard you and replied. There is no need for your lamentation, for the body you have always seen is lying here.

Essence. Yamarāja points out that Suyajña's body is still right there — what is missing is the one who heard and replied. Death proves that what mattered was never the body. Every funeral is a confession that we loved the soul all along.

BG 2.45

In the body the most important substance is the life air, but that also is neither the listener nor the speaker. Beyond even the life air, the soul also can do nothing, for the Supersoul is actually the director, in cooperation with the individual soul. The Supersoul conducting the activities of the body is different from the body and living force.

Essence. Even the life-air is not the listener or speaker; the soul acts only with the Supersoul's sanction. We are not even the doers we think we are — every breath is sanctioned. Humility before this fact is the start of devotional life.

BG 2.46

The five material elements, the ten senses and the mind all combine to form the various parts of the gross and subtle bodies. The living entity comes in contact with his material bodies, whether high or low, and later gives them up by his personal prowess. This strength can be perceived in a living entity’s personal power to possess different types of bodies.

Essence. The five elements, ten senses, and mind combine into bodies high and low; the soul acquires and discards these by its own power and direction. With knowledge from a true ācārya, one can stop choosing more bodies and return home. Right teaching unlocks real freedom.

BG 2.47

As long as the spirit soul is covered by the subtle body, consisting of the mind, intelligence and false ego, he is bound to the results of his fruitive activities. Because of this covering, the spirit soul is connected with the material energy and must accordingly suffer material conditions and reversals, continually, life after life.

Essence. As long as the subtle body — mind, intelligence, false ego — covers the soul, fruitive reactions follow life after life. The mind at the time of death determines the next body. Practice the engagement of mind in Kṛṣṇa now, so the final moment finds it already trained.

BG 2.48

It is fruitless to see and talk of the material modes of nature and their resultant so-called happiness and distress as if they were factual. When the mind wanders during the day and a man begins to think himself extremely important, or when he dreams at night and sees a beautiful woman enjoying with him, these are merely false dreams. Similarly, the happiness and distress caused by the material senses should be understood to be meaningless.

Essence. Sensory happiness and distress are no more real than a dream of a beautiful woman or a vision of one's own greatness during the day. Treating these as factual is the great mistake. Engage the mind in the Lord; the rest is wind.

BG 2.49

Those who have full knowledge of self-realization, who know very well that the spirit soul is eternal whereas the body is perishable, are not overwhelmed by lamentation. But persons who lack knowledge of self-realization certainly lament. Therefore it is difficult to educate a person in illusion.

Essence. Those who know the soul is eternal and the body perishable do not lament. Those without self-realization continue grieving even after teaching. It is hard to educate one who is in illusion — but the effort is the most worthwhile education there is.

BG 2.50

There was once a hunter who lured birds with food and captured them after spreading a net. He lived as if appointed by death personified as the killer of the birds.

Essence. Yamarāja begins a fresh parable: a hunter set out food and nets to capture birds, as if appointed by death itself. Some forces in our lives are professional captors, dressed as opportunity. Recognize lures.

BG 2.51

While wandering in the forest, the hunter saw a pair of kuliṅga birds. Of the two, the female was captivated by the hunter’s lure.

Essence. Of two kuliṅga birds, the female is captivated by the hunter's lure. One partner is often the first drawn into a snare. The danger to a couple usually enters through one heart first.

BG 2.52

O queens of Suyajṣa, the male kuliṅga bird, seeing his wife put into the greatest danger in the grip of Providence, became very unhappy. Because of affection, the poor bird, being unable to release her, began to lament for his wife.

Essence. The male kuliṅga, unable to free his mate, laments in helpless affection. Love that cannot save still pours out, because that is love's nature. But helpless grief alone changes nothing; only awakening to the Lord's larger plan does.

BG 2.53

Alas, how merciless is Providence! My wife, unable to be helped by anyone, is in such an awkward position and lamenting for me. What will Providence gain by taking away this poor bird? What will be the profit?

Essence. The bird wails: how merciless is providence — what does He gain by taking a poor creature like my wife? Suffering breeds the demand that God justify Himself. But the question 'what does He gain?' applies only to a needy God; the true Lord gains nothing.

BG 2.54

If unkind Providence takes away my wife, who is half my body, why should He not take me also? What is the use of my living with half of my body, bereaved by loss of my wife? What shall I gain in this way?

Essence. The bird begs to be taken too, calling his wife 'half my body.' Worldly devotion can be so total that the survivor pictures no future. Yet grief that begs for death has not yet met the truth that the loved one was never the body.

BG 2.55

The unfortunate baby birds, bereft of their mother, are waiting in the nest for her to feed them. They are still very small and have not yet grown their wings. How shall I be able to maintain them?

Essence. The bird worries: how will I feed the unfledged chicks now that their mother is gone? Real responsibilities follow loss, and the mind reaches for them in panic. The Lord protects even orphans — as the small Yamarāja-boy has already shown.

BG 2.56

Because of the loss of his wife, the kuliṅga bird lamented with tears in his eyes. Meanwhile, following the dictations of mature time, the hunter, who was very carefully hidden in the distance, released his arrow, which pierced the body of the kuliṅga bird and killed him.

Essence. While the male kuliṅga laments, the patient hunter releases his arrow and kills him too. Excessive grief blinds us to the arrow already taking aim. Mourning that abandons vigilance leaves us doubly exposed.

BG 2.57

Thus Yamarāja, in the guise of a small boy, told all the queens: You are all so foolish that you lament but do not see your own death. Afflicted by a poor fund of knowledge, you do not know that even if you lament for your dead husband for hundreds of years, you will never get him back alive, and in the meantime your lives will be finished.

Essence. Yamarāja-as-boy tells the queens: you lament without seeing your own death. Hundreds of years of grief will not bring him back, and meanwhile your life will be over. The most wonderful thing in this world is that everyone dies yet no one prepares.

BG 2.58

Hiraṇyakaśipu said: While Yamarāja, in the form of a small boy, was instructing all the relatives surrounding the dead body of Suyajṣa, everyone was struck with wonder by his philosophical words. They could understand that everything material is temporary, not continuing to exist.

Essence. Hiraṇyakaśipu notes that everyone was struck with wonder by the boy's philosophy and understood that material things do not endure. Truth is recognizable even when delivered by a child. Wonder is the first step in admitting what you already half-knew.

BG 2.59

After instructing all the foolish relatives of Suyajṣa, Yamarāja, in the form of a boy, disappeared from their vision. Then the relatives of King Suyajṣa performed the ritualistic funeral ceremonies.

Essence. After teaching, Yamarāja-as-boy vanishes, and the relatives finally perform the funeral rites. Wisdom often arrives, delivers its message, and disappears — leaving us to do the actual work. Receive the teaching, then act.

BG 2.60

Therefore none of you should be aggrieved for the loss of the body — whether your own or those of others. Only in ignorance does one make bodily distinctions, thinking “Who am I? Who are the others? What is mine? What is for others?”

Essence. Hiraṇyakaśipu concludes: do not grieve for bodies, your own or others'. The questions 'who am I, who are others, what is mine, what is theirs?' arise only from ignorance. Even a demon king can voice this truth without living it.

BG 2.61

Śrī Nārada Muni continued: Diti, the mother of Hiraṇyakaśipu and Hiraṇyākṣa, heard the instructions of Hiraṇyakaśipu along with her daughter-in-law, Ruṣābhānu, Hiraṇyākṣa’s wife. She then forgot her grief over her son’s death and thus engaged her mind and attention in understanding the real philosophy of life.

Essence. Diti and Ruṣābhānu, hearing Hiraṇyakaśipu, set aside grief and turn to philosophy. Grief at a death often opens a brief window for real inquiry — śmaśāna-vairāgya. The challenge is to extend that window past the funeral.
