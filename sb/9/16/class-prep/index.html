Chapter 16 · Class prep

BG 16.1

Śukadeva Gosvāmī said: My dear Mahārāja Parīkṣit, son of the Kuru dynasty, when Lord Paraśurāma was given this order by his father, he immediately agreed, saying, “Let it be so.” For one complete year he traveled to holy places. Then he returned to his father’s residence.

Essence. Paraśurāma takes his father's order — atone by pilgrimage for a year — without argument. The right response to elder correction is immediate, full agreement and a full year, not partial gestures.

BG 16.2

Once when Reṇukā, the wife of Jamadagni, went to the bank of the Ganges to get water, she saw the King of the Gandharvas, decorated with a garland of lotuses and sporting in the Ganges with celestial women [Apsarās].

Essence. Reṇukā sees the Gandharva king sporting in the Ganges with celestial women. Sense input received in passing — a glimpse on the way to do something else — can lodge in the heart and reshape the rest of the day.

BG 16.3

She had gone to bring water from the Ganges, but when she saw Citraratha, the King of the Gandharvas, sporting with the celestial girls, she was somewhat inclined toward him and failed to remember that the time for the fire sacrifice was passing.

Essence. Inclined toward the scene, she forgets the time for the fire sacrifice. Lust does not always need to act — sometimes its only damage is the schedule it disturbs and the duty it makes us miss.

BG 16.4

Later, understanding that the time for offering the sacrifice had passed, Reṇukā feared a curse from her husband. Therefore when she returned she simply put the waterpot before him and stood there with folded hands.

Essence. Returning, Reṇukā silently sets the pot before her husband and stands with folded hands. We may say nothing, but our body and timing already announce that something happened — silent shame is a kind of confession.

BG 16.5

The great sage Jamadagni understood the adultery in the mind of his wife. Therefore he was very angry and told his sons, “My dear sons, kill this sinful woman!” But the sons did not carry out his order.

Essence. Jamadagni perceives the inner adultery and orders his sons to kill her. The harshness is shocking, yet the underlying principle is uncompromising — even imagined infidelity is treated as a real spiritual event.

BG 16.6

Jamadagni then ordered his youngest son, Paraśurāma, to kill his brothers, who had disobeyed this order, and his mother, who had mentally committed adultery. Lord Paraśurāma, knowing the power of his father, who was practiced in meditation and austerity, killed his mother and brothers immediately.

Essence. Paraśurāma kills mother and brothers because he knows his father's spiritual power. Sometimes the highest devotion to a senior takes a form that horrifies ordinary feeling — and only later reveals the wisdom behind it.

BG 16.7

Jamadagni, the son of Satyavatī, was very much pleased with Paraśurāma and asked him to take any benediction he liked. Lord Paraśurāma replied, “Let my mother and brothers live again and not remember having been killed by me. This is the benediction I ask.”

Essence. Offered any boon, Paraśurāma asks that his mother and brothers be revived without memory of the killing. The truest filial love uses benediction to undo harm done in obedience.

BG 16.8

Thereafter, by the benediction of Jamadagni, Lord Paraśurāma’s mother and brothers immediately came alive and were very happy, as if awakened from sound sleep. Lord Paraśurāma had killed his relatives in accordance with his father’s order because he was fully aware of his father’s power, austerity and learning.

Essence. Mother and brothers awaken 'as from sound sleep.' Even severe consequences can be reversed by the Lord's mercy when the doer carried out the order without ego.

BG 16.9

My dear King Parīkṣit, the sons of Kārtavīryārjuna, who were defeated by the superior strength of Paraśurāma, never achieved happiness, for they always remembered the killing of their father.

Essence. Kārtavīryārjuna's sons never know happiness — they always remember their father's killing. Unhealed grief in a family becomes a generational drag on every joy.

BG 16.10

Once when Paraśurāma left the āśrama for the forest with Vasumān and his other brothers, the sons of Kārtavīryārjuna took the opportunity to approach Jamadagni’s residence to seek vengeance for their grudge.

Essence. When Paraśurāma is away in the forest, his enemies take the opportunity to act. Foes wait for our absence — security and presence are part of the protection we owe to those at home.

BG 16.11

The sons of Kārtavīryārjuna were determined to commit sinful deeds. Therefore when they saw Jamadagni sitting by the side of the fire to perform yajṣa and meditating upon the Supreme Personality of Godhead, who is praised by the best of selected prayers, they took the opportunity to kill him.

Essence. They strike when Jamadagni is meditating on the Lord. Most attacks on devotional life come at the moment of deepest absorption — precisely because that is the moment we are least defended.

BG 16.12

With pitiable prayers, Reṇukā, the mother of Paraśurāma and wife of Jamadagni, begged for the life of her husband. But the sons of Kārtavīryārjuna, being devoid of the qualities of kṣatriyas, were so cruel that despite her prayers they forcibly cut off his head and took it away.

Essence. Reṇukā's pleading does not move the cruel sons. Some hearts have so devolved from kṣatriya into vengeful instrument that no maternal voice can reach them.

BG 16.13

Lamenting in grief for the death of her husband, the most chaste Reṇukā struck her own body with her hands and cried very loudly, “O Rāma, my dear son Rāma!”

Essence. Reṇukā strikes her own body and cries 'O Rāma, O my son!' Devotional grief calls out for the protector by name — and that call carries across any distance.

BG 16.14

Although the sons of Jamadagni, including Lord Paraśurāma, were a long distance from home, as soon as they heard Reṇukā loudly calling “O Rāma, O my son,” they hastily returned to the āśrama, where they saw their father already killed.

Essence. Far from home, Paraśurāma and his brothers hear the mother's cry and race back. Real love is sensitive across distance — and great souls can be reached by a single sincere cry from one who belongs to them.

BG 16.15

Virtually bewildered by grief, anger, indignation, affliction and lamentation, the sons of Jamadagni cried, “O father, most religious, saintly person, you have left us and gone to the heavenly planets!”

Essence. The sons cry that their saintly father has 'gone to the heavenly planets.' Even bereaved devotees frame death in spiritually meaningful terms — grief is not denied, but it is placed in a larger context.

BG 16.16

Thus lamenting, Lord Paraśurāma entrusted his father’s dead body to his brothers and personally took up his axe, having decided to put an end to all the kṣatriyas on the surface of the world.

Essence. Paraśurāma entrusts the body to his brothers and personally takes up the axe. Division of labor in spiritual crisis is essential — not everyone fights, and not everyone tends the body, but each has a role.

BG 16.17

O King, Lord Paraśurāma then went to Māhiṣmatī, which was already doomed by the sinful killing of a brāhmaṇa. In the midst of that city he made a mountain of heads, severed from the bodies of the sons of Kārtavīryārjuna.

Essence. In the middle of Māhiṣmatī, Paraśurāma makes a mountain of heads from the sons of Kārtavīryārjuna. The Lord does not soften the picture; the consequences for offending brāhmaṇas are visible and intentionally remembered.

BG 16.18-19

With the blood of the bodies of these sons, Lord Paraśurāma created a ghastly river, which brought great fear to the kings who had no respect for brahminical culture. Because the kṣatriyas, the men of power in government, were performing sinful activities, Lord Paraśurāma, on the plea of retaliating for the murder of his father, rid all the kṣatriyas from the face of the earth twenty-one times. Indeed, in the place known as Samanta-paṣcaka he created nine lakes filled with their blood.

Essence. Twenty-one times Paraśurāma clears the earth of kṣatriyas, creating nine lakes of blood at Samanta-paṣcaka. The Lord's eternal mission is paritrāṇāya sādhūnāṁ vināśāya ca duṣkṛtām — protecting devotees and ending miscreants, sometimes on a massive scale.

BG 16.20

Thereafter, Paraśurāma joined his father’s head to the dead body and placed the whole body and head upon kuśa grass. By offering sacrifices, he began to worship Lord Vāsudeva, who is the all-pervading Supersoul of all the demigods and of every living entity.

Essence. Paraśurāma reassembles his father's body, places it on kuśa grass, and worships the all-pervading Lord. After even cosmic-scale violence, the devotee returns to worship — sacrifice is the home base, not the battle.

BG 16.21-22

After completing the sacrifice, Lord Paraśurāma gave the eastern direction to the hotā as a gift, the south to the brahmā, the west to the adhvaryu, the north to the udgātā, and the four corners — northeast, southeast, northwest and southwest — to the other priests. He gave the middle to Kaśyapa and the place known as Āryāvarta to the upadraṣṭā. Whatever remained he distributed among the sadasyas, the associate priests.

Essence. Paraśurāma gives directions, corners, middle, and Āryāvarta itself away to the priests. After his greatest deed, he keeps nothing — even the land he avenged becomes a gift to others.

BG 16.23

Thereafter, having completed the ritualistic sacrificial ceremonies, Lord Paraśurāma took the bath known as the avabhṛtha-snāna. Standing on the bank of the great river Sarasvatī, cleared of all sins, Lord Paraśurāma appeared like the sun in a clear, cloudless sky.

Essence. After the rituals, Paraśurāma bathes on the Sarasvatī and stands clear 'like the sun in a cloudless sky.' Properly completed sacrifice produces a visible cleanness that no merely private process can.

BG 16.24

Thus Jamadagni, being worshiped by Lord Paraśurāma, was brought back to life with full remembrance, and he became one of the seven sages in the group of seven stars.

Essence. Jamadagni returns to life with full memory and joins the seven sages of the saptarṣi-maṇḍala. Devoted lives, even violently interrupted, often land in places that ordinary lives never reach.

BG 16.25

My dear King Parīkṣit, in the next manvantara the lotus-eyed Personality of Godhead Lord Paraśurāma, the son of Jamadagni, will be a great propounder of Vedic knowledge. In other words, he will be one of the seven sages.

Essence. Paraśurāma will be one of the seven sages in the next manvantara — a future role even after a finished current pastime. The Lord's incarnations have careers across kalpas, not just within one chapter.

BG 16.26

Lord Paraśurāma still lives as an intelligent brāhmaṇa in the mountainous country known as Mahendra. Completely satisfied, having given up all the weapons of a kṣatriya, he is always worshiped, adored and offered prayers for his exalted character and activities by such celestial beings as the Siddhas, Cāraṇas and Gandharvas.

Essence. Paraśurāma still lives at Mahendra, having given up weapons, satisfied, worshiped by Siddhas and Gandharvas. The wrathful Lord settles into peaceful retirement — every righteous fight, after its time, deserves to be laid down.

BG 16.27

In this way the supreme soul, the Supreme Personality of Godhead, the Lord and the supreme controller, descended as an incarnation in the Bhṛgu dynasty and released the universe from the burden of undesirable kings by killing them many times.

Essence. The Lord descended into the Bhṛgu dynasty to release the universe from the burden of undesirable kings. Every painful clearing in history is, at depth, the Lord lightening the earth for those who would otherwise be crushed.

BG 16.28

Viśvāmitra, the son of Mahārāja Gādhi, was as powerful as the flames of fire. From the position of a kṣatriya, he achieved the position of a powerful brāhmaṇa by undergoing penances and austerities.

Essence. Viśvāmitra, born a kṣatriya, achieves the position of brāhmaṇa through tapasya. Spiritual status is not always inherited — sustained austerity can promote a soul beyond its starting category.

BG 16.29

O King Parīkṣit, Viśvāmitra had 101 sons, of whom the middle one was known as Madhucchandā. In relation to him, all the other sons were celebrated as the Madhucchandās.

Essence. Viśvāmitra has 101 sons, fifty older and fifty younger than Madhucchandā in the middle. Families come in unequal sizes; positions in birth order shape the destinies that follow.

BG 16.30

Viśvāmitra accepted the son of Ajīgarta known as Śunaḥśepha, who was born in the Bhṛgu dynasty and was also known as Devarāta, as one of his own sons. Viśvāmitra ordered his other sons to accept Śunaḥśepha as their eldest brother.

Essence. Viśvāmitra adopts Śunaḥśepha as a son and orders the others to accept him as eldest. Family expansion through adoption is honored — and the existing children are expected to make room.

BG 16.31

Śunaḥśepha’s father sold Śunaḥśepha to be sacrificed as a man-animal in the yajṣa of King Hariścandra. When Śunaḥśepha was brought into the sacrificial arena, he prayed to the demigods for release and was released by their mercy.

Essence. Śunaḥśepha was once sold by his own father to be sacrificed; only the demigods' mercy preserved him. Some souls' biographies begin with shocking betrayal — and the Lord still places them in noble families.

BG 16.32

Although Śunaḥśepha was born in the Bhārgava dynasty, he was greatly advanced in spiritual life, and therefore the demigods involved in the sacrifice protected him. Consequently he was also celebrated as the descendant of Gādhi named Devarāta.

Essence. Despite his Bhārgava birth, Śunaḥśepha was so spiritually advanced that demigods protected him. Real spiritual advancement attracts the attention and protection of higher beings — even across what we treat as caste.

BG 16.33

When requested by their father to accept Śunaḥśepha as the eldest son, the elder fifty of the Madhucchandās, the sons of Viśvāmitra, did not agree. Therefore Viśvāmitra, being angry, cursed them. “May all of you bad sons become mlecchas,” he said, “being opposed to the principles of Vedic culture.”

Essence. The elder Madhucchandās refuse to accept Śunaḥśepha as eldest, and Viśvāmitra curses them to become mlecchas. Disobeying a sage father, even on matters of social order, can produce drastic loss of cultural standing.

BG 16.34

When the elder Madhucchandās were cursed, the younger fifty, along with Madhucchandā himself, approached their father and agreed to accept his proposal. “Dear father,” they said, “we shall abide by whatever arrangement you like.”

Essence. The younger fifty immediately agree: 'We shall abide by whatever arrangement you like.' Sometimes the second half of a family carries the obedience the first half refused.

BG 16.35

Thus the younger Madhucchandās accepted Śunaḥśepha as their eldest brother and told him, “We shall follow your orders.” Viśvāmitra then said to his obedient sons, “Because you have accepted Śunaḥśepha as your eldest brother, I am very satisfied. By accepting my order, you have made me a father of worthy sons, and therefore I bless all of you to become the fathers of sons also.”

Essence. Pleased, Viśvāmitra blesses the obedient sons that they too shall be fathers of sons. Obedience to one's father is itself a kind of fertility — it earns the right to be a father in turn.

BG 16.36

Viśvāmitra said, “O Kuśikas [descendants of Kauśika], this Devarāta is my son and is one of you. Please obey his orders.” O King Parīkṣit, Viśvāmitra had many other sons, such as Aṣṭaka, Hārīta, Jaya and Kratumān.

Essence. Viśvāmitra orders: 'Devarāta is my son and is one of you — obey his orders.' New members of a community gain real standing only when those above them publicly confer it.

BG 16.37

Viśvāmitra cursed some of his sons and blessed the others, and he also adopted a son. Thus there were varieties in the Kauśika dynasty, but among all the sons, Devarāta was considered the eldest.

Essence. Some sons were cursed, some blessed, one adopted — yet Devarāta is reckoned the eldest. Mixed families with mixed reactions are normal in scripture; what matters is who is finally recognized at the head.
