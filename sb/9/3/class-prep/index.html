BG 3.1

Śrī Śukadeva Gosvāmī continued: O King, Śaryāti, another son of Manu, was a ruler completely aware of Vedic knowledge. He gave instructions about the functions for the second day of the yajṣa to be performed by the descendants of Aṅgirā.

Essence. Śaryāti, fluent in the Vedas, instructs Aṅgirā's descendants on second-day sacrificial duties. Real Vedic kingship is not just power but operative scholarship in the service of yajña.

BG 3.2

Śaryāti had a beautiful lotus-eyed daughter named Sukanyā, with whom he went to the forest to see the āśrama of Cyavana Muni.

Essence. Śaryāti brings his daughter to a sage's āśrama — a king teaches his child by exposure, not lecture. Holy places and holy company shape children more than instruction.

BG 3.3

While that Sukanyā, surrounded by her friends, was collecting various types of fruits from the trees in the forest, she saw within the hole of an earthworm two things glowing like luminaries.

Essence. Sukanyā sees two glowing points inside an anthill — the world is full of hidden presences mistaken for ordinary curiosities. We routinely encounter the sacred without recognizing it.

BG 3.4

As if induced by providence, the girl ignorantly pierced those two glowworms with a thorn, and when they were pierced, blood began to ooze out of them.

Essence. Without malice, in pure ignorance, Sukanyā pierces what turns out to be a great sage's eyes. The unknowing act still produces real consequence; ignorance does not erase impact.

BG 3.5

Thereupon, all the soldiers of Śaryāti were immediately obstructed from passing urine and stool. Upon perceiving this, Śaryāti spoke to his associates in surprise.

Essence. Suddenly every soldier in the camp cannot pass urine or stool — one person's offense paralyzes an entire community. Group consequences quickly trace back to individual lapses.

BG 3.6

How strange it is that one of us has attempted to do something wrong to Cyavana Muni, the son of Bhṛgu. It certainly appears that someone among us has polluted this āśrama.

Essence. Śaryāti immediately suspects an offense to the sage and asks who is responsible. Mature leadership treats community symptoms as moral data, not just logistical problems.

BG 3.7

Being very much afraid, the girl Sukanyā said to her father: I have done something wrong, for I have ignorantly pierced these two luminous substances with a thorn.

Essence. Sukanyā confesses immediately and honestly. The first step toward repair is unhedged confession — without it the whole group remains stuck.

BG 3.8

After hearing this statement by his daughter, King Śaryāti was very much afraid. In various ways, he tried to appease Cyavana Muni, for it was he who sat within the hole of the earthworm.

Essence. On hearing his daughter's confession, Śaryāti rushes to appease the sage. A father takes responsibility for his child's mistake rather than scolding her into helplessness.

BG 3.9

King Śaryāti, being very contemplative and thus understanding Cyavana Muni’s purpose, gave his daughter in charity to the sage. Thus released from danger with great difficulty, he took permission from Cyavana Muni and returned home.

Essence. Reading the sage's mind, Śaryāti gives his daughter in marriage as recompense. Sometimes the price of avoiding a curse is something we never imagined giving — and we must give it.

BG 3.10

Cyavana Muni was very irritable, but since Sukanyā had gotten him as her husband, she dealt with him carefully, according to his mood. Knowing his mind, she performed service to him without being bewildered.

Essence. Sukanyā learns to live with a difficult, irritable elder sage by reading his mood. A wife's victory, the verse suggests, is not equality of friction but skill in serving without bewilderment.

BG 3.11

Thereafter, some time having passed, the Aśvinī-kumāra brothers, the heavenly physicians, happened to come to Cyavana Muni’s āśrama. After offering them respectful obeisances, Cyavana Muni requested them to give him youthful life, for they were able to do so.

Essence. Cyavana asks the Aśvinī-kumāras for youth — even great sages may legitimately use available means to restore the body. Spiritual greatness does not require despising medicine.

BG 3.12

Cyavana Muni said: Although you are ineligible to drink soma-rasa in sacrifices, I promise to give you a full pot of it. Kindly arrange beauty and youth for me, because they are attractive to young women.

Essence. Cyavana offers soma — something the Aśvins were forbidden from — as a fair exchange for their help. He uses his spiritual capital to elevate them; influence is meant to be shared.

BG 3.13

The great physicians, the Aśvinī-kumāras, very gladly accepted Cyavana Muni’s proposal. Thus they told the brāhmaṇa, “Just dive into this lake of successful life.” [One who bathes in this lake has his desires fulfilled.]

Essence. The Aśvins tell Cyavana to dive into a lake that fulfills desires. Sometimes the answer is not negotiation but full immersion into a hidden facility we did not even know existed.

BG 3.14

After saying this, the Aśvinī-kumāras caught hold of Cyavana Muni, who was an old, diseased invalid with loose skin, white hair, and veins visible all over his body, and all three of them entered the lake.

Essence. The Aśvins lovingly carry the old, decrepit sage into the lake. Sometimes others must hold our hand to help us cross the threshold we could not cross alone.

BG 3.15

Thereafter, three men with very beautiful bodily features emerged from the lake. They were nicely dressed and decorated with earrings and garlands of lotuses. All of them were of the same standard of beauty.

Essence. Three equally beautiful men emerge — the Aśvins playfully blur identity to test Sukanyā. Transformation can look so complete that the new and the same become indistinguishable.

BG 3.16

The chaste and very beautiful Sukanyā could not distinguish her husband from the two Aśvinī-kumāras, for they were equally beautiful. Not understanding who her real husband was, she took shelter of the Aśvinī-kumāras.

Essence. Sukanyā refuses to guess and takes shelter of the Aśvins, asking them to point out her husband. When you cannot tell, ask — chastity is also intellectual honesty under confusion.

BG 3.17

The Aśvinī-kumāras were very pleased to see Sukanyā’s chastity and faithfulness. Thus they showed her Cyavana Muni, her husband, and after taking permission from him, they returned to the heavenly planets in their plane.

Essence. Pleased by Sukanyā's chastity, the Aśvins identify Cyavana and depart. Pure-hearted fidelity earns help from places we did not request, then sends those helpers away without claim.

BG 3.18

Thereafter, King Śaryāti, desiring to perform a sacrifice, went to the residence of Cyavana Muni. There he saw by the side of his daughter a very beautiful young man, as bright as the sun.

Essence. Śaryāti returns and is shocked to see a glowing young man next to his daughter. Appearances at first glance can lead even wise observers to false conclusions.

BG 3.19

After receiving obeisances from his daughter, the King, instead of offering blessings to her, appeared very displeased and spoke as follows.

Essence. Without offering his usual blessing, Śaryāti speaks in displeasure. Even great parents can let outward perception override their daughter's character; we must catch ourselves before we strike.

BG 3.20

O unchaste girl, what is this that you have desired to do? You have cheated the most respectable husband, who is honored by everyone, for I see that because he was old, diseased and therefore unattractive, you have left his company to accept as your husband this young man, who appears to be a beggar from the street.

Essence. Śaryāti rebukes his daughter as unchaste for trading an aged husband for a young man. A father's anxiety about appearances can pour out as accusation before facts emerge.

BG 3.21

O my daughter, who were born in a respectable family, how have you degraded your consciousness in this way? How is it that you are shamelessly maintaining a paramour? You will thus degrade the dynasties of both your father and your husband to hellish life.

Essence. Śaryāti warns that infidelity drags down both ancestral and marital lines. Personal choices in chastity ripple far beyond the individual; lineages bear the weight together.

BG 3.22

Sukanyā, however, being very proud of her chastity, smiled upon hearing the rebukes of her father. She smilingly told him, “My dear father, this young man by my side is your actual son-in-law, the great sage Cyavana, who was born in the family of Bhṛgu.”

Essence. Sukanyā smiles, secure in her chastity, and tells her father the truth — this young man is Cyavana. Inner integrity makes outer accusation bounce harmlessly off the heart.

BG 3.23

Thus Sukanyā explained how her husband had received the beautiful body of a young man. When the King heard this he was very surprised, and in great pleasure he embraced his beloved daughter.

Essence. Once the truth is told, Śaryāti's displeasure dissolves into joyful embrace. Honest explanation, received humbly, can swing a parent from rebuke to embrace within minutes.

BG 3.24

Cyavana Muni, by his own prowess, enabled King Śaryāti to perform the soma-yajṣa. The muni offered a full pot of soma-rasa to the Aśvinī-kumāras, although they were unfit to drink it.

Essence. Cyavana arranges that the Aśvins receive soma in Śaryāti's yajña — a sage repays kindness with elevation. Real gratitude lifts the helper's status, not just thanks them.

BG 3.25

King Indra, being perturbed and angry, wanted to kill Cyavana Muni, and therefore he impetuously took up his thunderbolt. But Cyavana Muni, by his powers, paralyzed Indra’s arm that held the thunderbolt.

Essence. Indra, furious that the Aśvins are being included, lifts his thunderbolt — but Cyavana paralyzes his arm. Higher principle, when defended by a saint, can freeze even a king of heaven mid-strike.

BG 3.26

Although the Aśvinī-kumāras were only physicians and were therefore excluded from drinking soma-rasa in sacrifices, the demigods agreed to allow them henceforward to drink it.

Essence. The demigods relent and welcome the Aśvins to share soma henceforward. Even heavenly customs evolve when a saintly voice persists in opening doors.

BG 3.27

King Śaryāti begot three sons, named Uttānabarhi, Ānarta and Bhūriṣeṇa. From Ānarta came a son named Revata.

Essence. Three sons of Śaryāti continue the line. Even a chapter's marvelous events flow into the ordinariness of names and descendants — wonder lives inside continuity.

BG 3.28

O Mahārāja Parīkṣit, subduer of enemies, this Revata constructed a kingdom known as Kuśasthalī in the depths of the ocean. There he lived and ruled such tracts of land as Ānarta, etc. He had one hundred very nice sons, of whom the eldest was Kakudmī.

Essence. Revata builds an underwater kingdom, Kuśasthalī, and rules far-flung lands with a hundred sons. Visionary kingship sees territory others cannot — even beneath the ocean.

BG 3.29

Taking his own daughter, Revatī, Kakudmī went to Lord Brahmā in Brahmaloka, which is transcendental to the three modes of material nature, and inquired about a husband for her.

Essence. Kakudmī takes his daughter Revatī to Brahmaloka to seek a husband. Wise parents consult the highest available authority for their child's lifelong partnership.

BG 3.30

When Kakudmī arrived there, Lord Brahmā was engaged in hearing musical performances by the Gandharvas and had not a moment to talk with him. Therefore Kakudmī waited, and at the end of the musical performances he offered his obeisances to Lord Brahmā and thus submitted his long-standing desire.

Essence. Brahmā is engrossed in gandharva music and Kakudmī waits patiently until he is free. Even the highest authority is sometimes busy; reverent patience is the only proper protocol.

BG 3.31

After hearing his words, Lord Brahmā, who is most powerful, laughed loudly and said to Kakudmī: O King, all those whom you may have decided within the core of your heart to accept as your son-in-law have passed away in the course of time.

Essence. Brahmā laughs and tells Kakudmī that all his planned son-in-laws have died long ago. Time invisibly devours the partners and futures we were so carefully selecting.

BG 3.32

Twenty-seven catur-yugas have already passed. Those upon whom you may have decided are now gone, and so are their sons, grandsons and other descendants. You cannot even hear about their names.

Essence. Twenty-seven catur-yugas have passed during the short audience — even the memory of those candidates has vanished. Earth-time and Brahmā-time differ so radically that we cannot grasp how brief we are.

BG 3.33

O King, leave here and offer your daughter to Lord Baladeva, who is still present. He is most powerful. Indeed, He is the Supreme Personality of Godhead, whose plenary portion is Lord Viṣṇu. Your daughter is fit to be given to Him in charity.

Essence. Brahmā directs Kakudmī to give Revatī to Baladeva, the Supreme Personality of Godhead. When every planned option has expired, the Lord Himself is the timeless match available now.

BG 3.34

Lord Baladeva is the Supreme Personality of Godhead. One who hears and chants about Him is purified. Because He is always the well-wisher of all living entities, He has descended with all His paraphernalia to purify the entire world and lessen its burden.

Essence. Baladeva has descended with all His paraphernalia to purify the earth and lighten its burden. Hearing and chanting about Him is itself the purification.

BG 3.35

Having received this order from Lord Brahmā, Kakudmī offered obeisances unto him and returned to his own residence. He then saw that his residence was vacant, having been abandoned by his brothers and other relatives, who were living in all directions because of fear of such higher living beings as the Yakṣas.

Essence. Kakudmī returns home and finds it long abandoned by relatives fleeing Yakṣas. The world he left no longer exists; while we are gone even a short while, life reshuffles.

BG 3.36

Thereafter, the King gave his most beautiful daughter in charity to the supremely powerful Baladeva and then retired from worldly life and went to Badarikāśrama to please Nara-Nārāyaṇa.

Essence. Kakudmī gives Revatī to Baladeva, then retires to Badarikāśrama to worship Nara-Nārāyaṇa. After arranging the most important duty, he turns directly toward the Lord, his real destination.
