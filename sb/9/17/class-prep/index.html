BG 17.1-3

Śukadeva Gosvāmī said: From Purūravā came a son named Āyu, whose very powerful sons were Nahuṣa, Kṣatravṛddha, Rajī, Rābha and Anenā. O Mahārāja Parīkṣit, now hear about the dynasty of Kṣatravṛddha. Kṣatravṛddha’s son was Suhotra, who had three sons, named Kāśya, Kuśa and Gṛtsamada. From Gṛtsamada came Śunaka, and from him came Śaunaka, the great saint, the best of those conversant with the Ṛg Veda.

Essence. From Āyu's sons branch many dynasties, and from Gṛtsamada eventually comes Śaunaka — best of those conversant with the Ṛg Veda. Long dynastic lists eventually flower into specific spiritual giants who actually deliver the Veda to others.

BG 17.4

The son of Kāśya was Kāśi, and his son was Rāṣṭra, the father of Dīrghatama. Dīrghatama had a son named Dhanvantari, who was the inaugurator of the medical science and an incarnation of Lord Vāsudeva, the enjoyer of the results of sacrifices. One who remembers the name of Dhanvantari can be released from all disease.

Essence. Dhanvantari, inaugurator of medical science, is an incarnation of Vāsudeva — and remembering his name itself releases one from disease. Medicine, properly conceived, is a divine descent; the holy name remains the deepest remedy.

BG 17.5

The son of Dhanvantari was Ketumān, and his son was Bhīmaratha. The son of Bhīmaratha was Divodāsa, and the son of Divodāsa was Dyumān, also known as Pratardana.

Essence. Ketumān, Bhīmaratha, Divodāsa, Dyumān — even the dynasty of the healing avatāra continues by ordinary means. Divine origin does not exempt a line from ordinary work of generation, governance, and care.

BG 17.6

Dyumān was also known as Śatrujit, Vatsa, Ṛtadhvaja and Kuvalayāśva. From him were born Alarka and other sons.

Essence. One king carries many names — Dyumān, Śatrujit, Vatsa, Ṛtadhvaja, Kuvalayāśva. The world remembers a single soul under many labels; we should not be too attached to which of our names is used.

BG 17.7

Alarka, the son of Dyumān, reigned over the earth for sixty-six thousand years, my dear King Parīkṣit. No one other than him has reigned over the earth for so long as a young man.

Essence. Alarka reigns sixty-six thousand years as a young man — no other has ruled so long in youthful vigor. Some lives are kept astonishingly long for purposes far larger than personal fulfillment.

BG 17.8

From Alarka came a son named Santati, and his son was Sunītha. The son of Sunītha was Niketana, the son of Niketana was Dharmaketu, and the son of Dharmaketu was Satyaketu.

Essence. Santati, Sunītha, Niketana, Dharmaketu, Satyaketu — names meaning continuity, good conduct, abode, banner of dharma, banner of truth. A dharmic family naturally names its children for the qualities it wishes to embody.

BG 17.9

O King Parīkṣit, from Satyaketu came a son named Dhṛṣṭaketu, and from Dhṛṣṭaketu came Sukumāra, the emperor of the entire world. From Sukumāra came a son named Vītihotra; from Vītihotra, Bharga; and from Bharga, Bhārgabhūmi.

Essence. Dhṛṣṭaketu, Sukumāra, Vītihotra, Bharga, Bhārgabhūmi — the line continues, sometimes producing emperors, sometimes simple bearers of the name. Every life moves the family one more rung forward, even if quietly.

BG 17.10

O Mahārāja Parīkṣit, all of these kings were descendants of Kāśi, and they could also be called descendants of Kṣatravṛddha. The son of Rābha was Rabhasa, from Rabhasa came Gambhīra, and from Gambhīra came a son named Akriya.

Essence. All these kings are descendants of Kāśi and Kṣatravṛddha. Multiple identities — Kāśya and Kṣatravṛddha simultaneously — coexist in the same line. We should not be surprised when a person rightly answers to several lineages.

BG 17.11

The son of Akriya was known as Brahmavit, O King. Now hear about the descendants of Anenā. From Anenā came a son named Śuddha, and his son was Śuci. The son of Śuci was Dharmasārathi, also called Citrakṛt.

Essence. Brahmavit ('knower of Brahman') appears in a list of mere names. Sometimes a single grand title is dropped into the middle of a roll, easy to miss — even brief mention of true brahma-jṣāna is itself a benediction in the text.

BG 17.12

From Citrakṛt was born a son named Śāntaraja, a self-realized soul who performed all kinds of Vedic ritualistic ceremonies and therefore did not beget any progeny. The sons of Rajī were five hundred, all very powerful.

Essence. Śāntaraja, a self-realized soul, performs Vedic ceremonies but begets no progeny. Not every life is meant to produce children; the realized soul's offspring may be his own liberation.

BG 17.13

On the request of the demigods, Rajī killed the demons and thus returned the kingdom of heaven to Lord Indra. But Indra, fearing such demons as Prahlāda, returned the kingdom of heaven to Rajī and surrendered himself at Rajī’s lotus feet.

Essence. Rajī defeats the demons and Indra surrenders the heavenly kingdom out of fear. Sometimes a 'higher' authority hands its position to a more competent lower one — humility in office is preferable to pretense.

BG 17.14

Upon Rajī’s death, Indra begged Rajī’s sons for the return of the heavenly planet. They did not return it, however, although they agreed to return Indra’s shares in ritualistic ceremonies.

Essence. After Rajī dies, his sons refuse to return heaven to Indra. Inheriting a position is different from earning it — and heirs often treat what was given as if it had always been theirs.

BG 17.15

Thereafter, Bṛhaspati, the spiritual master of the demigods, offered oblations in the fire so that the sons of Rajī would fall from moral principles. When they fell, Lord Indra killed them easily because of their degradation. Not a single one of them remained alive.

Essence. Bṛhaspati's oblations cause Rajī's sons to fall from morality, and Indra easily kills them. Once we have fallen from principle, even a previously inferior opponent can dispatch us — moral collapse is the precondition of military defeat.

BG 17.16

From Kuśa, the grandson of Kṣatravṛddha, was born a son named Prati. The son of Prati was Saṣjaya, and the son of Saṣjaya was Jaya. From Jaya, Kṛta was born, and from Kṛta, King Haryabala.

Essence. Prati, Saṣjaya, Jaya, Kṛta, Haryabala — the line continues through names meaning resistance, victory, completion. Even names of triumph end up as one verse in a list — triumph itself is no escape from time.

BG 17.17

From Haryabala came a son named Sahadeva, and from Sahadeva came Hīna. The son of Hīna was Jayasena, and the son of Jayasena was Saṅkṛti. The son of Saṅkṛti was the powerful and expert fighter named Jaya. These kings were the members of the Kṣatravṛddha dynasty. Now let me describe to you the dynasty of Nahuṣa.

Essence. Sahadeva, Hīna, Jayasena, Saṅkṛti — and Jaya, the powerful and expert fighter, closes the Kṣatravṛddha branch. The chapter ends with one more named warrior before transitioning to the next dynasty.
