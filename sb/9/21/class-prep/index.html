BG 21.1

Śukadeva Gosvāmī said: Because Bharadvāja was delivered by the Marut demigods, he was known as Vitatha. The son of Vitatha was Manyu, and from Manyu came five sons — Bṛhatkṣatra, Jaya, Mahāvīrya, Nara and Garga. Of these five, the one known as Nara had a son named Saṅkṛti.

Essence. Bharadvāja becomes Vitatha (delivered) and produces Manyu and five grandsons. Children rescued from rejection often become founders of multiple branches — the rescue itself is a multiplier.

BG 21.2

O Mahārāja Parīkṣit, descendant of Pāṇḍu, Saṅkṛti had two sons, named Guru and Rantideva. Rantideva is famous in both this world and the next, for he is glorified not only in human society but also in the society of the demigods.

Essence. Rantideva is famous in human and divine society alike. Some lives, lived with sufficient devotional intensity, end up being remembered in multiple worlds at once.

BG 21.3-5

Rantideva never endeavored to earn anything. He would enjoy whatever he got by the arrangement of providence, but when guests came he would give them everything. Thus he underwent considerable suffering, along with the members of his family. Indeed, he and his family members shivered for want of food and water, yet Rantideva always remained sober. Once, after fasting for forty-eight days, in the morning Rantideva received some water and some foodstuffs made with milk and ghee, but when he and his family were about to eat, a brāhmaṇa guest arrived.

Essence. Rantideva never strives to earn anything; he enjoys what providence sends and gives it away to guests, even while his family shivers. After forty-eight days of fast, food arrives — and the test begins.

BG 21.6

Because Rantideva perceived the presence of the Supreme Godhead everywhere, and in every living entity, he received the guest with faith and respect and gave him a share of the food. The brāhmaṇa guest ate his share and then went away.

Essence. Seeing the Lord in every being, Rantideva feeds a brāhmaṇa guest his share. Equal vision does not flatten distinctions — it simply ensures hospitality to whomever the Lord sends.

BG 21.7

Thereafter, having divided the remaining food with his relatives, Rantideva was just about to eat his own share when a śūdra guest arrived. Seeing the śūdra in relationship with the Supreme Personality of Godhead, King Rantideva gave him also a share of the food.

Essence. A śūdra guest arrives and receives the next share. Sama-darśinaḥ — equal vision — does not skip the lower in giving; it actively includes them.

BG 21.8

When the śūdra went away, another guest arrived, surrounded by dogs, and said, “O King, I and my company of dogs are very hungry. Please give us something to eat.”

Essence. Then a guest arrives surrounded by hungry dogs and asks for food. Even animals come to the door of a true devotee — and they too are guests who must be fed.

BG 21.9

With great respect, King Rantideva offered the balance of the food to the dogs and the master of the dogs, who had come as guests. The King offered them all respects and obeisances.

Essence. Rantideva gives the remaining food to the dogs and their master, offering all respects. Hospitality is not measured by social calibration — even dogs receive obeisances when the heart sees the Lord in everything.

BG 21.10

Thereafter, only the drinking water remained, and there was only enough to satisfy one person, but when the King was just about to drink it, a caṇḍāla appeared and said, “O King, although I am lowborn, kindly give me some drinking water.”

Essence. Only one cup of drinking water remains, enough for one person — and a caṇḍāla appears asking for it. The test escalates precisely as the resource shrinks; true devotion is measured at the very last drop.

BG 21.11

Aggrieved at hearing the pitiable words of the poor fatigued caṇḍāla, Mahārāja Rantideva spoke the following nectarean words.

Essence. Rantideva's words to the caṇḍāla are themselves like nectar — and would save anyone who heard them. The very utterance of a saint, in the right moment, is salvific.

BG 21.12

I do not pray to the Supreme Personality of Godhead for the eight perfections of mystic yoga, nor for salvation from repeated birth and death. I want only to stay among all the living entities and suffer all distresses on their behalf, so that they may be freed from suffering.

Essence. Rantideva does not ask for mystic perfections or even for liberation — he asks only to remain among living beings and suffer their distress so they may be freed. The highest devotional posture is voluntary suffering on behalf of others.

BG 21.13

By offering my water to maintain the life of this poor caṇḍāla, who is struggling to live, I have been freed from all hunger, thirst, fatigue, trembling of the body, moroseness, distress, lamentation and illusion.

Essence. Giving water to the caṇḍāla, Rantideva says he is freed from hunger, thirst, fatigue, and illusion. The act of self-emptying generosity dissolves the very deprivations one was suffering.

BG 21.14

Having spoken thus, King Rantideva, although on the verge of death because of thirst, gave his own portion of water to the caṇḍāla without hesitation, for the King was naturally very kind and sober.

Essence. Although on the verge of death, Rantideva hands over his water without hesitation. The moment of final desperation is also the moment of greatest opportunity for devotional history.

BG 21.15

Demigods like Lord Brahmā and Lord Śiva, who can satisfy all materially ambitious men by giving them the rewards they desire, then manifested their own identities before King Rantideva, for it was they who had presented themselves as the brāhmaṇa, śūdra, caṇḍāla and so on.

Essence. Brahmā, Śiva, and the demigods reveal that they were the guests testing him. The Lord and His associates often arrive disguised as beggars; routine hospitality is, in fact, a regular spiritual examination.

BG 21.16

King Rantideva had no ambition to enjoy material benefits from the demigods. He offered them obeisances, but because he was factually attached to Lord Viṣṇu, Vāsudeva, the Supreme Personality of Godhead, he fixed his mind at Lord Viṣṇu’s lotus feet.

Essence. Rantideva offers obeisances but fixes his mind on Viṣṇu's lotus feet — he wants nothing from the demigods. Pure devotion politely receives offers of benediction but never wavers from the Supreme.

BG 21.17

O Mahārāja Parīkṣit, because King Rantideva was a pure devotee, always Kṛṣṇa conscious and free from all material desires, the Lord’s illusory energy, māyā, could not exhibit herself before him. On the contrary, for him māyā entirely vanished, exactly like a dream.

Essence. Because Rantideva is a pure devotee, māyā cannot exhibit herself before him — she vanishes like a dream. Bhakti, fully developed, simply makes māyā irrelevant; no debate is required.

BG 21.18

All those who followed the principles of King Rantideva were totally favored by his mercy and became pure devotees, attached to the Supreme Personality of Godhead, Nārāyaṇa. Thus they all became the best of yogīs.

Essence. All who followed Rantideva became pure devotees, attached to Nārāyaṇa, and were called the best of yogīs. Following a true devotee — not just admiring one — is itself the yogic shortcut.

BG 21.19-20

From Garga came a son named Śini, and his son was Gārgya. Although Gārgya was a kṣatriya, there came from him a generation of brahmaṇas. From Mahāvīrya came a son named Duritakṣaya, whose sons were Trayyāruṇi, Kavi and Puṣkarāruṇi. Although these sons of Duritakṣaya took birth in a dynasty of kṣatriyas, they too attained the position of brāhmaṇas. Bṛhatkṣatra had a son named Hastī, who established the city of Hastināpura [now New Delhi].

Essence. From Garga came Śini and Gārgya, and Gārgya — though a kṣatriya — produced a brāhmaṇa generation. The category of brāhmaṇa, even structurally, has always emerged from qualification and not only birth.

BG 21.21

From King Hastī came three sons, named Ajamīḍha, Dvimīḍha and Purumīḍha. The descendants of Ajamīḍha, headed by Priyamedha, all achieved the position of brāhmaṇas.

Essence. From Hastī come Ajamīḍha, Dvimīḍha, Purumīḍha — and Ajamīḍha's descendants headed by Priyamedha all achieved brāhmaṇa standing. Single generations can reshape the entire functional caste of a line.

BG 21.22

From Ajamīḍha came a son named Bṛhadiṣu, from Bṛhadiṣu came a son named Bṛhaddhanu, from Bṛhaddhanu a son named Bṛhatkāya, and from Bṛhatkāya a son named Jayadratha.

Essence. Bṛhadiṣu, Bṛhaddhanu, Bṛhatkāya, Jayadratha — names with 'great-arrow,' 'great-bow,' 'great-body.' Lineages of warriors carry weapon-meanings forward generation after generation.

BG 21.23

The son of Jayadratha was Viśada, and his son was Syenajit. The sons of Syenajit were Rucirāśva, Dṛḍhahanu, Kāśya and Vatsa.

Essence. Viśada, Syenajit, Rucirāśva, Dṛḍhahanu — even the names quietly cycle through qualities. Most spiritual progress happens at the level of attention to qualities, not at the level of dramatic events.

BG 21.24

The son of Rucirāśva was Pāra, and the sons of Pāra were Pṛthusena and Nīpa. Nīpa had one hundred sons.

Essence. Nīpa has one hundred sons. Some lines explode in fertility; others taper. The Lord arranges different kinds of generativity in different homes — and our 'normal' is not the only legitimate kind.

BG 21.25

King Nīpa begot a son named Brahmadatta through the womb of his wife, Kṛtvī, who was the daughter of Śuka. And Brahmadatta, who was a great yogī, begot a son named Viṣvaksena through the womb of his wife, Sarasvatī.

Essence. Brahmadatta, born of Kṛtvī (daughter of one Śuka, not the Śukadeva of Bhāgavatam), is a great yogī who fathers Viṣvaksena. Distinguishing similar names in scripture protects us from confused devotional history.

BG 21.26

Following the instructions of the great sage Jaigīṣavya, Viṣvaksena compiled an elaborate description of the mystic yoga system. From Viṣvaksena, Udaksena was born, and from Udaksena, Bhallāṭa. All these sons are known as descendants of Bṛhadiṣu.

Essence. Following Jaigīṣavya, Viṣvaksena compiles an elaborate yoga manual. Sometimes our greatest contribution is not personal experience but the careful compilation of a tradition for those who come after.

BG 21.27

The son of Dvimīḍha was Yavīnara, whose son was Kṛtimān. The son of Kṛtimān was well known as Satyadhṛti. From Satyadhṛti came a son named Dṛḍhanemi, who became the father of Supārśva.

Essence. Yavīnara, Kṛtimān, Satyadhṛti, Dṛḍhanemi, Supārśva — yet another stretch of name-virtues. The Bhāgavatam is patient with lists; we should be patient in attention.

BG 21.28-29

From Supārśva came a son named Sumati, from Sumati came Sannatimān, and from Sannatimān came Kṛtī, who achieved mystic power from Brahmā and taught six saṁhitās of the Prācyasāma verses of the Sāma Veda. The son of Kṛtī was Nīpa; the son of Nīpa, Udgrāyudha; the son of Udgrāyudha, Kṣemya; the son of Kṣemya, Suvīra; and the son of Suvīra, Ripuṣjaya.

Essence. Kṛtī receives mystic power from Brahmā and teaches six saṁhitās of Sāma Veda. From there: Nīpa, Udgrāyudha, Kṣemya, Suvīra, Ripuṣjaya. Mystical attainment in one generation can seed teaching for generations beyond.

BG 21.30

From Ripuṣjaya came a son named Bahuratha. Purumīḍha was sonless. Ajamīḍha had a son named Nīla by his wife known as Nalinī, and the son of Nīla was Śānti.

Essence. Purumīḍha was sonless; Ajamīḍha has Nīla by Nalinī, then Śānti. Not every line continues — and the Bhāgavatam lets us hold both fertility and barrenness as part of one larger arrangement.

BG 21.31-33

The son of Śānti was Suśānti, the son of Suśānti was Puruja, and the son of Puruja was Arka. From Arka came Bharmyāśva, and from Bharmyāśva came five sons — Mudgala, Yavīnara, Bṛhadviśva, Kāmpilla and Saṣjaya. Bharmyāśva prayed to his sons, “O my sons, please take charge of my five states, for you are quite competent to do so.” Thus his five sons were known as the Paṣcālas. From Mudgala came a dynasty of brāhmaṇas known as Maudgalya.

Essence. Bharmyāśva entrusts his five sons with five states, and they become the Paṣcālas. From Mudgala comes the brāhmaṇa Maudgalya line. The Bhāgavatam cheerfully tracks how lines branch and rename — identity is layered.

BG 21.34

Mudgala, the son of Bharmyāśva, had twin children, one male and the other female. The male child was named Divodāsa, and the female child was named Ahalyā. From the womb of Ahalyā by the semen of her husband, Gautama, came a son named Śatānanda.

Essence. Mudgala has twins — Divodāsa and Ahalyā, the latter of whom marries Gautama and bears Śatānanda. Famous figures are often quietly seeded in dynastic lists before their dramatic stories.

BG 21.35

The son of Śatānanda was Satyadhṛti, who was expert in archery, and the son of Satyadhṛti was Śaradvān. When Śaradvān met Urvaśī, he discharged semen, which fell on a clump of śara grass. From this semen were born two all-auspicious babies, one male and the other female.

Essence. Śaradvān, on seeing Urvaśī, accidentally discharges semen onto śara grass and twin children emerge. Sense lapse can occur even to skilled archers and still produce something the Lord uses — but the lapse remains.

BG 21.36

While Mahārāja Śāntanu was on a hunting excursion, he saw the male and female children lying in the forest, and out of compassion he took them home. Consequently, the male child was known as Kṛpa, and the female child was named Kṛpī. Kṛpī later became the wife of Droṇācārya.

Essence. Śāntanu, hunting, compassionately picks up the abandoned twins — Kṛpa and Kṛpī, whose names mean 'mercy.' Even children of strange origin become bearers of names rooted in compassion when received by the merciful.
