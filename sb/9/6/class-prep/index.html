Chapter 6 · Class prep

BG 6.1

Śukadeva Gosvāmī said: O Mahārāja Parīkṣit, Ambarīṣa had three sons, named Virūpa, Ketumān and Śambhu. From Virūpa came a son named Pṛṣadaśva, and from Pṛṣadaśva came a son named Rathītara.

Essence. The Bhāgavatam preserves dynastic memory carefully. Remembering where we come from — biologically and spiritually — situates us within a larger story than our individual life.

BG 6.2

Rathītara had no sons, and therefore he requested the great sage Aṅgirā to beget sons for him. Because of this request, Aṅgirā begot sons in the womb of Rathītara’s wife. All these sons were born with brahminical prowess.

Essence. When ordinary efforts fail, sages and householders alike sought help from spiritually qualified persons. Recognizing where you cannot do something yourself, and asking the right person, is itself a discipline.

BG 6.3

Having been born from the womb of Rathītara’s wife, all these sons were known as the dynasty of Rathītara, but because they were born from the semen of Aṅgirā, they were also known as the dynasty of Aṅgirā. Among all the progeny of Rathītara, these sons were the most prominent because, owing to their birth, they were considered brāhmaṇas.

Essence. Identity is shaped by both origin and influence. We are products of our nominal family and of those whose 'seed' of teaching truly forms us — and both legacies travel with us.

BG 6.4

The son of Manu was Ikṣvāku. When Manu was sneezing, Ikṣvāku was born from Manu’s nostrils. King Ikṣvāku had one hundred sons, of whom Vikukṣi, Nimi and Daṇḍakā were the most prominent.

Essence. Even a sneeze, under the right arrangement, produces a king. The Lord's plans do not depend on dignified mechanics — we should not measure the importance of an event by its outward appearance.

BG 6.5

Of the one hundred sons, twenty-five became kings in the western side of Āryāvarta, a place between the Himālaya and Vindhya mountains. Another twenty-five sons became kings in the east of Āryāvarta, and the three principal sons became kings in the middle. The other sons became kings in various other places.

Essence. A wise ruler distributes responsibility rather than centralizing it. Spreading leadership across many shoulders — east, west, center — is a sign of mature stewardship, not loss of control.

BG 6.6

During the months of January, February and March, oblations offered to the forefathers are called aṣṭakā-śrāddha. The śrāddha ceremony is held during the dark fortnight of the month. When Mahārāja Ikṣvāku was performing his oblations in this ceremony, he ordered his son Vikukṣi to go immediately to the forest to bring some pure flesh.

Essence. Sacred ceremonies have their seasons, ingredients, and rules. The śāstra ties spiritual life to the calendar to keep us from drifting into purely private, mood-driven religiosity.

BG 6.7

Thereafter, Ikṣvāku’s son Vikukṣi went to the forest and killed many animals suitable for being offered as oblations. But when fatigued and hungry he became forgetful and ate a rabbit he had killed.

Essence. Vikukṣi was sent to procure offerings, but hunger and fatigue made him forgetful and he ate from what was meant for the Lord. Tiredness is a known doorway through which we steal from God what was supposed to be offered.

BG 6.8

Vikukṣi offered the remnants of the flesh to King Ikṣvāku, who gave it to Vasiṣṭha for purification. But Vasiṣṭha could immediately understand that part of the flesh had already been taken by Vikukṣi, and therefore he said that it was unfit to be used in the śrāddha ceremony.

Essence. Once we have personally tasted what was meant to be offered, the whole offering is spoiled. In Deity worship and in life, the order matters: first the Lord, then everyone else, then us.

BG 6.9

When King Ikṣvāku, thus informed by Vasiṣṭha, understood what his son Vikukṣi had done, he was extremely angry. Thus he ordered Vikukṣi to leave the country because Vikukṣi had violated the regulative principles.

Essence. A father willing to exile his own son for a breach of dharma shows what serious householders look like. Loving correction, even when costly, is part of guiding those entrusted to us.

BG 6.10

Having been instructed by the great and learned brāhmaṇa Vasiṣṭha, who discoursed about the Absolute Truth, Mahārāja Ikṣvāku became renounced. By following the principles for a yogī, he certainly achieved the supreme perfection after giving up his material body.

Essence. When the teaching is clear, a wise leader does not cling to office — he renounces, follows the path, and attains perfection. Power held too long becomes the very thing that blocks the perfection it was meant to support.

BG 6.11

After his father’s disappearance, Vikukṣi returned to the country and thus became the king, ruling the planet earth and performing various sacrifices to satisfy the Supreme Personality of Godhead. Vikukṣi later became celebrated as Śaśāda.

Essence. After being purified, Vikukṣi returns, rules, and uses kingship to satisfy the Lord. A mistake corrected can become the foundation of a more sober, devotional life.

BG 6.12

The son of Śaśāda was Puraṣjaya, who is also known as Indravāha and sometimes as Kakutstha. Please hear from me how he received different names for different activities.

Essence. Names accumulate around our deeds. Each significant action gives us another title — and at the end of life, that constellation of names is the truest description of who we became.

BG 6.13

Formerly, there was a devastating war between the demigods and the demons. The demigods, having been defeated, accepted Puraṣjaya as their assistant and then conquered the demons. Therefore this hero is known as Puraṣjaya, “he who conquered the residence of the demons.”

Essence. Even the demigods sometimes need human help to win their battles. Cooperation across very different levels of being is built into the Lord's arrangement — no one is too small to be needed.

BG 6.14

Puraṣjaya agreed to kill all the demons, on the condition that Indra would be his carrier. Because of pride, Indra could not accept this proposal, but later, by the order of the Supreme Lord, Viṣṇu, Indra did accept it and became a great bull carrier for Puraṣjaya.

Essence. Indra's pride first refused service, but the Lord's direct order overcame it. When even devas need divine instruction to set aside pride and serve, we ordinary souls should not expect humility to come without similar reminders.

BG 6.15-16

Well protected by armor and desiring to fight, Puraṣjaya took up a transcendental bow and very sharp arrows, and, while being highly praised by the demigods, he got up on the back of the bull [Indra] and sat on its hump. Thus he is known as Kakutstha. Being empowered by Lord Viṣṇu, who is the Supersoul and the Supreme Person, Puraṣjaya sat on the great bull and is therefore known as Indravāha. Surrounded by the demigods, he attacked the residence of the demons in the west.

Essence. Empowerment for great deeds is not personal achievement but the Lord's working through us. When victory comes, the honest devotee sees the Supersoul as the one who really sat atop the situation.

BG 6.17

There was a fierce battle between the demons and Puraṣjaya. Indeed, it was so fierce that when one hears about it one’s hairs stand on end. All the demons bold enough to come before Puraṣjaya were immediately sent to the residence of Yamarāja by his arrows.

Essence. Conflict is real, and decisive action against destructive forces is sometimes the dharmic response. Spirituality does not mean a vague avoidance of struggle — it means struggling on the right side.

BG 6.18

To save themselves from the blazing arrows of Indravāha, which resembled the flames of devastation at the end of the millennium, the demons who remained when the rest of their army was killed fled very quickly to their respective homes.

Essence. Once divine arrows are unleashed, opposition simply flees. There comes a point in the Lord's arrangements when the outcome is no longer in doubt — our job is to be on the right side of that outcome.

BG 6.19

After conquering the enemy, the saintly king Puraṣjaya gave everything, including the enemy’s riches and wives, to Indra, who carries a thunderbolt. For this he is celebrated as Puraṣjaya. Thus Puraṣjaya is known by different names because of his different activities.

Essence. Puraṣjaya wins everything and immediately hands it back to Indra. Real magnanimity is in not keeping the spoils — using victory as an opportunity for generosity rather than aggrandizement.

BG 6.20

The son of Puraṣjaya was known as Anenā, Anenā’s son was Pṛthu, and Pṛthu’s son was Viśvagandhi. Viśvagandhi’s son was Candra, and Candra’s son was Yuvanāśva.

Essence. Generation after generation, the Bhāgavatam patiently lists names. Each represents a soul who once felt their life was the center of the universe — a sobering reminder of our place in the long line.

BG 6.21

The son of Yuvanāśva was Śrāvasta, who constructed a township known as Śrāvastī Purī. The son of Śrāvasta was Bṛhadaśva, and his son was Kuvalayāśva. In this way the dynasty increased.

Essence. Cities, dynasties, all are built and one day named in passing. We should build, but not be foolish enough to think our constructions will be the center of someone's attention forever.

BG 6.22

To satisfy the sage Utaṅka, the greatly powerful Kuvalayāśva killed a demon named Dhundhu. He did this with the assistance of his twenty-one thousand sons.

Essence. Kuvalayāśva responds to a sage's distress by mobilizing his entire family in service. Spiritual emergencies are sometimes the occasion for the whole household to act together, not just for individuals to retreat.

BG 6.23-24

O Mahārāja Parīkṣit, for this reason Kuvalayāśva is celebrated as Dhundhumāra [“the killer of Dhundhu”]. All but three of his sons, however, were burned to ashes by the fire emanating from Dhundhu’s mouth. The remaining sons were Dṛḍhāśva, Kapilāśva and Bhadrāśva. From Dṛḍhāśva came a son named Haryaśva, whose son is celebrated as Nikumbha.

Essence. Even in noble missions, casualties occur. We must be sober about the cost of taking on great fights, and grateful when even a remnant survives to carry the line forward.

BG 6.25

The son of Nikumbha was Bahulāśva, the son of Bahulāśva was Kṛśāśva, the son of Kṛśāśva was Senajit, and the son of Senajit was Yuvanāśva. Yuvanāśva had no sons, and thus he retired from family life and went to the forest.

Essence. When childlessness ended Yuvanāśva's household life, he simply went to the forest. Sometimes the absence of an expected blessing is itself a redirection toward a deeper kind of life.

BG 6.26

Although Yuvanāśva went into the forest with his one hundred wives, all of them were very morose. The sages in the forest, however, being very kind to the King, began very carefully and attentively performing an Indra-yajṣa so that the King might have a son.

Essence. Even sages took pity on a childless king and worked for his welfare. Spiritual community is not only for inner journeys; it should also take practical interest in the unanswered prayers of its members.

BG 6.27

Being thirsty one night, the King entered the arena of sacrifice, and when he saw all the brāhmaṇas lying down, he personally drank the sanctified water meant to be drunk by his wife.

Essence. An unsanctified thirst, at the wrong moment, leads the king to drink what was meant for his wife. Small unconscious impulses, met without restraint, can rearrange entire destinies.

BG 6.28

When the brāhmaṇas got up from bed and saw the waterpot empty, they inquired who had done this work of drinking the water meant for begetting a child.

Essence. When the sacred water vanishes, the question is not 'who is to blame' but 'who has done this work.' Spiritual investigation is concerned with reality, not punishment.

BG 6.29

When the brāhmaṇas came to understand that the King, inspired by the supreme controller, had drunk the water, they all exclaimed “Alas! The power of providence is real power. No one can counteract the power of the Supreme.” In this way they offered their respectful obeisances unto the Lord.

Essence. When even sages see that providence has overruled their plan, they bow. Real wisdom is the readiness to take off our hat to the Lord's arrangement when it contradicts our careful design.

BG 6.30

Thereafter, in due course of time, a son with all the good symptoms of a powerful king came forth from the lower right side of King Yuvanāśva’s abdomen.

Essence. A child born from the side of his father is itself a sign that the Lord can use any opening to bring a soul into the world. Birth and circumstances do not limit the Lord's capacity to send great souls when they are needed.

BG 6.31

The baby cried so much for breast milk that all the brāhmaṇas were very unhappy. “Who will take care of this baby?” they said. Then Indra, who was worshiped in that yajṣa, came and solaced the baby. “Do not cry,” Indra said. Then Indra put his index finger in the baby’s mouth and said, “You may drink me.”

Essence. When no human mother is present, even Indra himself becomes a nurse — placing his finger in the baby's mouth. The Lord arranges caretakers we never imagined for those He has chosen for service.

BG 6.32

Because Yuvanāśva, the father of the baby, was blessed by the brāhmaṇas, he did not fall a victim to death. After this incident, he performed severe austerities and achieved perfection in that very spot.

Essence. Blessings from genuinely qualified souls protect a person from premature death and lead them to perfection. Whom we are blessed by — not whom we boast about — quietly shapes our trajectory.

BG 6.33-34

Māndhātā, the son of Yuvanāśva, was the cause of fear for Rāvaṇa and other thieves and rogues who caused anxiety. O King Parīkṣit, because they feared him, the son of Yuvanāśva was known as Trasaddasyu. This name was given by King Indra. By the mercy of the Supreme Personality of Godhead, the son of Yuvanāśva was so powerful that when he became emperor he ruled the entire world, consisting of seven islands, without any second ruler.

Essence. Māndhātā became the very fear of evildoers — wherever he ruled, rogues found no foothold. A devotee's presence is meant to make wrongdoing harder, not just to enrich private spirituality.

BG 6.35-36

The Supreme Personality of Godhead is not different from the auspicious aspects of great sacrifices, such as the ingredients of the sacrifice, the chanting of Vedic hymns, the regulative principles, the performer, the priests, the result of the sacrifice, the arena of sacrifice, and the time of sacrifice. Knowing the principles of self-realization, Māndhātā worshiped that transcendentally situated Supreme Soul, the Supreme Personality of Godhead, Lord Viṣṇu, who comprises all the demigods. He also gave immense charity to the brāhmaṇas, and thus he performed yajṣa to worship the Lord.

Essence. Sacrifice, mantras, regulations, priests, time, and place are not separate from the Lord — they are His features. Worship done knowingly sees the Lord in every component, not only in the deity at the end.

BG 6.37

All places, from where the sun rises on the horizon, shining brilliantly, to where the sun sets, are known as the possession of the celebrated Māndhātā, the son of Yuvanāśva.

Essence. An emperor's claim once stretched from sunrise to sunset. Sit with that for a moment, then notice he is now a sentence in someone else's book. Worldly fame is not a stable home for the self.

BG 6.38

Māndhātā begot three sons in the womb of Bindumatī, the daughter of Śaśabindu. These sons were Purukutsa, Ambarīṣa, and Mucukunda, a great mystic yogī. These three brothers had fifty sisters, who all accepted the great sage Saubhari as their husband.

Essence. From a single noble family come a yogī, an emperor, and fifty daughters who accept one ascetic husband. Within the same household, very different paths can unfold — we should not project a single mold on all our children or peers.

BG 6.39-40

Saubhari Ṛṣi was engaged in austerity, deep in the water of the river Yamunā, when he saw a pair of fish engaged in sexual affairs. Thus he perceived the pleasure of sex life, and induced by this desire he went to King Māndhātā and begged for one of the King’s daughters. In response to this request, the King said, “O brāhmaṇa, any of my daughters may accept any husband according to her personal selection.”

Essence. Even a sage submerged in penance can be undone by the wrong glance. Sense impressions, even from creatures pursuing their nature, can plant desires that uproot years of austerity.

BG 6.41-42

Saubhari Muni thought: I am now feeble because of old age. My hair has become grey, my skin is slack, and my head always trembles. Besides, I am a yogī. Therefore women do not like me. Since the King has thus rejected me, I shall reform my body in such a way as to be desirable even to celestial women, what to speak of the daughters of worldly kings.

Essence. Rejected for his aging body, Saubhari reshapes it through yogic power to win attraction. Material desire will marshal extraordinary effort to overcome obstacles to enjoyment — strength which, redirected, could have crowned his original tapasya.

BG 6.43

Thereafter, when Saubhari Muni became quite a young and beautiful person, the messenger of the palace took him inside the residential quarters of the princesses, which were extremely opulent. All fifty princesses then accepted him as their husband, although he was only one man.

Essence. Mystic power can make one universally attractive, but attractiveness multiplies entanglement. Whatever opens many doors in the material world also generally hands us many keys we did not need.

BG 6.44

Thereafter, the princesses, being attracted by Saubhari Muni, gave up their sisterly relationship and quarreled among themselves, each one of them contending, “This man is just suitable for me, and not for you.” In this way there ensued a great disagreement.

Essence. Once sisters, now rivals — the same man is now an object of competition rather than a person. Material enjoyment converts loving relationships into contests, even within families.

BG 6.45-46

Because Saubhari Muni was expert in chanting mantras perfectly, his severe austerities resulted in an opulent home, with garments, ornaments, properly dressed and decorated maidservants and manservants, and varieties of parks with clear-water lakes and gardens. In the gardens, fragrant with varieties of flowers, birds chirped and bees hummed, surrounded by professional singers. Saubhari Muni’s home was amply provided with valuable beds, seats, ornaments, and arrangements for bathing, and there were varieties of sandalwood creams, flower garlands, and palatable dishes. Thus surrounded by opulent paraphernalia, the muni engaged in family affairs with his numerous wives.

Essence. Mantra power can produce mansions, gardens, music, and luxury, but Saubhari uses it to build a gilded prison. Spiritual potency wasted on sense gratification is one of the saddest sights in the Bhāgavatam.

BG 6.47

Māndhātā, the King of the entire world, consisting of seven islands, was struck with wonder when he saw the household opulence of Saubhari Muni. Thus he gave up his false prestige in his position as emperor of the world.

Essence. The emperor of the world is left awestruck by a hermit's luxury — and quietly demoted in his own self-estimation. Even worldly success has people above us; our 'top' is always someone else's middle.

BG 6.48

In this way, Saubhari Muni enjoyed sense gratification in the material world, but he was not at all satisfied, just as a fire never ceases blazing if constantly supplied with drops of fat.

Essence. Sense gratification is a fire that grows with feeding, never sated. The very engine of modern life — endless economic improvement — is just more drops of fat on this same flame.

BG 6.49

Thereafter, one day while Saubhari Muni, who was expert in chanting mantras, was sitting in a secluded place, he thought to himself about the cause of his falldown, which was simply that he had associated himself with the sexual affairs of the fish.

Essence. Saubhari finally traces his fall to one thing: he associated with the sexual play of fish. Even brief, seemingly trivial association with the wrong activities can be the root of long entanglement.

BG 6.50

Alas! While practicing austerity, even within the depths of the water, and while observing all the rules and regulations practiced by saintly persons, I lost the results of my long austerities simply by association with the sexual affairs of fish. Everyone should observe this falldown and learn from it.

Essence. Long austerities, deep in the water, observing every rule — all lost from a single careless contact. Saubhari makes himself a public lesson so that others might be saved from the same trajectory.

BG 6.51

A person desiring liberation from material bondage must give up the association of persons interested in sex life and should not employ his senses externally [in seeing, hearing, talking, walking and so on]. One should always stay in a secluded place, completely fixing his mind at the lotus feet of the unlimited Personality of Godhead, and if one wants any association at all, he should associate with persons similarly engaged.

Essence. The seeker is told to avoid those obsessed with sex life, restrain the senses, dwell in seclusion, fix the mind on the Lord, and only associate with similarly fixed devotees. The recipe is unfashionable but unchanged.

BG 6.52

In the beginning I was alone and engaged in performing the austerities of mystic yoga, but later, because of the association of fish engaged in sex, I desired to marry. Then I became the husband of fifty wives, and in each of them I begot one hundred sons, and thus my family increased to five thousand members. By the influence of the modes of material nature, I became fallen and thought that I would be happy in material life. Thus there is no end to my material desires for enjoyment, in this life and the next.

Essence. From one fall, fifty wives, five thousand sons, and an endless reach of desire. Saubhari demonstrates how a single misstep can multiply into a whole architecture of attachments.

BG 6.53

In this way he passed his life in household affairs for some time, but then he became detached from material enjoyment. To renounce material association, he accepted the vānaprastha order and went to the forest. His devoted wives followed him, for they had no shelter other than their husband.

Essence. Detachment finally comes; Saubhari takes vānaprastha and his wives, finding no other shelter, follow. Genuine turning toward the Lord eventually pulls along even those tied to us by our former entanglements.

BG 6.54

When Saubhari Muni, who was quite conversant with the self, went to the forest, he performed severe penances. In this way, in the fire at the time of death, he ultimately engaged himself in the service of the Supreme Personality of Godhead.

Essence. If the desire for material enjoyment is truly burned out by the end, even the subtle body dissolves, and the pure soul returns to the Lord. The body's death is only a doorway; what walks through depends on what we have already cultivated.

BG 6.55

O Mahārāja Parīkṣit, by observing their husband progressing in spiritual existence, Saubhari Muni’s wives were also able to enter the spiritual world by his spiritual power, just as the flames of a fire cease when the fire is extinguished.

Essence. Wives 'unqualified' on their own attained the spiritual world through faithful service to a husband who became spiritually advanced. Devoted relationship with a sincere bhakta carries real spiritual power.
