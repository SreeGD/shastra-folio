Chapter 14 · Class prep

BG 14.1

Śrīla Śukadeva Gosvāmī said to Mahārāja Parīkṣit: O King, thus far you have heard the description of the dynasty of the sun-god. Now hear the most glorious and purifying description of the dynasty of the moon-god. This description mentions kings like Aila [Purūravā] of whom it is glorious to hear.

Essence. Śukadeva pivots from the Sūrya dynasty to the Soma dynasty — both 'most glorious and purifying.' Different lineages of devotional history both purify; we don't need to choose which great river to honor.

BG 14.2

Lord Viṣṇu [Garbhodakaśāyī Viṣṇu] is also known as Sahasra-śīrṣā Puruṣa. From the lake of His navel sprang a lotus, on which Lord Brahmā was generated. Atri, the son of Lord Brahmā, was as qualified as his father.

Essence. From Viṣṇu's navel comes the lotus, from the lotus Brahmā, and from Brahmā, Atri — as qualified as his father. Real spiritual succession passes on actual qualification, not merely social position.

BG 14.3

From Atri’s tears of jubilation was born a son named Soma, the moon, who was full of soothing rays. Lord Brahmā appointed him the director of the brāhmaṇas, drugs and luminaries.

Essence. Soma, born from Atri's tears of jubilation, is appointed director of brāhmaṇas, drugs, and luminaries. Even tears of joy can give rise to whole spheres of responsibility — emotion sincerely offered to the Lord bears unexpected fruit.

BG 14.4

After conquering the three worlds [the upper, middle and lower planetary systems], Soma, the moon-god, performed a great sacrifice known as the Rājasūya-yajṣa. Because he was very much puffed up, he forcibly kidnapped Bṛhaspati’s wife, whose name was Tārā.

Essence. Soma, puffed up after conquering the three worlds, kidnaps Bṛhaspati's wife Tārā. Sudden success without inner growth is dangerous — material victories often unlock our worst behaviors next.

BG 14.5

Although requested again and again by Bṛhaspati, the spiritual master of the demigods, Soma did not return Tārā. This was due to his false pride. Consequently, a fight ensued between the demigods and the demons.

Essence. Despite the guru's repeated requests, Soma refuses to return Tārā. False pride can override every reasonable plea — and when even sound voices cannot recover us, war becomes inevitable.

BG 14.6

Because of enmity between Bṛhaspati and Śukra, Śukra took the side of the moon-god and was joined by the demons. But Lord Śiva, because of affection for the son of his spiritual master, joined the side of Bṛhaspati and was accompanied by all the ghosts and hobgoblins.

Essence. Old enmities sort the sides — Śukra with the demons against Bṛhaspati, Śiva with Bṛhaspati out of guru-loyalty. Loyalty to one's guru's son can land us in fights we never chose, while old grudges quietly recruit us for the wrong team.

BG 14.7

King Indra, accompanied by all kinds of demigods, joined the side of Bṛhaspati. Thus there was a great fight, destroying both demons and demigods, only for the sake of Tārā, Bṛhaspati’s wife.

Essence. Demigods and demons destroy each other 'only for the sake of Tārā.' Mass devastation often begins with a single unyielded attachment of one influential person.

BG 14.8

When Lord Brahmā was fully informed by Aṅgirā about the entire incident, he severely chastised the moon-god, Soma. Thus Lord Brahmā delivered Tārā to her husband, who could then understand that she was pregnant.

Essence. Brahmā chastises Soma severely and delivers Tārā back. Senior intervention — when finally accepted — can resolve catastrophes that escalated only because junior parties refused to yield earlier.

BG 14.9

Bṛhaspati said: You foolish woman, your womb, which was meant for me to impregnate, has been impregnated by someone other than me. Immediately deliver your child! Immediately deliver it! Be assured that after the child is delivered, I shall not burn you to ashes. I know that although you are unchaste, you wanted a son. Therefore I shall not punish you.

Essence. Bṛhaspati spares Tārā's life but demands she immediately deliver the child. He recognizes the offense, withholds the harshest reaction, and yet does not pretend nothing happened. Mature mercy is not denial.

BG 14.10

Śukadeva Gosvāmī continued: By Bṛhaspati’s order, Tārā, who was very much ashamed, immediately gave birth to the child, who was very beautiful, with a golden bodily hue. Both Bṛhaspati and the moon-god, Soma, desired the beautiful child.

Essence. Both Bṛhaspati and Soma want the beautiful golden child. Children of disputed parentage become objects of competing claims — and the truth has not yet been spoken.

BG 14.11

Fighting again broke out between Bṛhaspati and the moon-god, both of whom claimed, “This is my child, not yours!” All the saints and demigods present asked Tārā whose child the newborn baby actually was, but because she was ashamed she could not immediately answer.

Essence. Asked to identify the father, Tārā cannot answer for shame. Shame, however human, can lock us out of the truth that would actually resolve the situation.

BG 14.12

The child then became very angry and demanded that his mother immediately tell the truth. “You unchaste woman,” he said, “what is the use of your unnecessary shame? Why do you not admit your fault? Immediately tell me about your faulty behavior.”

Essence. The newborn child himself angrily demands his mother tell the truth. Sometimes the very next generation forces the honesty the previous one keeps avoiding.

BG 14.13

Lord Brahmā then brought Tārā to a secluded place, and after pacifying her he asked to whom the child actually belonged. She replied very slowly, “This is the son of Soma, the moon-god.” Then the moon-god immediately took charge of the child.

Essence. Brahmā gently takes Tārā aside, pacifies her, and only then asks. Truth-telling requires safe space — public demands rarely produce honest answers.

BG 14.14

O Mahārāja Parīkṣit, when Lord Brahmā saw that the child was deeply intelligent, he gave the child the name Budha. The moon-god, the ruler of the stars, enjoyed great jubilation because of this son.

Essence. Brahmā names the deeply intelligent child Budha — and Soma rejoices over a son born of disputed circumstances. The Lord's arrangement allows even messily-born children to be brilliant and beloved.

BG 14.15-16

Thereafter, from Budha, through the womb of Ilā, a son was born named Purūravā, who was described in the beginning of the Ninth Canto. When his beauty, personal qualities, magnanimity, behavior, wealth and power were described by Nārada in the court of Lord Indra, the celestial woman Urvaśī was attracted to him. Pierced by the arrow of Cupid, she thus approached him.

Essence. From Budha and Ilā comes Purūravā, so attractive that even celestial Urvaśī comes pierced by Cupid's arrow. The compound effects of good lineage and bhakti can produce souls whose magnetism reaches beyond their own world.

BG 14.17-18

Having been cursed by Mitra and Varuṇa, the celestial woman Urvaśī had acquired the habits of a human being. Therefore, upon seeing Purūravā, the best of males, whose beauty resembled that of Cupid, she controlled herself and then approached him. When King Purūravā saw Urvaśī, his eyes became jubilant in the ecstasy of joy, and the hairs on his body stood on end. With mild, pleasing words, he spoke to her as follows.

Essence. Urvaśī, cursed to have human habits, approaches Purūravā with passion. Even celestial beings, under the right curse, develop the same vulnerabilities we have — and the meeting of two cursed beauties is rarely a happy long story.

BG 14.19

King Purūravā said: O most beautiful woman, you are welcome. Please sit here and tell me what I can do for you. You may enjoy with me as long as you desire. Let us pass our life happily in a sexual relationship.

Essence. Purūravā instantly invites the unknown woman to enjoy life with him 'as long as you desire.' Speed of commitment, made under attraction, leaves no time for the discernment that any union actually requires.

BG 14.20

Urvaśī replied: O most handsome man, who is the woman whose mind and sight would not be attracted by you? If a woman takes shelter of your chest, she cannot refuse to enjoy with you in a sexual relationship.

Essence. Urvaśī admits no woman could refuse him. Mutual physical attraction, once engaged, becomes nearly impossible to dis-engage by willpower alone — the time to manage it is before the engagement.

BG 14.21

My dear King Purūravā, please give protection to these two lambs, who have fallen down with me. Although I belong to the heavenly planets and you belong to earth, I shall certainly enjoy sexual union with you. I have no objection to accepting you as my husband, for you are superior in every respect.

Essence. Urvaśī asks Purūravā to protect two lambs that fell down with her — and proposes marriage on those terms. Every union comes with conditions and dependents we must accept; we rarely get the partner without the lambs.

BG 14.22

Urvaśī said: “My dear hero, only preparations made in ghee [clarified butter] will be my eatables, and I shall not want to see you naked at any time, except at the time of sexual intercourse.” The great-minded King Purūravā accepted these proposals.

Essence. Urvaśī sets two conditions — only ghee preparations, never see her husband naked except during intimacy. Specific, even unusual, commitments may be the very glue that keeps a fragile bond intact.

BG 14.23

Purūravā replied: O beautiful one, your beauty is wonderful and your gestures are also wonderful. Indeed, you are attractive to all human society. Therefore, since you have come of your own accord from the heavenly planets, who on earth would not agree to serve a demigoddess such as you.

Essence. Purūravā agrees, marveling at her beauty and divine origin. A man dazzled by his partner's apparent superiority often agrees to terms whose weight he has not understood.

BG 14.24

Śukadeva Gosvāmī continued: The best of human beings, Purūravā, began freely enjoying the company of Urvaśī, who engaged in sexual activities with him in many celestial places, such as Caitraratha and Nandana-kānana, where the demigods enjoy.

Essence. The pair travel from one celestial garden to another, enjoying themselves freely. The honeymoon phase is real and beautiful — but its very ease can disguise what conditions actually sustain the union.

BG 14.25

Urvaśī’s body was as fragrant as the saffron of a lotus. Being enlivened by the fragrance of her face and body, Purūravā enjoyed her company for many days with great jubilation.

Essence. Urvaśī's body is fragrant as lotus saffron — Purūravā is enlivened for many days. Beauty alone can sustain a relationship for a long time, but never indefinitely — the body's freshness is not the foundation.

BG 14.26

Not seeing Urvaśī in his assembly, the King of heaven, Lord Indra, said, “Without Urvaśī my assembly is no longer beautiful.” Considering this, he requested the Gandharvas to bring her back to his heavenly planet.

Essence. Indra, missing Urvaśī, says his court is 'no longer beautiful' and arranges for her recovery. Beautiful people are claimed by multiple kingdoms — and the one currently holding them is rarely the only suitor.

BG 14.27

Thus the Gandharvas came to earth, and at midnight, when everything was dark, they appeared in the house of Purūravā and stole the two lambs entrusted to the King by his wife, Urvaśī.

Essence. At midnight, in deep darkness, the Gandharvas steal the lambs. The very condition that secured the marriage is removed by an external party at the moment of least vigilance.

BG 14.28

Urvaśī treated the two lambs like her own sons. Therefore, when they were being taken by the Gandharvas and began crying, Urvaśī heard them and rebuked her husband. “Now I am being killed,” she said, “under the protection of an unworthy husband, who is a coward and a eunuch although he thinks himself a great hero.”

Essence. Urvaśī erupts: under an unworthy husband she is being 'killed,' a coward who only seems brave by day. Crisis exposes whether our partner believes us to be the protector we have claimed to be.

BG 14.29

“Because I depended on him, the plunderers have deprived me of my two sons the lambs, and therefore I am now lost. My husband lies down at night in fear, exactly like a woman, although he appears to be a man during the day.”

Essence. She accuses him of cowering at night like a woman though appearing manly by day. The Bhāgavatam plainly preserves the harshest words; we should not be shocked when relationships under stress turn brutal.

BG 14.30

Purūravā, stricken by the sharp words of Urvaśī like an elephant struck by its driver’s pointed rod, became very angry. Not even dressing himself properly, he took a sword in hand and went out naked into the night to follow the Gandharvas who had stolen the lambs.

Essence. Stung by her words, Purūravā goes out naked into the night with a sword. Embarrassed manhood often acts impulsively without proper preparation — the dignity dropped is rarely recoverable.

BG 14.31

After giving up the two lambs, the Gandharvas shone brightly like lightning, thus illuminating the house of Purūravā. Urvaśī then saw her husband returning with the lambs in hand, but he was naked, and therefore she left.

Essence. He recovers the lambs but his nakedness illuminates the house — and Urvaśī, seeing the broken condition, leaves. A condition broken even for one valid reason still counts as broken; love does not always survive technical violations.

BG 14.32

No longer seeing Urvaśī on his bed, Purūravā was most aggrieved. Because of his great attraction for her, he was very much disturbed. Thus, lamenting, he began traveling about the earth like a madman.

Essence. Bereft of Urvaśī, Purūravā wanders the earth like a madman. Loss of a beloved can derange even a king — material love, by its nature, equips us for this madness.

BG 14.33

Once during his travels all over the world, Purūravā saw Urvaśī, accompanied by five companions, on the bank of the Sarasvatī at Kurukṣetra. With jubilation in his face, he then spoke to her in sweet words as follows.

Essence. Eventually he finds her at Kurukṣetra on the Sarasvatī, accompanied by five companions. Sacred places have a way of arranging encounters that random wandering will not — there is a reason to go to tīrthas.

BG 14.34

O my dear wife, O most cruel one, kindly stay, kindly stay. I know that I have never made you happy until now, but you should not give me up for that reason. This is not proper for you. Even if you have decided to give up my company, let us nonetheless talk for some time.

Essence. Purūravā begs her to stay, admitting he has never truly made her happy. Belated honesty about one's failings is real, but it is rarely sufficient to repair what they caused.

BG 14.35

O goddess, now that you have refused me, my beautiful body will fall down here, and because it is unsuitable for your pleasure, it will be eaten by foxes and vultures.

Essence. He threatens to die so foxes and vultures will eat his body. Manipulative grief — including the threat of self-destruction — is also a temptation that the Bhāgavatam unflinchingly shows.

BG 14.36

Urvaśī said: My dear King, you are a man, a hero. Don’t be impatient and give up your life. Be sober and don’t allow the senses to overcome you like foxes. Don’t let the foxes eat you. In other words, you should not be controlled by your senses. Rather, you should know that the heart of a woman is like that of a fox. There is no use making friendship with women.

Essence. Urvaśī tells him not to let the 'foxes' of his senses devour him — and warns that a woman's heart is fox-like, so friendship with women is futile. Whatever we make of the cultural claim, the deeper warning is against making the senses our advisor.

BG 14.37

Women as a class are merciless and cunning. They cannot tolerate even a slight offense. For their own pleasure they can do anything irreligious, and therefore they do not fear killing even a faithful husband or brother.

Essence. Urvaśī describes women as merciless and cunning, capable of irreligious behavior for their own pleasure. Whatever the era's tone, the underlying caution is against assuming romantic loyalty will be stronger than self-interest.

BG 14.38

Women are very easily seduced by men. Therefore, polluted women give up the friendship of a man who is their well-wisher and establish false friendship among fools. Indeed, they seek newer and newer friends, one after another.

Essence. Women are described as easily seduced, abandoning well-wishers for new false friends. The śāstra speaks frankly; the modern reader can mine the underlying principle that unprotected attention drifts toward novelty.

BG 14.39

O my dear King, you will be able to enjoy with me as my husband at the end of every year, for one night only. In this way you will have other children, one after another.

Essence. Urvaśī offers him one night per year. Even fleeting access to the beloved becomes precious — and the structure of that one annual night was actually a remarkable concession on her part.

BG 14.40

Understanding that Urvaśī was pregnant, Purūravā returned to his palace. At the end of the year, there at Kurukṣetra, he again obtained the association of Urvaśī, who was then the mother of a heroic son.

Essence. Pregnant Urvaśī returns at year's end with a heroic son. Even diminished relationships can still produce real fruit — and we should not refuse the fruit because the relationship is not what we wished.

BG 14.41

Having regained Urvaśī at the end of the year, King Purūravā was most jubilant, and he enjoyed her company in sex for one night. But then he was very sorry at the thought of separation from her, so Urvaśī spoke to him as follows.

Essence. After their one night, separation distresses him again. Periodic access to a beloved often leaves the lover worse off than steady distance — intermittent reinforcement is the most addictive pattern.

BG 14.42

Urvaśī said: “My dear King, seek shelter of the Gandharvas, for they will be able to deliver me to you again.” In accordance with these words, the King satisfied the Gandharvas by prayers, and the Gandharvas, being pleased with him, gave him an Agnisthālī girl who looked exactly like Urvaśī. Thinking that the girl was Urvaśī, the King began walking with her in the forest, but later he could understand that she was not Urvaśī but Agnisthālī.

Essence. Urvaśī sends him to the Gandharvas, who give him an Agnisthālī girl who only looks like her. Lust is satisfied by appearances at first, but the moment of real contact reveals the substitution.

BG 14.43

King Purūravā then left Agnisthālī in the forest and returned home, where he meditated all night upon Urvaśī. In the course of his meditation, the Tretā millennium began, and therefore the principles of the three Vedas, including the process of performing yajṣa to fulfill fruitive activities, appeared within his heart.

Essence. Returning home, he meditates all night on Urvaśī, and the principles of yajṣa appear in his heart. Even intense attachment, brought into stillness, can become the doorway through which spiritual knowledge enters.

BG 14.44-45

When the process of fruitive yajṣa became manifest within his heart, King Purūravā went to the same spot where he had left Agnisthālī. There he saw that from the womb of a śamī tree, an aśvattha tree had grown. He then took a piece of wood from that tree and made it into two araṇis. Desiring to go to the planet where Urvaśī resided, he chanted mantras, meditating upon the lower araṇi as Urvaśī, the upper one as himself, and the piece of wood between them as his son. In this way he began to ignite a fire.

Essence. He carves araṇis from an aśvattha-growing-in-śamī tree, treats them as Urvaśī, himself, and son, and ignites yajṣa fire. Even longing for a beloved can be translated into the sacred fire of sacrifice — symbolism elevated to ritual.

BG 14.46

From Purūravā’s rubbing of the araṇis came a fire. By such a fire one can achieve all success in material enjoyment and be purified in seminal birth, initiation and in the performance of sacrifice, which are invoked with the combined letters a-u-m. Thus the fire was considered the son of King Purūravā.

Essence. The fire produced from those rubbings becomes a kind of son to Purūravā — fire-born offspring through yajṣa is recognized alongside seminal and initiated sons. Spiritual fatherhood through sacred work is just as real as biological.

BG 14.47

By means of that fire, Purūravā, who desired to go to the planet where Urvaśī resided, performed a sacrifice, by which he satisfied the Supreme Personality of Godhead, Hari, the enjoyer of the results of sacrifice. Thus he worshiped the Lord, who is beyond the perception of the senses and is the reservoir of all the demigods.

Essence. By that fire he performs sacrifice and satisfies Hari, who is the actual proprietor of every loka. Whatever the apparent goal, sincere yajṣa pleases the Supreme — and that is what really opens any door.

BG 14.48

In the Satya-yuga, the first millennium, all the Vedic mantras were included in one mantra — praṇava, the root of all Vedic mantras. In other words, the Atharva Veda alone was the source of all Vedic knowledge. The Supreme Personality of Godhead Nārāyaṇa was the only worshipable Deity; there was no recommendation for worship of the demigods. Fire was one only, and the only order of life in human society was known as haṁsa.

Essence. In Satya-yuga there was one Veda, one mantra (oṁ), one Deity (Nārāyaṇa), one fire, and one āśrama (haṁsa). Original spirituality is radically simple — multiplications came later as accommodations for our complexity.

BG 14.49

O Mahārāja Parīkṣit, at the beginning of Tretā-yuga, King Purūravā inaugurated a karma-kāṇḍa sacrifice. Thus Purūravā, who considered the yajṣic fire his son, was able to go to Gandharvaloka as he desired.

Essence. At Tretā's beginning, Purūravā inaugurates karma-kāṇḍa sacrifice and travels to Gandharvaloka. The age conditions the method — what works in Tretā may not be ideal in Kali, where the holy name is recommended.
