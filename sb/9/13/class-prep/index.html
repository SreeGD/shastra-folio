BG 13.1

Śrīla Śukadeva Gosvāmī said: After beginning sacrifices, Mahārāja Nimi, the son of Ikṣvāku, requested the great sage Vasiṣṭha to take the post of chief priest. At that time, Vasiṣṭha replied, “My dear Mahārāja Nimi, I have already accepted the same post in a sacrifice begun by Lord Indra.”

Essence. Nimi asks Vasiṣṭha to be his chief priest, but the sage has already accepted the same role for Indra. Even the greatest spiritual functionaries have prior commitments — and good service requires honoring previous yeses.

BG 13.2

“I shall return here after finishing the yajṣa for Indra. Kindly wait for me until then.” Mahārāja Nimi remained silent, and Vasiṣṭha began to perform the sacrifice for Lord Indra.

Essence. Vasiṣṭha asks Nimi to wait until the Indra yajṣa is finished; Nimi remains silent. Silence is not always assent — it can be the polite cover for a different plan we have not yet voiced.

BG 13.3

Mahārāja Nimi, being a self-realized soul, considered that this life is flickering. Therefore, instead of waiting long for Vasiṣṭha, he began performing the sacrifice with other priests.

Essence. Considering life flickering, Nimi performs the sacrifice with other priests rather than waiting. Awareness of mortality drives the wise to act now — but acting without honoring elders has its own karma, even when reasoning is sound.

BG 13.4

After completing the sacrificial performance for King Indra, the spiritual master Vasiṣṭha returned and found that his disciple Mahārāja Nimi had disobeyed his instructions. Thus Vasiṣṭha cursed him, saying, “May the material body of Nimi, who considers himself learned, immediately fall.”

Essence. Returning, Vasiṣṭha curses his disciple's body to fall for considering himself learned. Pride displayed by a disciple to a guru, even under the cover of philosophical reasoning, can produce harsh reactions.

BG 13.5

For unnecessarily cursing him when he had committed no offense, Mahārāja Nimi countercursed his spiritual master. “For the sake of getting contributions from the King of heaven,” he said, “you have lost your religious intelligence. Therefore I pronounce this curse: your body also will fall.”

Essence. Nimi counter-curses Vasiṣṭha — pointing out that the sage was greedy for Indra's compensation. Even great brāhmaṇas can compromise principle for remuneration, and the right disciple may, painfully, have to name it.

BG 13.6

After saying this, Mahārāja Nimi, who was expert in the science of spiritual knowledge, gave up his body. Vasiṣṭha, the great-grandfather, gave up his body also, but through the semen discharged by Mitra and Varuṇa when they saw Urvaśī, he was born again.

Essence. Both give up their bodies; Vasiṣṭha is reborn through Mitra and Varuṇa's seminal discharge upon seeing Urvaśī. Even mighty sages can be undone in a moment by lust — and the seed thus emitted, kept in a pot, fathers a great soul.

BG 13.7

During the performance of the yajṣa, the body relinquished by Mahārāja Nimi was preserved in fragrant substances, and at the end of the Satra-yāga the great saints and brāhmaṇas made the following request to all the demigods assembled there.

Essence. Nimi's body is preserved in fragrant substances during the yajṣa. The forms left behind by saintly persons continue to merit care, even after the soul has departed.

BG 13.8

“If you are satisfied with this sacrifice and if you are actually able to do so, kindly bring Mahārāja Nimi back to life in this body.” The demigods said yes to this request by the sages, but Mahārāja Nimi said, “Please do not imprison me again in a material body.”

Essence. The demigods agree to revive Nimi, but he refuses — he does not want re-imprisonment in a material body. Some grace must be politely declined when accepting it would chain us again.

BG 13.9

Mahārāja Nimi continued: Māyāvādīs generally want freedom from accepting a material body because they fear having to give it up again. But devotees whose intelligence is always filled with the service of the Lord are unafraid. Indeed, they take advantage of the body to render transcendental loving service.

Essence. Māyāvādīs avoid bodies for fear of pain; devotees, fearless, take any body that lets them serve. The motive — escape versus service — entirely changes the spiritual significance of accepting or rejecting embodiment.

BG 13.10

I do not wish to accept a material body, for such a body is the source of all distress, lamentation and fear, everywhere in the universe, just as it is for a fish in the water, which lives always in anxiety because of fear of death.

Essence. Nimi compares embodied life to a fish's anxious life underwater — always under threat of death. Higher and lower planets alike are temporary, and pretending otherwise is the fundamental confusion.

BG 13.11

The demigods said: Let Mahārāja Nimi live without a material body. Let him live in a spiritual body as a personal associate of the Supreme Personality of Godhead, and, according to his desire, let him be manifest or unmanifest to common materially embodied people.

Essence. The demigods grant Nimi a spiritual body to live as an associate of the Lord, manifest or unmanifest to ordinary people. There is a kind of devotional existence whose presence is real even when not visible to material eyes.

BG 13.12

Thereafter, to save the people from the danger of an unregulated government, the sages churned Mahārāja Nimi’s material body, from which, as a result, a son was born.

Essence. To save the people from danger of unregulated rule, the sages churn Nimi's body and produce a son. Power vacuums are dangerous, and the wise act to fill them with someone qualified before chaos sets in.

BG 13.13

Because he was born in an unusual way, the son was called Janaka, and because he was born from the dead body of his father, he was known as Vaideha. Because he was born from the churning of his father’s material body, he was known as Mithila, and because he constructed a city as King Mithila, the city was called Mithilā.

Essence. Janaka, Vaideha, Mithila — three names for the same king, each marking his unusual birth and his founding of a city. Single souls accumulate names by what they undergo and accomplish.

BG 13.14

O King Parīkṣit, from Mithila came a son named Udāvasu; from Udāvasu, Nandivardhana; from Nandivardhana, Suketu; and from Suketu, Devarāta.

Essence. Udāvasu, Nandivardhana, Suketu, Devarāta — the dynasty continues quietly. There is a meditative usefulness to simply hearing each name acknowledged, even though we will not remember them.

BG 13.15

From Devarāta came a son named Bṛhadratha and from Bṛhadratha a son named Mahāvīrya, who became the father of Sudhṛti. The son of Sudhṛti was known as Dhṛṣṭaketu, and from Dhṛṣṭaketu came Haryaśva. From Haryaśva came a son named Maru.

Essence. Bṛhadratha, Mahāvīrya, Sudhṛti, Dhṛṣṭaketu, Haryaśva, Maru — each name signifies a quality (great chariot, great power, firm intellect). Naming children for desired virtues is itself a devotional act.

BG 13.16

The son of Maru was Pratīpaka, and the son of Pratīpaka was Kṛtaratha. From Kṛtaratha came Devamīḍha; from Devamīḍha, Viśruta; and from Viśruta, Mahādhṛti.

Essence. Pratīpaka, Kṛtaratha, Devamīḍha, Viśruta, Mahādhṛti — fame, dedication, firmness pass through the line. Lineages carry their own family of qualities; we should know which ones our line tends to produce.

BG 13.17

From Mahādhṛti was born a son named Kṛtirāta, from Kṛtirāta was born Mahāromā, from Mahāromā came a son named Svarṇaromā, and from Svarṇaromā came Hrasvaromā.

Essence. Kṛtirāta, Mahāromā, Svarṇaromā, Hrasvaromā — the line continues toward its most famous descendant. Long careful lineages culminate in just the right person at just the right time.

BG 13.18

From Hrasvaromā came a son named Śīradhvaja [also called Janaka]. When Śīradhvaja was plowing a field, from the front of his plow [śīra] appeared a daughter named Sītādevī, who later became the wife of Lord Rāmacandra. Thus he was known as Śīradhvaja.

Essence. Śīradhvaja is plowing a field when Sītā appears at the tip of his plow — divinity emerges from the most ordinary labor. The Lord's consort can be uncovered in the soil of routine work, not only in grand temples.

BG 13.19

The son of Śīradhvaja was Kuśadhvaja, and the son of Kuśadhvaja was King Dharmadhvaja, who had two sons, namely Kṛtadhvaja and Mitadhvaja.

Essence. Kuśadhvaja, Dharmadhvaja, Kṛtadhvaja, Mitadhvaja — the names all carry the word 'dhvaja' (flag). Family identity coheres around shared symbols of dharma.

BG 13.20-21

O Mahārāja Parīkṣit, the son of Kṛtadhvaja was Keśidhvaja, and the son of Mitadhvaja was Khāṇḍikya. The son of Kṛtadhvaja was expert in spiritual knowledge, and the son of Mitadhvaja was expert in Vedic ritualistic ceremonies. Khāṇḍikya fled in fear of Keśidhvaja. The son of Keśidhvaja was Bhānumān, and the son of Bhānumān was Śatadyumna.

Essence. Kṛtadhvaja's son is expert in spiritual knowledge; Mitadhvaja's son in ritual; one flees the other in fear. Even within the same dharmic family, internal rivalry can drive members into exile.

BG 13.22

The son of Śatadyumna was named Śuci. From Śuci, Sanadvāja was born, and from Sanadvāja came a son named Ūrjaketu. The son of Ūrjaketu was Aja, and the son of Aja was Purujit.

Essence. Śuci, Sanadvāja, Ūrjaketu, Aja, Purujit — the line of Janakas continues. The Bhāgavatam quietly insists that we acknowledge those who held the role before us in any honor we claim.

BG 13.23

The son of Purujit was Ariṣṭanemi, and his son was Śrutāyu. Śrutāyu begot a son named Supārśvaka, and Supārśvaka begot Citraratha. The son of Citraratha was Kṣemādhi, who became the king of Mithilā.

Essence. Ariṣṭanemi, Śrutāyu, Supārśvaka, Citraratha, Kṣemādhi — the dynasty marches forward. Sometimes the only worship is to stay faithfully at the post handed to us.

BG 13.24

The son of Kṣemādhi was Samaratha, and his son was Satyaratha. The son of Satyaratha was Upaguru, and the son of Upaguru was Upagupta, a partial expansion of the fire-god.

Essence. Upagupta is a partial expansion of the fire-god. Even within ordinary dynastic listings, the Bhāgavatam quietly mentions the divine origins of certain figures — there are more partial incarnations among us than we suspect.

BG 13.25

The son of Upagupta was Vasvananta, the son of Vasvananta was Yuyudha, the son of Yuyudha was Subhāṣaṇa, and the son of Subhāṣaṇa was Śruta. The son of Śruta was Jaya, from whom there came Vijaya. The son of Vijaya was Ṛta.

Essence. Vasvananta, Yuyudha, Subhāṣaṇa, Śruta, Jaya, Vijaya, Ṛta — even more names, each meaning something noble. Even when we do not know the person, we can honor the meaning of the name they carried.

BG 13.26

The son of Ṛta was Śunaka, the son of Śunaka was Vītahavya, the son of Vītahavya was Dhṛti, and the son of Dhṛti was Bahulāśva. The son of Bahulāśva was Kṛti, and his son was Mahāvaśī.

Essence. Śunaka, Vītahavya, Dhṛti, Bahulāśva, Kṛti, Mahāvaśī — the line continues to its end. The Bhāgavatam will close this dynasty, and we should not take eternal continuation for granted in any of our own lines.

BG 13.27

Śukadeva Gosvāmī said: My dear King Parīkṣit, all the kings of the dynasty of Mithila were completely in knowledge of their spiritual identity. Therefore, even though staying at home, they were liberated from the duality of material existence.

Essence. All the kings of Mithilā were self-realized — though staying at home, they were liberated from material duality. Liberation does not require leaving the household; clarity about the self is the actual gate.
