Value at a Glance

Nyaya means being fair. Being fair means giving each person what is right for them — not the same for all, but what each one needs. A big brother may need a bigger plate; a baby may need a softer blanket. Nyaya listens to both sides. Nyaya speaks up when something is unfair. Nyaya gives everyone a chance.

The Verse

Bhagavad-gita 2.38: Krishna tells Arjuna: make your heart the SAME in happy and sad, getting and not getting, winning and losing. Then stand up for what is right. If you do it this way, you will never do wrong.

What it is NOT

Nyaya is not giving the biggest piece to the child who shouts loudest. It is not taking one side just because they are your friend. It is not punishing someone before you hear what happened. It is not staying quiet when a smaller child is being hurt. Fair is not always equal — fair is giving each one what is right.

  • Deciding your friend is right in a fight without asking the other child
  • Keeping the biggest laddu for yourself and giving the smallest one to your little sister
  • Looking away when a bigger child grabs a smaller child's pencil

Stories Your Child Heard

Yudhishthira and the Thirsty Lake — Long ago, the five Pandava brothers lived in the deep forest. They were thirsty. Yudhishthira, the eldest, sent Nakula to find water. Nakula found a clear lake. As he bent to drink, a voice came from the water: 'Answer me first, then drink.' Nakula was too thirsty. He drank. He fell asleep. One by one, Sahadeva, Arjuna, and Bhima came. Each drank without listening. Each fell asleep.

Meera and the Three Pencils — Meera was eight, and class monitor on art day. Teacher gave her three shiny new pencils. 'Meera, share these in your group. I trust you to be fair.' Meera's group had four children. Jai was her best friend — he had new pencils at home. Ravi was new and quiet; his box was empty. Tara had the biggest box in school — twenty colours. Little Ani from Std 1 had just been crying because her one pencil snapped.

The Sanskrit Word

nyāya (न्याय) — NYAA-ya

Meaning: Fairness. Giving each person what is right for them — not the same for all, but what each one needs. Listening to both sides before deciding.

Ask your child to teach you this word!

Home Practice

For one week, be the Fair Helper at home. Every day do three small things: (1) Share ONE thing — a snack, a toy, a turn — by NEED, not equally. Give the smaller child a softer piece, the hungrier child a bit more. (2) If a small argument starts between you and a sibling or cousin, do not shout first. Say 'Wait — let me tell you my side; then you tell yours.' Use a calm voice. (3) If you notice something unfair at home (someone forgotten, someone given less), speak up gently to Amma or Appa: 'I think this is not fair; can we look?' At bedtime, tell your parent ONE fair thing you did and draw a tiny scale ⚖ on your calendar. At week's end, count your scales together as a family.

Duration: Every day for one week
Look for: By week's end, the child's home calendar has at least 5 scale marks, the child can describe two moments when they listened to both sides before speaking, and parents notice the child saying 'Wait, let me hear' or 'That would be more fair' in an ordinary family moment.

Signs of Growth

By week's end, the child's home calendar has at least 5 scale marks, the child can describe two moments when they listened to both sides before speaking, and parents notice the child saying 'Wait, let me hear' or 'That would be more fair' in an ordinary family moment.

When our hands learn to give each person what is right, our hearts grow calm and strong. And a calm, strong heart — steady in joy and sorrow, in winning and losing — has its own beautiful name: samatva, the evenness that is the seed of every higher value Krishna teaches.
