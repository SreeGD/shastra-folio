Verse Anchor

Bhagavad-gita 2.38

सुखदुःखे समे कृत्वा लाभालाभौ जयाजयौ।
ततो युद्धाय युज्यस्व नैवं पापमवाप्स्यसि॥

sukha-duḥkhe same kṛtvā lābhālābhau jayājayau tato yuddhāya yujyasva naivaṁ pāpam avāpsyasi

Word-for-word: sukha — happy; duḥkhe — sad; same — the same; kṛtvā — after making; lābha — getting; alābhau — not getting; jaya — winning; ajayau — losing; tataḥ — then; yuddhāya — for the right fight; yujyasva — get ready; na evam — like this; pāpam — wrong; avāpsyasi — you will do.

Translation: Krishna tells Arjuna: make your heart the SAME in happy and sad, getting and not getting, winning and losing. Then stand up for what is right. If you do it this way, you will never do wrong.

Srimad Bhagavatam Verse

SB 1.17.24

तपः शौचं दया सत्यमिति धर्माः पदा विभोः।
निर्मिताः सत्ययुगादौ वदन्ति मुनयो मखे॥

tapaḥ śaucaṁ dayā satyam iti dharmāḥ padā vibhoḥ nirmitāḥ satya-yugādau vadanti munayo makhe

Translation: Austerity, cleanliness, kindness, and truthfulness — these are the four legs of dharma, the sacred path that holds up the world. A fair heart stands on all four.

What is Nyaya?

Nyaya means being fair. Being fair means giving each person what is right for them — not the same for all, but what each one needs. A big brother may need a bigger plate; a baby may need a softer blanket. Nyaya listens to both sides. Nyaya speaks up when something is unfair. Nyaya gives everyone a chance.

What it is NOT

Nyaya is not giving the biggest piece to the child who shouts loudest. It is not taking one side just because they are your friend. It is not punishing someone before you hear what happened. It is not staying quiet when a smaller child is being hurt. Fair is not always equal — fair is giving each one what is right.

  • Deciding your friend is right in a fight without asking the other child
  • Keeping the biggest laddu for yourself and giving the smallest one to your little sister
  • Looking away when a bigger child grabs a smaller child's pencil

Sanskrit Word Study

nyāya (न्याय)

Pronunciation: NYAA-ya

Meaning: Fairness. Giving each person what is right for them — not the same for all, but what each one needs. Listening to both sides before deciding.

Example: When Meera gave pencils to those who had none first, she was practising nyaya.

Story from the Scriptures

Yudhishthira and the Thirsty Lake — Mahabharata, Vana Parva (simplified for primary students)

Long ago, the five Pandava brothers lived in the deep forest. They were thirsty. Yudhishthira, the eldest, sent Nakula to find water. Nakula found a clear lake. As he bent to drink, a voice came from the water: 'Answer me first, then drink.' Nakula was too thirsty. He drank. He fell asleep.

One by one, Sahadeva, Arjuna, and Bhima came. Each drank without listening. Each fell asleep.

At last Yudhishthira walked to the lake. He saw his four brothers lying still. His heart was heavy. The same voice spoke: 'Answer me. Then drink.'

Yudhishthira bowed. 'I will answer first.'

'What is faster than the wind?' Yudhishthira said, 'The mind.'

'What is heavier than the earth?' 'A mother's love.'

'What is the greatest happiness?' 'A clean heart at night.'

Every answer was fair. Every answer was true.

Then the voice — a Yaksha, a forest spirit — said: 'You have answered well. I will bring ONE brother back. Which one do you choose?'

Yudhishthira thought of mighty Bhima. He thought of Arjuna the archer. Both were his own mother's sons.

He folded his hands. 'Please, bring Nakula back.'

The Yaksha was surprised. 'Not Bhima? Not Arjuna? Why Nakula?'

Yudhishthira said softly, 'My mother Kunti already has me. If only one son may live, her sister Madri — who is gone — must also have a son. Nakula is Madri's son. That is the fair choice.'

The Yaksha's voice grew warm. 'You are truly just. A king who thinks this way is a king of nyaya.'

All four brothers woke up. They drank. They lived. And Yudhishthira became known everywhere as Dharmaraja — the Fair King.

A Story from Today

Meera and the Three Pencils

Meera was eight, and class monitor on art day. Teacher gave her three shiny new pencils. 'Meera, share these in your group. I trust you to be fair.'

Meera's group had four children. Jai was her best friend — he had new pencils at home. Ravi was new and quiet; his box was empty. Tara had the biggest box in school — twenty colours. Little Ani from Std 1 had just been crying because her one pencil snapped.

Three pencils. Four children. Everyone looked at Meera.

Jai leaned close. 'Give me one! I am your best friend.'

Meera's hands felt hot. It would be easy to give Jai one. But something in her chest tipped like a tiny scale.

She remembered Daddi's story. 'Yudhishthira asked for Nakula, not Bhima — because Madri had no other son. Fair is not always equal.'

Meera took a breath and stood tall.

'Ravi,' she said gently, 'your box is empty. This red one is for you.' Ravi's eyes went wide.

'Ani,' said Meera, kneeling. 'Your pencil broke. This blue one is for you.' Ani's tears turned into a smile.

'Tara, can you share one colour from your full box today?' Tara blinked, then nodded, and pulled out a green pencil.

'Jai, this last one is for you. Is this plan fair?'

Jai was quiet. Then he smiled. 'Ravi needed one more than me. Ani too. Yes — it is fair.' He hugged Meera. 'You are a true fair monitor.'

Everyone coloured happily. Meera's chest felt light. That is nyaya.

Core Teaching

  1. Nyaya means being fair. Fair is not always equal. Fair is giving each person what is right for them — the one with nothing gets more, the one with much gives a little. Krishna's heart is a fair heart.
  2. Before we decide anything, we listen to BOTH sides. We do not take a side just because it is our friend's side. We ask, 'What really happened?' — then we speak.
  3. When something unfair happens in front of us — a bigger child hurts a smaller one, a friend is blamed wrongly — nyaya asks us to speak up kindly. Quiet when wrong is happening is not nyaya.
When our hands learn to give each person what is right, our hearts grow calm and strong. And a calm, strong heart — steady in joy and sorrow, in winning and losing — has its own beautiful name: samatva, the evenness that is the seed of every higher value Krishna teaches.
