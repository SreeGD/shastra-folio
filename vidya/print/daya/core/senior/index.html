Verse Anchor

Bhagavad-gita 16.2

अहिंसा सत्यमक्रोधस्त्यागः शान्तिरपैशुनम् ।
दया भूतेष्वलोलुप्त्वं मार्दवं ह्रीरचापलम् ॥२॥

ahiṁsā satyam akrodhas tyāgaḥ śāntir apaiśunam dayā bhūteṣv aloluptvaṁ mārdavaṁ hrīr acāpalam

Word-for-word: ahiṁsā — nonviolence; satyam — truthfulness; akrodhaḥ — freedom from anger; tyāgaḥ — renunciation; śāntiḥ — tranquillity; apaiśunam — aversion to faultfinding; dayā — mercy; bhūteṣu — towards all living entities; aloluptvam — freedom from greed; mārdavam — gentleness; hrīḥ — modesty; acāpalam — determination

Translation: Nonviolence, truthfulness, freedom from anger, renunciation, tranquillity, aversion to faultfinding, compassion for all living entities, freedom from covetousness, gentleness, modesty, steady determination.

Srimad Bhagavatam Verse

SB 10.14.32

यस्य यद्दयया स्वार्थे यत्करोति यदिच्छया। दैवेनैवाभिपद्यन्ते यत्र लोका: सचराचरा:

Translation: Whatever a person does in this world out of compassion and love, by divine arrangement all moving and nonmoving beings automatically receive the benefit of such actions.

What is Daya?

Daya is the divine quality of compassion that naturally arises when we recognize the spiritual kinship we share with all living beings. True compassion moves beyond mere sympathy to active care—feeling moved by another's suffering and taking thoughtful action to help alleviate it. It flows from understanding that every being is part of the same spiritual family.

What it is NOT

Daya is not mere pity that looks down on others from a position of superiority. It is not enabling harmful behavior in the name of kindness, nor is it emotional overwhelm that leaves us paralyzed by others' pain. True compassion is not blind sentiment that ignores wisdom.

  • Pity that maintains distance and superiority over those who suffer
  • Enabling addiction or harmful behavior to avoid difficult conversations
  • Compassion fatigue that leads to emotional burnout and withdrawal
  • Sentimental kindness that ignores practical wisdom and boundaries

Sanskrit Word Study

dayā (दया)

Pronunciation: dah-YAH

Meaning: Compassion, mercy, divine kindness that flows from recognizing the spiritual kinship of all beings

Example: When we develop true dayā, we naturally want to alleviate others' suffering not from a sense of superiority, but from understanding our fundamental connection to all life.

Story from the Scriptures

Karna's Ultimate Generosity — Mahabharata, Vana Parva 300-302

Young Karna had always been known for his extraordinary generosity. Born with divine golden armor and earrings that made him invincible, he never refused any request from a Brahmin, no matter the cost to himself.

As the great war approached, Indra, king of the gods and father of Arjuna, grew worried. He knew that with his divine protection, Karna would be impossible to defeat in battle. So Indra devised a plan—he would disguise himself as a poor Brahmin and ask for the very armor that protected Karna's life.

One morning, as Karna finished his prayers to the sun god, an old Brahmin approached him. The man's clothes were torn, his body thin from hunger. "O generous son," the Brahmin said, "I have heard of your fame. They say you never refuse a request from one like me."

Karna's heart immediately filled with compassion. "Speak your wish, respected one. Whatever I have is yours."

The Brahmin's eyes gleamed. "I ask for your golden armor and earrings—the very ones you were born with."

Karna's blood turned cold. He recognized something divine in the Brahmin's bearing, and a terrible understanding dawned. This was no ordinary request. Someone wanted him defenseless for the coming war. Because he was known as the greatest archer after Arjuna, because he fought on the side of the Kauravas, this had to be connected to his enemies.

For a moment, Karna hesitated. His mind raced with the implications. Without his divine protection, he would be vulnerable in battle. His death would be almost certain. His wife, his children—what would become of them?

But then Karna looked into the Brahmin's eyes and saw not an enemy, but a being in need. His teacher Parashurama had taught him that true dharma meant keeping one's word regardless of personal cost. Although his heart pounded with fear, his voice remained steady.

"Honored Brahmin, what you ask will cost me my life. But I have never refused a sincere request, and I will not start now."

Without another word, Karna drew his knife. The armor and earrings were not mere ornaments—they were part of his flesh, grown from his very bones. As he cut them away, blood flowed freely. The pain was excruciating, but Karna's hands never trembled.

When he placed the golden armor and earrings in the Brahmin's hands, the disguised Indra was moved despite himself. "Son," he said, his voice thick with emotion, "your compassion has conquered even the gods. Though I came as your enemy's father, I leave as one who honors your greatness."

As Indra revealed his true form and departed, Karna stood bleeding but at peace. He had chosen daya over self-preservation, generosity over survival. Although he knew this choice would likely cost him everything, he felt no regret. True compassion, he understood, sometimes demands the ultimate sacrifice.

A Story from Today

The Price of Compassion

Arjun first noticed something was wrong when his classmates started complaining about missing lunch money. Small amounts—ten or twenty rupees—but enough to leave some students hungry. As class monitor, everyone expected him to investigate.

The pattern became clear after a week of careful observation. The thefts always happened during physical education class, when bags were left unattended. Because Arjun had twisted his ankle, he stayed back in the classroom and finally saw the truth that made his stomach clench: his best friend Rohit, quietly going through backpacks.

Arjun's first instinct was anger. How could Rohit betray their classmates' trust? But as he watched his friend's face—the guilt, the desperate way Rohit counted the coins—something deeper stirred in Arjun's heart. This wasn't greed. This was desperation.

That evening, Arjun followed Rohit home instead of going to cricket practice. He watched from across the street as his friend entered a small pharmacy and emerged with a bag of medicines. Then Rohit hurried to the tiny apartment where he lived with his mother.

Through the window, Arjun saw the truth that changed everything. Rohit's mother lay on a thin mattress, her face pale and drawn. She was diabetic, and without regular insulin, she would die. Rohit's father had abandoned them two years ago, leaving them with nothing but debt.

Arjun's anger dissolved into something much more complex. Although he understood Rohit's desperation, he also saw how the stealing was hurting their classmates. Some came to school without breakfast and depended on their lunch money for their only meal of the day. Rohit's compassion for his mother was creating suffering for others.

The next morning, Arjun made his choice. Instead of reporting Rohit to the principal, he pulled his friend aside during lunch break.

"I know about your mother," Arjun said gently. "And I know about the money."

Rohit's face crumpled. "Arjun, please don't tell anyone. She'll die without the medicine, and I don't know what else to do."

"I'm not going to report you," Arjun said. "But we need to find another way. What you're doing is hurting people who are also struggling."

That afternoon, Arjun went to their class teacher, Mrs. Sharma, with a carefully planned story. "Ma'am, I've been thinking about starting a small fundraiser for medical emergencies in our community. Sometimes families need help but are too proud to ask."

Mrs. Sharma's eyes lit up. "What a wonderful idea, Arjun. How would it work?"

Over the next week, Arjun organized what he called the "Community Care Fund." Students could contribute whatever they wanted—even five rupees made a difference. He approached local shopkeepers for small donations and organized a talent show where students performed for contributions.

The response overwhelmed him. Because the fund was presented as helping "families in our neighborhood," no one felt singled out or pitied. Students gave generously, teachers contributed, and even the school principal added money from the staff fund.

When Arjun handed Rohit an envelope with enough money to cover his mother's medicines for three months, his friend's eyes filled with tears.

"How did you...?"

"Everyone contributed," Arjun said. "But Rohit, you have to promise me—no more stealing. If you need help, come to me first. We'll find a way that doesn't hurt others."

Rohit nodded, unable to speak. The next day, he quietly returned what money he could to the classmates he had taken from, claiming he had "found" it.

Months later, the Community Care Fund had helped four different families and become a permanent part of their school culture. Rohit's mother's health stabilized, and he found a part-time job tutoring younger students to help with expenses.

Arjun learned that true compassion required both heart and wisdom. It meant seeing the whole picture—understanding why people suffer and addressing root causes, not just symptoms. Most importantly, he discovered that the best way to help someone preserve their dignity was to create solutions that lifted everyone up together.

Core Teaching

  1. True compassion combines a tender heart with practical wisdom—feeling moved by suffering while taking thoughtful action that addresses root causes, not just symptoms.
  2. Authentic daya recognizes the spiritual kinship between all beings, seeing others not as separate from ourselves but as part of the same divine family deserving of care and respect.
  3. The highest compassion sometimes requires difficult choices that prioritize long-term wellbeing over immediate comfort, including setting boundaries when kindness might enable harmful behavior.
Next we will explore seva (selfless service) — the natural expression of daya in action, where compassion moves from feeling to dedicated service for others' wellbeing.
