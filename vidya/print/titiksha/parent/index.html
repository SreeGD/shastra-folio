Value at a Glance

Titiksha means bearing little troubles with a quiet heart. When the sun is hot on my head, when my legs are tired from a long walk, when a friend says a mean word, when I must wait a long time for my turn — I can stay steady. I do not fall apart. I do not cry and shout over small things. I remember Krishna, take a slow breath, and say inside, 'This will pass. I can bear it.' Titiksha is a soft, brave patience that belongs to every child of Krishna.

The Verse

Bhagavad-gita 2.14: Krishna says: O Arjuna, hot and cold, pleasure and pain — these touches come and go like little visitors. They never stay. They are not forever. So, My dear friend — just bear them quietly. This quiet bearing is called titiksha.

What it is NOT

Titiksha is not pretending nothing hurts. It is okay to feel hot, tired, sad, or hurt — those feelings are real. Titiksha is not hurting ourselves or staying in danger — if the sun is too hot, we go to the shade; if someone is unkind, we tell a grown-up. Titiksha is not being sulky and silent. It is not showing off — 'Look how tough I am!' It is a quiet, gentle strength. Not stone — warm stone.

  • Crying loudly when we have to wait five minutes for lunch
  • Shouting 'UNFAIR!' and stomping off at the first little trouble
  • Falling to the floor in a tantrum when the ice cream is the wrong flavour
  • Saying 'I can't! I can't!' before even trying to carry a small bag

Stories Your Child Heard

Yudhishthira in the Forest — Long ago, there was a kind king named Yudhishthira, eldest of five Pandava brothers. They lived in a golden palace on soft cotton beds. One day, through a trick, the cousins took the kingdom. 'Go to the forest for twelve years,' they said. 'No palace. No silks.'

Arnav and the Long Hot Afternoon — It was Tuesday in May. Arnav was seven. His class went on a trip to an old Krishna temple. The sun was a big yellow ball — HOT. The bus stopped far from the temple. 'We walk from here,' said the teacher. 'Twenty minutes.'

The Sanskrit Word

titikṣā (तितिक्षा) — ti-TEEK-sha

Meaning: Quiet bearing. A gentle, brave patience that lets me stay steady when little troubles come — hot sun, tired legs, mean words, long waits. It is not pretending nothing hurts — it is remembering that the trouble will pass, and Krishna is walking with me through it.

Ask your child to teach you this word!

Home Practice

For one week, keep a small Titiksha Jar beside your bed. Beside it, keep a little bowl with small pebbles or dry lentils. Every evening before sleep, think about the day. For EACH small trouble you bore quietly — a hot classroom, a long wait in the car, a mean word from a friend, a scraped knee, a tired walk — drop ONE pebble into your jar. Whisper: 'Krishna, I bore this quietly for You.' In the morning, touch the jar gently and whisper: 'Ti-tik-sha. Today I will bear small things with a quiet heart.'

Duration: Every day for one week
Look for: By the end of the week, the child can name at least 3 specific 'small troubles' they bore quietly, the Titiksha Jar contains at least 5-7 pebbles, and the child spontaneously uses the word titiksha when meeting a small hardship ('That is okay, Amma — titiksha'). Parents notice fewer tantrums at transitions (waiting, heat, minor disappointments).

Signs of Growth

By the end of the week, the child can name at least 3 specific 'small troubles' they bore quietly, the Titiksha Jar contains at least 5-7 pebbles, and the child spontaneously uses the word titiksha when meeting a small hardship ('That is okay, Amma — titiksha'). Parents notice fewer tantrums at transitions (waiting, heat, minor disappointments).

When our heart grows quiet and brave through titiksha, we are ready for the steady courage of all seasons — which has its own beautiful name: dhairya.
