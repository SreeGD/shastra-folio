Verse Anchor

Bhagavad-gita 2.14

मात्रास्पर्शास्तु कौन्तेय शीतोष्णसुखदुःखदाः।
आगमापायिनोऽनित्यास्तांस्तितिक्षस्व भारत॥

mātrā-sparśās tu kaunteya śītoṣṇa-sukha-duḥkha-dāḥ āgamāpāyino 'nityās tāṁs titikṣasva bhārata

Word-for-word: mātrā-sparśāḥ — the touches of the senses (hot, cold, pleasure, pain); tu — but; kaunteya — O son of Kuntī (Arjuna); śīta — cold; uṣṇa — hot; sukha — pleasure; duḥkha — pain; dāḥ — giving; āgama — coming; apāyinaḥ — going; anityāḥ — not permanent; tān — them; titikṣasva — learn to tolerate; bhārata — O best of the Bhāratas.

Translation: Krishna says: O Arjuna, hot and cold, pleasure and pain — these touches come and go like little visitors. They never stay. They are not forever. So, My dear friend — just bear them quietly. This quiet bearing is called titiksha.

Srimad Bhagavatam Verse

तत्तेऽनुकम्पां सुसमीक्षमाणो
भुञ्जान एवात्मकृतं विपाकम्।
हृद्वाग्वपुर्भिर्विदधन्नमस्ते
जीवेत यो मुक्तिपदे स दायभाक्॥

tat te 'nukampāṁ su-samīkṣamāṇo bhuñjāna evātma-kṛtaṁ vipākam hṛd-vāg-vapurbhir vidadhan namas te jīveta yo mukti-pade sa dāya-bhāk

Translation: Brahma prays to Krishna: 'My dear Lord, one who quietly bears whatever little difficulties come in life as Your kind arrangement — who sees everything as Your mercy — who bows to You with heart, words, and body — that dear child of Yours walks gently into the kingdom of freedom.'

What is Titiksha?

Titiksha means bearing little troubles with a quiet heart. When the sun is hot on my head, when my legs are tired from a long walk, when a friend says a mean word, when I must wait a long time for my turn — I can stay steady. I do not fall apart. I do not cry and shout over small things. I remember Krishna, take a slow breath, and say inside, 'This will pass. I can bear it.' Titiksha is a soft, brave patience that belongs to every child of Krishna.

What it is NOT

Titiksha is not pretending nothing hurts. It is okay to feel hot, tired, sad, or hurt — those feelings are real. Titiksha is not hurting ourselves or staying in danger — if the sun is too hot, we go to the shade; if someone is unkind, we tell a grown-up. Titiksha is not being sulky and silent. It is not showing off — 'Look how tough I am!' It is a quiet, gentle strength. Not stone — warm stone.

  • Crying loudly when we have to wait five minutes for lunch
  • Shouting 'UNFAIR!' and stomping off at the first little trouble
  • Falling to the floor in a tantrum when the ice cream is the wrong flavour
  • Saying 'I can't! I can't!' before even trying to carry a small bag

Sanskrit Word Study

titikṣā (तितिक्षा)

Pronunciation: ti-TEEK-sha

Meaning: Quiet bearing. A gentle, brave patience that lets me stay steady when little troubles come — hot sun, tired legs, mean words, long waits. It is not pretending nothing hurts — it is remembering that the trouble will pass, and Krishna is walking with me through it.

Example: When Arnav kept walking under the hot sun, whispering 'One more step' and remembering Krishna, he was practising titiksha.

Story from the Scriptures

Yudhishthira in the Forest — Mahabharata, Vana Parva (traditional retelling of the Pandavas' forest exile)

Long ago, there was a kind king named Yudhishthira, eldest of five Pandava brothers. They lived in a golden palace on soft cotton beds.

One day, through a trick, the cousins took the kingdom. 'Go to the forest for twelve years,' they said. 'No palace. No silks.'

Yudhishthira did not shout. He folded his royal clothes, put on tree-bark cloth, and said, 'Come, brothers. The forest will teach us.'

The forest was HARD. The sun was hot like fire. At night, cold wind bit their ears. Their feet walked on sharp stones. Food was only roots and berries. The bed was dry leaves.

One evening, Bhima sat down with a big sigh. 'Brother, my feet are burning! My tummy is empty! When will this end?'

Yudhishthira smiled softly and put his hand on Bhima's shoulder.

'Little brother, Krishna tells us — hot and cold, happy and sad, all come and go like visitors at a door. They never stay. So we bear them quietly. That is titiksha.'

Bhima took a deep breath. 'But it is HARD.'

'I know,' said Yudhishthira. 'That is why it is a gift.'

Every day Yudhishthira thanked the forest — the river for water, the mango tree for fruits, the ants for teaching him to walk softly. He never grumbled.

Slowly, wonderful things happened. Wise rishis came from far away and sat beside him under the peepal. Deer came close without fear. One evening Krishna Himself came and laughed with Yudhishthira like a best friend.

Twelve years passed. The forest had given the Pandavas something better than silks — it gave them titiksha. A quiet, steady, brave heart.

A Story from Today

Arnav and the Long Hot Afternoon

It was Tuesday in May. Arnav was seven. His class went on a trip to an old Krishna temple. The sun was a big yellow ball — HOT.

The bus stopped far from the temple. 'We walk from here,' said the teacher. 'Twenty minutes.'

Rohan groaned. 'In this sun?'

They started walking. The sun felt heavy on Arnav's head. After five minutes, WHOOSH — a wind took his blue cap into a thorny bush.

Now the sun was directly on his hair.

Arnav tilted his water bottle. Empty. His tongue felt dry. His legs began to ache.

Rohan started to cry. 'I can't! I want to go HOME!'

Arnav almost cried too.

Then he remembered. Last Sunday his Dadi had taught him a Sanskrit word. TITIKSHA. She had said, 'Beta, when something small is hard, breathe, remember Krishna is walking with you, and keep going.'

Arnav closed his eyes. He took a slow breath. He whispered — 'Krishna, walk with me.'

He opened his eyes. Far ahead, the tiny orange flag on the temple fluttered gently.

'One step,' he whispered. 'One more step.'

He walked. He did not shout. He gave his last drop of water to dry Rohan's tears. 'Come, friend. Titiksha. One more step.'

When they reached the temple gate, the teacher smiled big. 'Arnav, you kept going!'

Inside the cool, dark temple, Arnav bowed to Krishna. His legs ached. His head was hot. But inside, something felt quiet and tall — taller than the temple tower.

That was titiksha.

Core Teaching

  1. Titiksha means I can bear little troubles quietly. Hot sun, long walk, empty tummy, mean word, long wait — these come and go like visitors. They never stay. So I take a breath, remember Krishna, and keep going.
  2. Krishna says in the Bhagavad-gita — mātrā-sparśāḥ. The touches of the world come and go. They are not forever. A brave child does not fall apart at every small hard thing. A brave child says, 'This will pass. I can bear it. Krishna is with me.'
  3. Titiksha is not pretending nothing hurts. Feelings are real. But titiksha is a gentle inner strength — like a little tree bending in the wind and not breaking. When we bear small troubles with a quiet heart, Krishna comes close, rishis come close, and our own heart grows tall.
When our heart grows quiet and brave through titiksha, we are ready for the steady courage of all seasons — which has its own beautiful name: dhairya.
