Value at a Glance

Kshama means forgiveness. When someone hurts me — with words, with hands, with a broken promise — I do not hold the hurt tight inside my heart like a burning stone. I let it go. Forgiving does not mean saying 'it was okay' or that they can do it again. It means freeing MY OWN heart from the sting. The sting is not the gift Krishna wants me to keep. A clean, soft heart is.

The Verse

Bhagavad-gita 16.3: Krishna tells Arjuna: shining courage, forgiveness that lets go of hurt, steady holding on, a clean heart, no wish to hurt back, and no puffed-up pride — these beautiful qualities shine in the child who is born for goodness.

What it is NOT

Kshama is not pretending nothing happened. It is not saying 'it was fine' when it was not fine. It is not letting someone hurt you again and again. It is not weakness. Kshama is the STRONGEST thing a small heart can do — put down the hot stone before it burns the hand holding it.

  • Holding on to a mean word for days, growing grumpier and grumpier
  • Hitting back the person who hit you — 'now you know how it feels'
  • Secretly planning to make the person who hurt you feel bad tomorrow

Stories Your Child Heard

Little Prahlada Forgives His Father — Long ago there was a tiny boy named Prahlada. He was only five years old. But his heart was as big as the sky, because it was full of love for Krishna. Prahlada's father was the strongest king in the world. His name was Hiranyakashipu. But his heart was dark. He hated Krishna, and he hated anyone who loved Krishna.

Anya and the Broken Elephant — Anya was seven. She had one most-special thing in the whole world. It was a tiny wooden elephant her Thatha had carved for her. The elephant had soft-painted eyes and a curly trunk. Anya called him Chotu. One Sunday her best friend Kavya came over to play. They built a block-castle for Chotu. Kavya picked up Chotu to put him on the castle — and FIZZ-OOPS! — Chotu slipped from her fingers. He fell on the stone floor. When Anya picked him up, his tiny trunk had snapped off.

The Sanskrit Word

kṣamā (क्षमा) — ksha-MAA

Meaning: Forgiveness — letting go of the hurt that burns inside my own heart when someone wrongs me. Not 'it was okay'; rather, 'my heart will not stay on fire.'

Ask your child to teach you this word!

Home Practice

Every night before bed this week, do the Forgiveness Garden practice with Amma or Appa. Think of ONE small hurt from your day — a mean word, a forgotten promise, a broken thing, a pushing hand. Hold your fists tight for three seconds. Then take three slow breaths. On each breath, open your fingers a little more. On the third breath, whisper: 'Kshama. I let this go. My heart is clean.' Then draw one tiny flower in your home calendar. If there was no hurt that day, draw a flower anyway — as thanks to Krishna for a peaceful day. At the end of the week, count your flowers together as a family.

Duration: Once each night for one week (7 flowers minimum)
Look for: By the end of the week, the child's home calendar has at least 5 flowers drawn, the child can demonstrate the fist-open breathing practice without a reminder at least once, and Amma or Appa notices the child using a phrase like 'it's okay, I forgive' or 'I'm putting the stone down' at least once during an ordinary family moment.

Signs of Growth

By the end of the week, the child's home calendar has at least 5 flowers drawn, the child can demonstrate the fist-open breathing practice without a reminder at least once, and Amma or Appa notices the child using a phrase like 'it's okay, I forgive' or 'I'm putting the stone down' at least once during an ordinary family moment.

When our heart learns to let go of hurt — again and again — it becomes clean and quiet inside. And a clean, quiet heart hears Krishna's gentle voice more easily. That listening heart has its own beautiful name: shanti — the peace we find after kshama has done its work.
