Verse Anchor

Bhagavad-gita 18.33

धृत्या यया धारयते मनःप्राणेन्द्रियक्रियाः।
योगेनाव्यभिचारिण्या धृतिः सा पार्थ सात्त्विकी॥

dhṛtyā yayā dhārayate manaḥ-prāṇendriya-kriyāḥ yogenāvyabhicāriṇyā dhṛtiḥ sā pārtha sāttvikī

Word-for-word: dhṛtyā — by steady holding; yayā — by which; dhārayate — one sustains; manaḥ — mind; prāṇa — breath; indriya — senses; kriyāḥ — activities; yogena — by yoga; avyabhicāriṇyā — without wandering; dhṛtiḥ — steadfastness; sā — that; pārtha — O Arjuna; sāttvikī — in the mode of goodness.

Translation: Krishna says: The steady hand that keeps holding — that holds the mind quiet, the breath calm, and the senses kind, without wandering — THAT steady hand is the good kind. Its name is dhṛti.

What is Dhriti?

Dhriti is the quiet hand that keeps holding. When I started something bright and happy, and then the middle got long and tired — dhriti is the hand inside me that says, 'Keep going, little one. Just one more line. Just one more day.' Dhriti does not shout. Dhriti does not run. Dhriti only STAYS. Krishna's own hand is inside my dhriti, helping me hold on.

What it is NOT

Dhriti is not stubbornness — holding on to a bad habit just because I started. It is not pretending I am not tired. It is not pushing myself until I cry. True dhriti is gentle and steady, like a quiet lamp that keeps shining — not like a firework that bursts bright and dies. It stays because it is right, not because 'I must win.'

  • Starting the colouring with joy on page 1 and throwing it away on page 2 because it got boring
  • Practising my song only on the days I feel like it, and skipping the hard days
  • Giving up on the long maths problem after one wrong answer
  • Saying 'I already tried!' after trying only once

Sanskrit Word Study

dhṛti (धृति)

Pronunciation: DHRI-ti (aspirated 'dh', short 'i')

Meaning: The quiet hand that keeps holding. Staying steady when the middle gets long and tired. Not sparkle, not shouting — just gentle, patient, Krishna-filled holding-on.

Example: When Kavya played the sitar for thirty mornings, even on the tired days, that was dhriti.

Story from the Scriptures

Bharata Holds the Sandals for Fourteen Long Years — Ramayana — Ayodhya Kanda and Uttara Kanda (Valmiki)

Long ago in Ayodhya, four brothers loved each other dearly — Rama, Lakshmana, Bharata, and Shatrughna.

One sad day, a big mistake sent Lord Rama to the forest for fourteen years. Bharata was far away. When he heard, his heart broke like a clay pot.

He ran to the forest and fell at Rama's feet. 'Brother, come home. The throne is yours. I cannot sit on it.'

Rama lifted him gently. 'Little brother, our father's promise must be kept. You must hold the kingdom for me.'

Bharata's eyes filled with tears. 'Then give me a sign.'

Rama took off his wooden sandals — his padukas — and placed them in Bharata's hands. 'These will be your king. Place them on the throne.'

Bharata carried the sandals home. He placed them gently on the golden throne. He did not sit on the throne himself. Not for one day.

He wore plain bark cloth, slept on the hard floor, ate simple food. Every single morning he bowed. 'Brother, I am still here. I am still waiting.'

The fifth year his friends whispered, 'Prince, wear the crown!' Bharata only shook his head.

Some days his heart hurt. But his quiet hand kept holding. The kingdom was ruled with justice. The poor were fed. The temples were clean.

Year twelve. Year thirteen. The sandals still sat on the throne.

On the last day of the fourteenth year — trumpets! Rama was coming home!

Bharata ran and fell at his feet. Rama held him tight. 'Little brother. Your dhriti has held the whole kingdom together.'

That is dhriti. The quiet hand that keeps holding — for as long as love asks.

A Story from Today

Kavya and the Thirty-Day Sitar

Kavya was eight. One Sunday she heard a sitar concert and fell in LOVE. She ran home. 'Amma, Appa — I want to learn!'

Appa thought. 'Kavya-beta, dhriti first. Will you promise thirty mornings — even on hard days?'

'YES!'

Guruji gave her a small sitar and a green card with thirty squares. 'Even five minutes on a tired day.'

Day one — her fingers flew. Tick.

Day five — fingertips began to sting. Tick.

Day seven — the strings sounded like a cat sneezing. Tick.

Day ten. Kavya sat on her bed. 'I don't want to today,' she whispered. Her fingertips had red lines. Her heart felt like a flat balloon.

Amamma — her grandmother — came in softly and took her hand.

'Little one, this is the real moment. The sparkle is gone. Only dhriti is left. Krishna is watching closest now.'

'But Amamma, it sounds UGLY.'

Amamma smiled. 'Remember Bharata and the sandals? Fourteen years he kept holding. Shall we do five small minutes today?'

Kavya picked up the sitar. She played five minutes. Badly. But she played. Tick.

Day eleven — five minutes. Tick.

Day fifteen — her fingers didn't hurt as much. Tick.

Day twenty — the sound was getting rounder.

Day thirty. Kavya took a long breath and played 'Sa Re Ga Ma.' The notes came clean and sweet, like four small birds.

'It worked, Amamma.'

Amamma touched her forehead. 'The quiet hand held, beta. THAT is dhriti. More beautiful than the music.'

Kavya closed her eyes. 'Krishna, this song is for You.'

Core Teaching

  1. Dhriti is the quiet hand that keeps holding. Udyama is the bright beginning. But dhriti is what comes AFTER the sparkle — when the middle gets long and tired. A quiet hand that stays is stronger than a loud hand that runs.
  2. Krishna says in the Gita — sāttvikī dhṛti holds the mind quiet, the breath calm, and the senses kind, without wandering. This is not stubbornness. This is gentle, steady staying — because the work is good, because Krishna is watching, because love asks us to hold on.
  3. Dhriti grows one small day at a time. Five minutes on a tired day is more precious than thirty minutes on an excited day. Bharata held the sandals for 5,110 days. Kavya held the sitar for thirty mornings. Your quiet hand can hold anything — if you let Krishna hold it with you.
When our quiet hand has held on through all the long days, something deep and shining begins to grow inside — a slow fire that burns off laziness and grows bright strength. That slow, bright fire has its own beautiful name: tapas.
