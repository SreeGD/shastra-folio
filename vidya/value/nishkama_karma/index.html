Karma

Nishkama Karma

निष्काम कर्म · nish-KAH-ma KAR-ma

EN हिन्दी

What it is

Nishkama karma means doing the right thing because it is right — not because we want a prize, a sticker, or a clap. We help because someone needs help. We study because learning is good. We are kind because kindness is beautiful. Whether anyone sees or not, we keep doing our work with a happy heart.

What it is not

Nishkama karma is not being lazy. It is not skipping our chores because 'it does not matter.' It is not being sad when nobody notices. It is also not doing good things ONLY so that people will praise us. The work itself — done with love — is the real reward.

Word study

Word
निष्काम कर्म (niṣkāma karma)
Say it
nish-KAH-ma KAR-ma
Meaning
Doing the right work without wanting a prize. 'Nish' means 'without'; 'kama' means 'selfish wish'; 'karma' means 'action.' Together: work done without the 'what will I get?' feeling — done with love, offered to Krishna.
Example
When Meera cleaned the art room and told no one, that was nishkama karma.

Anchor verse

Bhagavad-gita 2.47

कर्मण्येवाधिकारस्ते मा फलेषु कदाचन।
मा कर्मफलहेतुर्भूर्मा ते सङ्गोऽस्त्वकर्मणि॥

karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadācana mā karma-phala-hetur bhūr mā te saṅgo 'stv akarmaṇi

Krishna says: Your work is yours to do. The results are not. So do your best, little one — but do not do it FOR the prize. And do not stop doing good just because nobody is watching.

King Janaka — The King Who Ruled But Did Not Hold

Srimad Bhagavatam and Mahabharata traditions; Ashtavakra Gita framing

Long, long ago in the kingdom of Mithila, there ruled a very special king. His name was Janaka. He had a golden crown. He had a big palace. He had elephants and horses and many wise ministers.

But Janaka was not like other kings.

Other kings ruled because they loved being king. Janaka ruled because it was his duty. Other kings kept gold for themselves. Janaka gave it to the people. Other kings wanted everyone to clap for them. Janaka worked quietly, the way a farmer waters his plants — because that is what a farmer does.

One day, a wise sage came to Mithila. He looked at Janaka on his throne and frowned.

'Maharaja, how can YOU be a man of God? You wear a crown! You sit on a throne! How can a king be free inside?'

Janaka smiled gently. He called a servant. 'Please bring a small bowl of milk. Fill it right to the top.'

The servant brought the bowl.

Janaka said to the sage, 'Take this bowl. Walk all around my palace. See every room. See the treasury, the horses, the gardens. But do not spill ONE drop.'

The sage walked slowly. His eyes stayed on the milk. His hands stayed very steady. He saw the whole palace but spilled not one drop.

When he came back, Janaka asked, 'Did you enjoy the gardens?'

'I did not look at them.'

'Did you count the horses?'

'I did not look at them.'

'Why not?'

'Because I was watching the milk!'

Janaka smiled. 'That is how I rule, dear sage. My hands work. My eyes watch. But my heart stays on Krishna. I give every decision my full love. But the fruit — the praise, the gold, the throne — I do not cling to. It is like the milk. I carry it carefully. But it was never really mine.'

The sage bowed. 'Maharaja, YOU are the true karma-yogi.'

Janaka said softly, 'We are all asked to do our work. We are not asked to own the results. Do your duty like a clean lamp that simply shines. This is nishkama karma.'

Verse

SB 11.2.36

कायेन वाचा मनसेन्द्रियैर्वा
बुद्ध्यात्मना वानुसृतस्वभावात्।
करोति यद्यत्सकलं परस्मै
नारायणायेति समर्पयेत्तत्॥

kāyena vācā manasendriyair vā buddhyātmanā vānusṛta-svabhāvāt karoti yad yat sakalaṁ parasmai nārāyaṇāyeti samarpayet tat

Krishna says: Whatever you do — with your body, your voice, your mind, your senses, your thinking, or even just by being yourself — offer every bit of it to Narayana. Say softly, 'This is not mine. This is Yours.' That is the secret of selfless action.

Meera and the Missing Paintbrushes

It was Saturday morning. Meera came to art class fifteen minutes early because she loved the smell of paint and paper. She pushed open the classroom door.

The room was a mess.

The older children had used the room on Friday evening. They had left paintbrushes all over — some on the tables, some on the floor, one even on a chair. The brushes were dry and stiff with old red paint. Water bowls sat half-full. The rag was crumpled up in a ball.

Meera's eyes went big. 'Oh no. The teacher will scold everyone.'

She stood very still. Her class-teacher was not here yet. No other children had come. Nobody would know if Meera just sat in her seat and waited.

It was NOT her mess. It was NOT her job. And there was no sticker, no star chart, no reward for cleaning it.

Meera took a deep breath. She thought of what Nani had told her about nishkama karma — 'We do the kind thing because it is right, beta. Not because we want a prize.'

Meera rolled up her sleeves. She carried each stiff brush to the sink. She ran warm water over the bristles, one by one. The old red paint swirled away down the drain. She wiped each brush with the clean edge of the rag.

Then she lined the brushes in a neat row on the shelf, tallest to smallest. She tipped out the old water bowls. She folded the rag.

She did not sing. She did not hum. She did not look at the door to see if the teacher was coming. She just worked quietly, the way rain falls on a garden.

When she was done, Meera washed her own hands and went to her desk. She opened her art book. She did not tell anyone.

The teacher walked in. She saw the clean room. She blinked, smiled, and went to set up her own paints.

She never asked who had cleaned.

And Meera never told.

But inside her chest, Meera felt a soft steady warmth — like a little lamp that had been lit. That warmth did not need a clap. It was its own reward.

This is nishkama karma. Doing the right thing because it is right.

Core teaching

  • Nishkama karma means doing our work with full love — and letting go of the reward. We do the kind thing because it is RIGHT, not because we want a prize, a sticker, or a clap.
  • The work is ours to do. The fruit is Krishna's to give. We study fully. We help fully. We share fully. Then we leave the result in Krishna's hands and sleep with a peaceful heart.
  • A lamp does not light a room to get praise. It lights the room because that is its nature. When we live like a quiet lamp — shining whether people watch or not — our whole life becomes a beautiful offering.

Try this at home

For one week, keep your Quiet Lamp Jar on your desk or shelf. Every day, do ONE kind thing that NOBODY knows about — tidy a shoe, fold a napkin, refill the water jug, help a sibling silently, put away a toy that is not yours. For each secret kindness, drop ONE pebble or button into the jar. Do not count in front of others. Do not tell. At bedtime, close your eyes and whisper: 'Krishna, these pebbles are Yours. The work was for You.' At the end of the week, empty the jar by pouring the pebbles back into the garden — they belonged to Krishna, not to you.

Duration: Every day for one week
Watch for: By the end of the week, the child can describe at least 3 specific quiet kindnesses performed without announcing them, demonstrates the hands-on-heart gesture while saying 'nishkama karma', and has not asked for praise or acknowledgment for the jar's contents.

Activities

  • For the Prize or For the Right? · 6 min

    Circle the lamp if the child is doing the right thing for its own sake, or the star if they are doing it for a reward.

  • My Quiet Lamp · 10 min

    Draw a beautiful diya. Inside the flame, write one kind thing you can do this week without telling anyone.

  • Say It With Me · 3 min

    Chant 'nishkama karma' three times, hands on heart, like a steady bell.

  • The Secret Kindness Role-Play · 10 min

    With a partner, practice two ways of doing a kind thing — loud and clap-wanting, versus quiet and lamp-like. Notice which feels bigger inside.

  • The Quiet Lamp Jar · 5 min

    At home, keep a Quiet Lamp Jar. Drop one pebble for every secret kindness this week. Tell no one. Offer the pebbles to Krishna at week's end.
