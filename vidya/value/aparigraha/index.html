Aparigraha

अपरिग्रह · ah-pa-ree-GRA-ha

EN हिन्दी

What it is

Aparigraha means NOT piling up more than I need. I take just enough — one helping, not three. I keep what I truly use, and I share the rest. I do not want everyone's things to become mine. My shelf stays neat. My heart stays light. Krishna is the real owner — I am only His small helper with a small, tidy corner.

What it is not

Aparigraha is not having nothing. It is not throwing everything away in one big dramatic pile. It is not being stingy with what we DO have. It is not bragging about how little we own. True aparigraha is QUIET. It is just — enough. Not more. Not less.

Word study

Meaning

Anchor verse

Bhagavad-gita 2.71

श्रीभगवानुवाच
विहाय कामान्यः सर्वान्पुमांश्चरति निःस्पृहः।
निर्ममो निरहङ्कारः स शान्तिमधिगच्छति॥

śrī-bhagavān uvāca vihāya kāmān yaḥ sarvān pumāṁś carati niḥspṛhaḥ nirmamo nirahaṅkāraḥ sa śāntim adhigacchati

Krishna says: The one who quietly sets down endless wanting, who stops saying 'mine, mine, mine', and who walks through life with a light heart — that person reaches real peace.

The Rishi Who Owned Only a Bowl

Traditional ashram teaching tale, echoing the spirit of Patanjali Yoga Sutra 2.30

Long ago, in a green forest by a small river, a kind old rishi lived in a tiny hut. He had only three things. One cotton cloth to wear. One mat to sleep on. And ONE round wooden bowl.

Every morning he walked to the river, washed his bowl, and filled it with a little rice and some fruit. He ate, drank water, and sang Krishna's names all day. His eyes were bright. His heart was light.

One day a great king heard about the rishi. The king came in a golden chariot with many servants.

'O holy one,' the king said, bowing, 'you are so wise. Please come to my palace. I will give you a hundred servants. A hundred silk cloths. A hundred soft beds. A hundred bowls of gold and silver!'

The rishi smiled gently. He picked up his little wooden bowl. He held it in the sunlight.

'Great king,' he said softly, 'look at my bowl. Every morning it fills. Every evening it empties. It is just — enough.

'If I take a hundred bowls, I will have to count them. I will have to watch them. I will worry when one goes missing. I will fear thieves at night. My heart will become a storehouse, not a garden.

'One bowl is enough for rice. One cloth is enough for the body. One mat is enough for sleep. More than that, great king, is not wealth — it is weight.'

The king listened. His eyes grew wet. He looked at all his silks and jewels in the chariot. They suddenly felt heavy.

The rishi touched the king's shoulder gently. 'You are a good king. Keep what you truly use. Share the rest. Krishna is the owner of all. We are only His helpers for a little while.'

The king bowed low and returned to his palace. That very week, he opened his storehouses and shared grain with every hungry family in the land.

The rishi? He washed his one bowl, sang Krishna's name, and slept peacefully under the stars.

This is aparigraha. Not piling up. Just — enough.

Verse

SB 7.14.8

यावद्भ्रियेत जठरं तावत्स्वत्वं हि देहिनाम्।
अधिकं योऽभिमन्येत स स्तेनो दण्डमर्हति॥

yāvad bhriyeta jaṭharaṁ tāvat svatvaṁ hi dehinām adhikaṁ yo 'bhimanyeta sa steno daṇḍam arhati

One may take for oneself only as much as fills the belly. Whoever claims more than that is, the sages say, taking what does not belong to him. Aparigraha means trusting that Krishna has already given us just enough.

Aarav's Crowded Shelf

Aarav was seven years old. His toy shelf was FULL. So full that toys fell off every time he opened it.

There were three red cars. Four blue trucks. Two teddy bears with missing ears. A pile of blocks. A puzzle with lost pieces. A robot whose battery had been dead for a year. And somewhere — deep down — his favourite: a bright red fire engine.

One Sunday, Amma asked, 'Aarav-beta, please bring your red fire engine. We will play.'

Aarav ran to his shelf. He pulled out the cars. He pulled out the trucks. He pulled out the teddies. The blocks crashed to the floor. Where was the fire engine? Where?

After twenty minutes, he sat down and cried. 'Amma, I cannot find it!'

Amma came and sat on the floor beside him. She did not scold. She looked at all the toys.

'Aarav,' she said gently, 'how many of these do you play with every week?'

Aarav thought. 'The fire engine. The blocks. Maybe one teddy.'

'And the rest?'

Aarav looked. The dead robot. The puzzle with missing pieces. The extra cars. 'Not really, Amma.'

Amma smiled. 'Beta, when our shelf is too full, we cannot even FIND what we love. There is a beautiful old word — aparigraha. It means keeping only what we truly need. And sharing the rest.'

Amma brought an empty cardboard box. Together, they lifted out every toy Aarav had not played with in a long time. Aarav hesitated at first. But then Amma whispered, 'There are children who would LOVE this robot with a new battery. Imagine their smile.'

Box by box, they filled it. Slowly, Aarav's shelf cleared. There at the back — shining red — was his fire engine! Aarav laughed and hugged it.

The next day, they took the box to a shelter. A little boy's eyes LIT UP when he saw the robot.

Aarav walked home. His pocket was empty. His shelf was almost empty. But his heart? Big. Light. Full of sunshine.

'Amma,' he whispered, 'aparigraha feels GOOD.'

Amma kissed his head. 'Krishna is the real owner, beta. We are only keepers. A little corner is enough.'

Core teaching

  • Aparigraha means I take just enough — not more, not less. One helping at dinner. The toys I actually play with. The clothes I actually wear. I do not pile up things I will never use. My corner stays small and tidy.
  • Aparigraha is not the same as giving (dana) or letting go of what is in my hand (tyaga). Aparigraha is the quiet choice — BEFORE I grab — to NOT pile up. I see the extra toy and I smile: 'I do not need that. Someone else can have it.'
  • When my shelf is full of things I do not use, my heart feels heavy. When I keep only what I need and share the rest, my heart feels light. Krishna is the real owner of everything. I am only a small helper with a small, tidy corner.

Try this at home

This week, set up your Share Box at home. Each day, walk around your room and find ONE thing you do not really use — a toy, a book, a piece of clothing that is too small, a sticker you never used. Put it gently in the Share Box. Also practice the One-Helping Plate at every meal — take just enough first, ask only if you are still hungry. At bedtime, whisper: 'Krishna, I kept just enough today. The rest is Yours to share.' At week's end, give the Share Box to someone who needs what is inside.

Duration: Every day for one week
Watch for: By the end of the week, the Share Box has at least 5-7 items ready to donate, the child has eaten one helping first at most meals, and can name at least 2 specific things they DID NOT pile up (a toy not asked for at the shop, a second sweet not reached for).

Activities

  • Need It or Pile It · 6 min

    Circle NEED IT or PILE IT for each scenario.

  • My Tidy Shelf · 10 min

    Draw a small shelf with only what you truly use. Draw outside the shelf what you can share.

  • Say It With Me · 3 min

    Chant 'aparigraha' three times with palm-open, palm-gently-close gesture.

  • The Share Box · 12 min

    Decorate a Share Box. Fill it this week with items to pass on to someone who needs them.

  • The One-Helping Plate · 5 min

    At every meal, take one helping first. Ask for more only if still hungry. Notice: was one enough?
