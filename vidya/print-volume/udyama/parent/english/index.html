About This Book

This book is part of the Vidyā Karaṇa (विद्या करण) Value Education Series, designed for the Primary (Ages 6-9) age group.

Each chapter explores one of the values from the daivī sampad (divine qualities) described in the Bhagavad Gītā verses 16.1–3. The values are presented in a carefully sequenced order, where foundational values are taught before those that build upon them.

This Parent Book helps you reinforce at home what your child is learning at school. You do not need to read the full stories — your child will tell you! Instead, this book gives you conversation starters, signs to look for, and simple practices to do together as a family.

For each value, you will find:

  • Value at a Glance — What the value means, in plain language
  • The Verse — The scriptural foundation, in your language
  • Stories Your Child Heard — Brief summaries so you can ask about them
  • The Sanskrit Word — Ask your child to teach it to you!
  • Home Practice — The same action your child was given at school
  • Dinner Table Questions — 3–4 conversation starters for family discussion
  • Signs of Growth — Observable behaviours showing your child is internalizing the value

The most powerful teaching happens not in the classroom but at the dinner table.

Udyama

Udyama means bright-eyed beginning. It is the happy hop inside me when I say 'Yes! I will try!' I do not wait to be pushed. I do not sit and sulk. I roll up my sleeves, I smile, and I begin. If I stumble, I get up and try again. If the work is big, I break it small and start with the first piece. Udyama is the sunrise inside my chest.

The Verse

Bhagavad-gita 6.24: Krishna says: Begin your work with bright eyes and a strong wish (sankalpa). Step by step, with quiet determination (niścayena), keep going. Even if your feet are small, a strong sankalpa can climb a mountain.

What Udyama is NOT

Udyama is not running around madly just to look busy. It is not the worried kind of trying — 'I must win! I must be first!' It is not doing our work angrily or with a frowny face. And it is not only trying for a sticker or a sweet. True udyama is cheerful work — eyes bright, hands busy, heart light.

  • Waiting for Amma to ask five times before I start my homework
  • Saying 'I can't' before I have even tried
  • Starting a puzzle, dropping it after one minute, and walking off
  • Working only because I will get a prize — not because I love the work

Stories Your Child Heard

Lava and Kusha Practice Their Arrows at Sunrise — Deep in the forest, Rishi Valmiki had a small ashram. Two little boys lived there with their mother Sita. Their names were Lava and Kusha. They were twins. They were only seven years old. One evening, Rishi Valmiki gave them each a tiny bow. 'Boys,' he said, 'today you begin to learn archery.'

Raghav Starts the Classroom Garden — Raghav was in Standard Two in a little school in Hyderabad. His classroom had a window. Outside the window was a PATCH of brown empty dirt. Just dirt. No grass. No flowers. Just dirt. One day, teacher Meena-ji said, 'Children, one day we must plant a garden there. That patch is sad.'

The Sanskrit Word

udyama (उद्यम) — UD-ya-ma

Meaning: Bright-eyed beginning. The happy hop inside that says 'I will try!' Getting up on my own. The little sun in the chest. The one-more-try heart.

Ask your child to teach you this word!

Home Practice

For one week, keep your Sunrise Journal by your bed. Each morning, try to wake yourself before Amma calls — the little sun should wake you. Sit up, draw a tiny sun in your book, and write or draw ONE thing you will BEGIN today — brush teeth by yourself, help Papa with breakfast, start homework without being asked, water a plant, tidy your toys. At bedtime, tick it if you did it. Before sleeping, whisper: 'Krishna, my little sun shone today. Thank You for lighting me up.'

Duration: Every day for one week
Look for: By the end of the week, the child can describe at least 3 mornings when they woke themselves or began a task without being told, and can demonstrate the bright-eyes-hop gesture while chanting 'udyama' with a smile. The journal has at least 5 little suns drawn.

Signs of Growth

By the end of the week, the child can describe at least 3 mornings when they woke themselves or began a task without being told, and can demonstrate the bright-eyes-hop gesture while chanting 'udyama' with a smile. The journal has at least 5 little suns drawn.

When our little sun keeps shining even when the work is long and the path is steep, that steady-shining strength has its own beautiful name: dhṛti.
