About This Book

This book is part of the Vidyā Karaṇa (विद्या करण) Value Education Series, designed for the Primary (Ages 6-9) age group.

Each chapter explores one of the values from the daivī sampad (divine qualities) described in the Bhagavad Gītā verses 16.1–3. The values are presented in a carefully sequenced order, where foundational values are taught before those that build upon them.

This Parent Book helps you reinforce at home what your child is learning at school. You do not need to read the full stories — your child will tell you! Instead, this book gives you conversation starters, signs to look for, and simple practices to do together as a family.

For each value, you will find:

  • Value at a Glance — What the value means, in plain language
  • The Verse — The scriptural foundation, in your language
  • Stories Your Child Heard — Brief summaries so you can ask about them
  • The Sanskrit Word — Ask your child to teach it to you!
  • Home Practice — The same action your child was given at school
  • Dinner Table Questions — 3–4 conversation starters for family discussion
  • Signs of Growth — Observable behaviours showing your child is internalizing the value

The most powerful teaching happens not in the classroom but at the dinner table.

Asteya

Asteya means we do not take what is not ours. We ASK first. If something belongs to someone else, we say, 'May I please borrow this?' We return it the same way we got it. Honest hands feel light and happy inside.

The Verse

Yoga Sutras 2.30: Do not hurt. Speak the truth. Do not take what is not yours. Be pure in heart. Do not grab and hoard. These are the five gentle rules.

What Asteya is NOT

Asteya is not slipping something into our pocket when no one is looking. It is not keeping what we found without trying to return it. It is not taking the biggest piece of cake before anyone else, or copying a friend's answer.

  • Slipping a friend's eraser into your bag because you like it
  • Finding a shiny coin on the floor and keeping it without asking who lost it
  • Copying a classmate's homework and calling it your own

Stories Your Child Heard

Hanuman Brings Back the Ring — Long ago, Lord Rama had a little helper named Hanuman. Hanuman was strong like a mountain, and his heart was soft like a flower. One day, Mother Sita was taken far away to the island of Lanka. Rama was very sad. He called Hanuman and said, 'Please go find Sita. Take this ring from my finger. When she sees it, she will know you are my friend.'

Meera and the Found Wallet — Meera was walking home through the little park. The neem leaves were dancing. A koel was singing on the branch. As she passed the red bench, something caught her eye.

The Sanskrit Word

asteya (अस्तेय) — ah-STAY-ya

Meaning: Not-stealing. Asking before we take. Returning what we borrow. Honest hands and an honest heart.

Ask your child to teach you this word!

Home Practice

For one week, do the Honest Hands Diary. Every day at school and at home, practice ASKING before you borrow anything — a crayon, a toy, a snack, a hug. At bedtime, share with Amma or Appa one time you asked, and one time you returned something. If you find something that is not yours, hand it to a grown-up right away. Also: every night before sleep, place your hands at your heart and whisper, 'Krishna, my hands were honest today.'

Duration: Every day for one week
Look for: By the end of the week, the child can describe at least 3 times they asked before taking, and at least 2 times they returned something that was borrowed. They can also name one thing that 'belongs to everyone' and should be shared.

Signs of Growth

By the end of the week, the child can describe at least 3 times they asked before taking, and at least 2 times they returned something that was borrowed. They can also name one thing that 'belongs to everyone' and should be shared.

When we stop grabbing and learn to ask, our hearts become happy with what we already have — and that happy feeling is called santosha.
