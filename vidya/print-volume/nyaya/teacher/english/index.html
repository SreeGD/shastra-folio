About This Book

This book is part of the Vidyā Karaṇa (विद्या करण) Value Education Series, designed for the Primary (Ages 6-9) age group.

Each chapter explores one of the values from the daivī sampad (divine qualities) described in the Bhagavad Gītā verses 16.1–3. The values are presented in a carefully sequenced order, where foundational values are taught before those that build upon them.

This Teacher Guide is designed to be used alongside the Student Book. It does not repeat the stories or activities — instead, it equips you to deliver each chapter with confidence.

For each value, you will find:

  • Chapter Overview — Value summary, verse reference, core principles, and MI targets at a glance
  • Five-Minute Introduction — An opening hook to start the lesson
  • 40-Minute Lesson Plan — Ready-to-use timing for 40-minute and 2-hour sessions
  • Skit Script — A classroom drama bringing the value to life
  • Role-Play Scenarios — Age-appropriate situations for practice
  • Discussion Guide — Questions with sample responses and facilitation tips
  • Quiz Bank + Answer Key — 10 MCQs with explanations for each chapter
  • Assessment Tools — Rubrics, pre/post surveys, portfolio prompts, and observation logs
  • Common Misconceptions — What children often get wrong and how to address it

The LLM is the karaṇa (instrument); you are the teacher. Trust your instincts.

न्याय
Nyaya

Nyaya means being fair.

Chapter Overview

Nyaya means being fair. Being fair means giving each person what is right for them — not the same for all, but what each one needs. A big brother may need a bigger plate; a baby may need a softer blanket. Nyaya listens to both sides. Nyaya speaks up when something is unfair. Nyaya gives everyone a chance.

The Verse: Bhagavad-gita 2.38 — Krishna tells Arjuna: make your heart the SAME in happy and sad, getting and not getting, winning and losing. Then stand up for what is right. If you do it this way, you will never do wrong.

Core Teaching

  1. Nyaya means being fair. Fair is not always equal. Fair is giving each person what is right for them — the one with nothing gets more, the one with much gives a little. Krishna's heart is a fair heart.
  2. Before we decide anything, we listen to BOTH sides. We do not take a side just because it is our friend's side. We ask, 'What really happened?' — then we speak.
  3. When something unfair happens in front of us — a bigger child hurts a smaller one, a friend is blamed wrongly — nyaya asks us to speak up kindly. Quiet when wrong is happening is not nyaya.

Five-Minute Introduction

Begin with a circle. Bring a small balance toy (or two open palms as a scale). Place a small coin in one palm. Ask: 'Is this scale balanced?' (No — one side goes down.) Place the same coin in the OTHER palm. 'Now?' (Yes — it's even.) Then ask a harder question: 'Imagine a hungry big brother on one side and a tiny baby on the other side. Should I give them the SAME amount of rice?' Let children think. Gently explain: 'A baby's tummy is small. A big brother's tummy is big. FAIR means giving each what they need — not the same for all.' Say: 'This is called nyaya. Nyaya is not giving everyone the same. Nyaya is giving each one what is RIGHT for them.' Chant 'nyā-ya, nyā-ya, nyā-ya' three times with palms-as-scale. Then ask: 'Has anyone ever felt something was unfair today?' Take 2-3 short answers.

40-Minute Lesson Plan

5 min — Opening circle + scale demo + chant nyā-ya. 10 min — Read aloud: Yudhishthira and the Thirsty Lake. Pause at the choice of Nakula and let children guess why. 8 min — Read aloud: Meera and the Three Pencils. Ask 'What would YOU have done?' 10 min — Worksheet Activity 1 (Fair/Not Fair) and Activity 2 (Fair Plate drawing). 5 min — Closing chant and introduce Fair Helper Week take-home. 2 min — Line up.

Two-Hour Lesson Plan

10 min — Opening circle with balance toy demo and chant 'nyaya' with palm-scale gesture. 15 min — Yudhishthira story with a small blue cloth 'lake' on the floor; four children mime falling asleep one by one; one child becomes Yudhishthira and answers a question. Slow down on the moment he asks for Nakula. 15 min — Meera's story; act out the three-pencils moment as a short skit with volunteers. 20 min — Worksheet Activity 1 (Fair/Not Fair circling) and Activity 2 (Fair Plate drawing). 20 min — Paired Two-Side Role-Play (Activity 4); rotate partners twice; every child practises listening to BOTH sides. 15 min — The Fair Scale Craft: each child makes a mini balance with paper cups, string, and a stick; on each cup write one thing they will 'weigh fairly' this week. 15 min — Circle Court: teacher poses three pretend classroom problems; children take turns as 'Fair Monitor' and listen to both mock sides before speaking. 10 min — Closing: each child names one fair thing they will do tomorrow. 5 min — Line up. Buffer: 0 min.

Discussion Guide

Primary children already have a strong 'That's not FAIR!' sense — use it. But gently widen it from 'same for all' to 'right for each.' The balance-scale metaphor is the strongest anchor; keep returning to it. Act out the Yudhishthira story with real drama — let children gasp at the choice of Nakula. Most primary hurts come from sharing (who got the biggest piece?) and judging (who started it?). Address both. Avoid abstract talk of 'justice'; keep it to concrete classroom moments — the pencil share, the turn at the swing, the blame for a spilled drink. Watch for the child who shouts 'That's not fair!' to get more for themselves — gently redirect: 'Let us check — was it unfair, or did someone just get what they needed today?' And watch for the quiet child who never asks for their share; help the class notice them. A fair teacher models listening to BOTH sides before deciding anything — this is the single most powerful lesson, because children watch. Never announce a judgment without first asking each child involved, 'Tell me what happened.' They will learn nyaya by being heard.

Common Misconceptions

  • Misconception: 'Fair means exactly the same for everyone.' Response: Not always. If your little sister gets a small plate and you get a big plate, that IS fair — because her tummy is small. Fair means each one gets what is RIGHT for them. A blanket for a cold baby, a bigger shoe for a bigger foot. Same for all is sometimes unfair.
  • Misconception: 'If my friend is in a fight, I must take my friend's side.' Response: That is loyalty, not fairness. A true friend is fair enough to say, 'Let me hear both sides.' Sometimes your friend was right. Sometimes they were wrong. A fair friend helps them see the truth — even their own mistakes. That is a bigger kind of love.
  • Misconception: 'If I see something unfair but it is not about me, I should stay quiet.' Response: When a bigger child pushes a smaller child, or a friend is blamed for something they did not do, speaking up is nyaya. We speak kindly, not angrily. We say: 'Please, this is not fair.' Or we tell a grown-up. Silence when wrong is happening becomes a tiny part of the wrong itself.
