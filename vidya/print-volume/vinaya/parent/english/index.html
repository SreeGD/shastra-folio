About This Book

This book is part of the Vidyā Karaṇa (विद्या करण) Value Education Series, designed for the Primary (Ages 6-9) age group.

Each chapter explores one of the values from the daivī sampad (divine qualities) described in the Bhagavad Gītā verses 16.1–3. The values are presented in a carefully sequenced order, where foundational values are taught before those that build upon them.

This Parent Book helps you reinforce at home what your child is learning at school. You do not need to read the full stories — your child will tell you! Instead, this book gives you conversation starters, signs to look for, and simple practices to do together as a family.

For each value, you will find:

  • Value at a Glance — What the value means, in plain language
  • The Verse — The scriptural foundation, in your language
  • Stories Your Child Heard — Brief summaries so you can ask about them
  • The Sanskrit Word — Ask your child to teach it to you!
  • Home Practice — The same action your child was given at school
  • Dinner Table Questions — 3–4 conversation starters for family discussion
  • Signs of Growth — Observable behaviours showing your child is internalizing the value

The most powerful teaching happens not in the classroom but at the dinner table.

Vinaya

Vinaya is the bow inside me. It is what makes my head go DOWN when I see my teacher, my grandmother, my guru, or Krishna. It is the quiet voice that says 'I have so much to learn. Please teach me.' When I do vinaya, I put my hands together. I bow low. I listen before I speak. I know I am small — not sad-small, just happy-small, like a little lamp waiting to be lit by a bigger lamp.

The Verse

Bhagavad-gita 13.8: Krishna says: First, my child, learn amānitvam — no-pride. Learn to bow your head before those who know more. Sit close to your teacher. That is where real learning begins. A head that bows is a head that can fill with wisdom.

What Vinaya is NOT

Vinaya is not being scared. I am not bowing because I am afraid — I am bowing because I KNOW my teacher has gifts for me. Vinaya is not pretending to be humble while secretly thinking I am the best. Vinaya is not feeling bad about myself. Krishna made me lovely. Vinaya only means I hold my head LOW when a bigger head is near — because a low cup catches more water than a cup held high.

  • Standing up very tall in front of teacher and saying 'I ALREADY KNOW THIS!' before she speaks
  • Interrupting Ajji when she is telling a story — to say my own story is better
  • Bowing at the altar while my heart is saying 'hurry up, I want to eat prasadam'
  • Talking back to teacher when she corrects my handwriting

Stories Your Child Heard

Satyakama Walks to Gautama and Bows — Long, long ago, in a simple mud house at the edge of a forest, there lived a little boy named Satyakama. He was only eight years old. He had bright black eyes and a head of curly hair. His mother's name was Jabala. She worked in the homes of rich people to earn their rice. One evening, Satyakama ran up to his mother. His cheeks were pink with a bright wish.

Rohan Learns the Right Way to Bow — Rohan was seven years old. He was in Standard Two. He lived with Amma, Papa, and his grandmother — Ajji. Every single morning, before breakfast, Rohan and Ajji stood in front of the little family altar. There was a small Krishna and Radha murti, a tiny Ganesha, and a photo of Ajji's guru. Ajji would ring a small bell — tinkle, tinkle — and then she would bow. Not just her head. Her WHOLE body. She would go down on her knees, bend forward, and touch her forehead to the floor in front of Krishna. One, two, three times.

The Sanskrit Word

vinaya (विनय) — vi-NAH-ya

Meaning: The bow inside. The humility before teachers and elders. The listening-first heart. The head that goes down so that wisdom can pour in. Krishna lists it as the FIRST gift on the path of real knowledge.

Ask your child to teach you this word!

Home Practice

For one week, keep your Morning Bow Book by the family altar. Every morning before breakfast, stand in front of Krishna (or the family deity). Ring a small bell if there is one — tinkle, tinkle. Then bow. If you can, go all the way down — knees, forehead to the floor, like Satyakama did. If you cannot, just bow your head with folded hands. Do this three times. Then draw ONE tiny folded-hands namaste in your book. Under it, write or whisper ONE name — the name of ONE elder (Ajji, Amma, Papa, teacher, guru-ji) you will bow to today. At bedtime, tick it if you bowed. Before sleeping, whisper: 'Krishna, my head bowed today. Thank You for filling my cup.'

Duration: Every day for one week
Look for: By the end of the week, the child can describe at least 3 mornings when they did the full morning bow at the altar, and at least 3 times they bowed before an elder during the day (touching feet, folded hands, or soft 'please show me' when corrected). They can demonstrate the dandavat (log-bow) position on the floor while softly saying 'vinaya.' The book has at least 5 little namastes drawn.

Signs of Growth

By the end of the week, the child can describe at least 3 mornings when they did the full morning bow at the altar, and at least 3 times they bowed before an elder during the day (touching feet, folded hands, or soft 'please show me' when corrected). They can demonstrate the dandavat (log-bow) position on the floor while softly saying 'vinaya.' The book has at least 5 little namastes drawn.

When the bow inside vinaya grows into a steady warm flame of love for the teacher who guides us, that blooming devotion has its own beautiful name: guru-bhakti.
