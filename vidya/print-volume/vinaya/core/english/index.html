About This Book

This book is part of the Vidyā Karaṇa (विद्या करण) Value Education Series, designed for the Primary (Ages 6-9) age group.

Each chapter explores one of the values from the daivī sampad (divine qualities) described in the Bhagavad Gītā verses 16.1–3. The values are presented in a carefully sequenced order, where foundational values are taught before those that build upon them.

This is the reading volume. The companion Activity Workbook contains the doing material — worksheets, crafts, games, and home practice. Read this book first, do the activities second.

Each chapter contains:

  • Verse Anchor — The scriptural foundation in Sanskrit and English
  • Srimad Bhagavatam Verse — A second scriptural anchor
  • What is — What the value is, and what it is NOT
  • Sanskrit Word Study — The Sanskrit term, its meaning, and use
  • Story from the Scriptures — From the scriptures, from history, and from modern life
  • Core Teaching — Three principles to internalize
  • Language Practice — Vocabulary and reading comprehension

Verse Anchor

Bhagavad-gita 13.8

अमानित्वमदम्भित्वमहिंसा क्षान्तिरार्जवम्।
आचार्योपासनं शौचं स्थैर्यमात्मविनिग्रहः॥

amānitvam adambhitvam ahiṁsā kṣāntir ārjavam ācāryopāsanaṁ śaucaṁ sthairyam ātma-vinigrahaḥ

Word-for-word: amānitvam — no-pride, the bowed head; adambhitvam — no show-off; ahiṁsā — no hurting; kṣāntiḥ — patience; ārjavam — straight-and-simple; ācārya-upāsanam — sitting close to the teacher; śaucam — cleanness; sthairyam — steadiness; ātma-vinigrahaḥ — holding the little me gently.

Translation: Krishna says: First, my child, learn amānitvam — no-pride. Learn to bow your head before those who know more. Sit close to your teacher. That is where real learning begins. A head that bows is a head that can fill with wisdom.

Srimad Bhagavatam Verse

SB 11.17.27

आचार्यं मां विजानीयान्नावमन्येत कर्हिचित्।
न मर्त्यबुद्ध्यासूयेत सर्वदेवमयो गुरुः॥

ācāryaṁ māṁ vijānīyān nāvamanyeta karhicit na martya-buddhyāsūyeta sarva-deva-mayo guruḥ

Translation: Krishna tells Uddhava: 'Children, see your teacher as My own form. Never, never disrespect the teacher — not even in your thoughts. Do not think 'teacher is only a person like me.' Inside the teacher, ALL the devas are sitting. When you bow to teacher, you bow to Me.'

What is Vinaya?

Vinaya is the bow inside me. It is what makes my head go DOWN when I see my teacher, my grandmother, my guru, or Krishna. It is the quiet voice that says 'I have so much to learn. Please teach me.' When I do vinaya, I put my hands together. I bow low. I listen before I speak. I know I am small — not sad-small, just happy-small, like a little lamp waiting to be lit by a bigger lamp.

What it is NOT

Vinaya is not being scared. I am not bowing because I am afraid — I am bowing because I KNOW my teacher has gifts for me. Vinaya is not pretending to be humble while secretly thinking I am the best. Vinaya is not feeling bad about myself. Krishna made me lovely. Vinaya only means I hold my head LOW when a bigger head is near — because a low cup catches more water than a cup held high.

  • Standing up very tall in front of teacher and saying 'I ALREADY KNOW THIS!' before she speaks
  • Interrupting Ajji when she is telling a story — to say my own story is better
  • Bowing at the altar while my heart is saying 'hurry up, I want to eat prasadam'
  • Talking back to teacher when she corrects my handwriting

Sanskrit Word Study

vinaya (विनय)

Pronunciation: vi-NAH-ya

Meaning: The bow inside. The humility before teachers and elders. The listening-first heart. The head that goes down so that wisdom can pour in. Krishna lists it as the FIRST gift on the path of real knowledge.

Example: When Satyakama lay flat before Gautama Rishi and told the truth, that was vinaya.

Story from the Scriptures

Satyakama Walks to Gautama and Bows — Chandogya Upanishad, Chapter 4 (retold for children)

Long, long ago, in a simple mud house at the edge of a forest, there lived a little boy named Satyakama. He was only eight years old. He had bright black eyes and a head of curly hair. His mother's name was Jabala. She worked in the homes of rich people to earn their rice.

One evening, Satyakama ran up to his mother. His cheeks were pink with a bright wish.

'Amma! Amma! I want to go to the gurukul. I want to learn about BRAHMAN — the big Self of everything. I want to bow to a rishi and sit at his feet and learn.'

Jabala smiled very softly. 'My son, that is a beautiful wish. But when you go, the rishi will ask — 'whose son are you?' What will you say?'

Satyakama's face fell. 'Amma... whose son AM I?'

Jabala put her hand on his head. 'My child, I do not know. I was very young. I worked in many houses. Your father's name — I cannot tell you. But you must go. Go to the great rishi Gautama in the forest. When he asks, tell him the TRUTH. Say — 'I am Satyakama. My mother is Jabala. That is all I know.' Do not hide. Do not make up a story. Just bow — and tell the truth.'

Satyakama hugged his mother. The next morning before sunrise, he left.

He walked. And walked. And walked. Through the village. Across the river. Into the deep forest. His little feet got dusty. His tummy got hungry. But his heart was SHINING — because he was going to learn.

Finally, he saw a small ashram under a big banyan tree. Rishi Gautama sat there with some older students. Gautama had a long white beard and very kind eyes.

Satyakama's heart began to thump-thump-thump. What if the rishi sent him away? What if the older boys laughed at him?

But he remembered his mother's words. Bow. Tell the truth.

Satyakama walked up. He put his little bag down. He put his hands together — namaste. And then he did something very beautiful. He lay FLAT on the ground, face down, with his forehead touching the earth in front of Gautama's feet. This is called dandavat — the log-bow — the deepest bow of all.

He said, very softly — 'Great Rishi. I have come to learn. Please take me as your student.'

Gautama smiled. 'Get up, child. What is your name?'

'Satyakama, Guruji.'

'And whose son are you, my boy?'

Satyakama's eyes filled with tears. But he did not hide. He did not lie. He bowed his head again and whispered, 'Guruji... I asked my mother the same question. She said she does not know. She worked in many houses when she was young. She is only Jabala. I am only Jabala's son, Satyakama. That is all I know.'

A great silence fell in the ashram.

The older boys gasped. One whispered — 'He will be thrown out. No gurukul takes a boy with no father's name.'

But Gautama's eyes filled with tears too — not angry tears — shining tears. He got up from his seat. He lifted little Satyakama by both shoulders.

'My son,' Gautama said, and his voice was warm as sunshine, 'a boy who bows so deeply and tells the truth so bravely is ALREADY a brahmana. The truth is a bigger family than any name. Come. Come and sit at my feet. I will teach you about Brahman. I will teach you EVERYTHING.'

Satyakama cried and laughed at the same time. He sat down at Gautama's feet. The rishi placed his kind hand on the boy's head.

Years later, Satyakama became one of the greatest sages of the land. His knowledge of Brahman was so deep that even rishis came to learn from him. But every single day of his long life, he remembered — 'It all began when I walked into the forest, lay flat on the ground, and bowed.'

That bow had a name. Vinaya.

A Story from Today

Rohan Learns the Right Way to Bow

Rohan was seven years old. He was in Standard Two. He lived with Amma, Papa, and his grandmother — Ajji.

Every single morning, before breakfast, Rohan and Ajji stood in front of the little family altar. There was a small Krishna and Radha murti, a tiny Ganesha, and a photo of Ajji's guru. Ajji would ring a small bell — tinkle, tinkle — and then she would bow. Not just her head. Her WHOLE body. She would go down on her knees, bend forward, and touch her forehead to the floor in front of Krishna. One, two, three times.

'Rohan-beta,' Ajji always said, 'the head that bows is the head that fills with light. This is vinaya. A little bow every morning — and we become a big cup ready for Krishna to pour into.'

Rohan loved it. Some mornings he went all the way to the floor. Some mornings he was in a hurry and just bowed his head. But he ALWAYS bowed.

Now — at school, Rohan was a clever boy. He was quick with numbers. His handwriting was NEAT. His teacher was Asha-ma'am, a lady with a soft voice and a kind face.

One Friday afternoon, Asha-ma'am was walking around the class checking everyone's maths workbooks. She stopped at Rohan's desk. She read his page. She tapped one sum gently with her finger.

'Rohan, this sum is not right. Look — 23 plus 18 is not 31. Let us try it together.'

Rohan's face went HOT. His ears went red. His chin went UP. He opened his mouth — 'But Asha-ma'am, I KNOW —'

And then... something happened inside him.

He suddenly saw Ajji's face. He saw Krishna on the little altar. He heard the tinkle-tinkle of the morning bell. He felt his own forehead touching the cool floor.

His chin came DOWN. Slowly. His breath went in — one slow breath.

He put his hands together on his desk — namaste-style.

'Sorry, Asha-ma'am,' he said in a small, soft voice. 'Please show me.'

Asha-ma'am's face went SOFT. She knelt down beside his desk. She took his pencil. 'Look, kanna. 23 plus 18. First we add the ones — 3 plus 8 is 11. We write 1 and carry 1. Then 2 plus 1 plus 1 is 4. So 23 plus 18 is 41. Not 31.'

'Oh!' said Rohan. 'I missed the carry-1!'

'Yes,' said Asha-ma'am, smiling. 'And Rohan — thank you for listening to me. That was beautiful. Some children fight when they are corrected. You BOWED. That is vinaya.'

The funniest thing happened then — Rohan's heart suddenly felt LIGHTER. Like a little bag of worries had fallen off his shoulders. The sum was wrong — so WHAT? Now he knew the right way. His head had bowed — and wisdom had come IN.

That evening, at the altar, Rohan bowed with his whole body. Head to floor. Three times, like Ajji. Then he sat up and whispered — 'Krishna, today I bowed at school too. My chin wanted to go UP. But I brought it DOWN. Then Asha-ma'am showed me how to add. Thank You, Krishna.'

Ajji peeked in and saw him. Her old eyes went shiny.

'Rohan-beta,' she said softly, 'today you did not just bow your body. You bowed your HEART. That is the real vinaya.'

Rohan smiled. He touched the floor one more time.

That bow had a name. Vinaya.

Core Teaching

  1. Vinaya is the bow inside me. When I see my teacher, my grandmother, my guru, or Krishna — something in me goes DOWN. My head bows. My hands come together. My voice goes soft. I am not scared — I am RECEIVING. A head that bows is a head that fills with wisdom. A cup held low catches more water than a cup held high.
  2. Vinaya is the listening-first heart. When teacher corrects me, I do not say 'I ALREADY KNOW.' I say 'Please show me.' When Ajji tells a story, I do not interrupt with my own. I listen. I breathe. I wait. When Krishna speaks to my heart in the quiet, I listen before I speak. Krishna says — amānitvam — no-pride — this is where real learning begins.
  3. Vinaya is NOT feeling bad about myself. Krishna made me lovely. Vinaya is not hiding behind a wall of false shyness. Vinaya only means I know — truly, deeply — 'I have SO much to learn.' My teacher knows more than me. My grandmother has lived longer than me. My guru has walked the path before me. Krishna is the Big Teacher of ALL. Before them, my head goes DOWN. And that is where I grow TALL.
