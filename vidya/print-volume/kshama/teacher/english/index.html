About This Book

This book is part of the Vidyā Karaṇa (विद्या करण) Value Education Series, designed for the Primary (Ages 6-9) age group.

Each chapter explores one of the values from the daivī sampad (divine qualities) described in the Bhagavad Gītā verses 16.1–3. The values are presented in a carefully sequenced order, where foundational values are taught before those that build upon them.

This Teacher Guide is designed to be used alongside the Student Book. It does not repeat the stories or activities — instead, it equips you to deliver each chapter with confidence.

For each value, you will find:

  • Chapter Overview — Value summary, verse reference, core principles, and MI targets at a glance
  • Five-Minute Introduction — An opening hook to start the lesson
  • 40-Minute Lesson Plan — Ready-to-use timing for 40-minute and 2-hour sessions
  • Skit Script — A classroom drama bringing the value to life
  • Role-Play Scenarios — Age-appropriate situations for practice
  • Discussion Guide — Questions with sample responses and facilitation tips
  • Quiz Bank + Answer Key — 10 MCQs with explanations for each chapter
  • Assessment Tools — Rubrics, pre/post surveys, portfolio prompts, and observation logs
  • Common Misconceptions — What children often get wrong and how to address it

The LLM is the karaṇa (instrument); you are the teacher. Trust your instincts.

क्षमा
Kshama

Kshama means forgiveness.

Chapter Overview

Kshama means forgiveness. When someone hurts me — with words, with hands, with a broken promise — I do not hold the hurt tight inside my heart like a burning stone. I let it go. Forgiving does not mean saying 'it was okay' or that they can do it again. It means freeing MY OWN heart from the sting. The sting is not the gift Krishna wants me to keep. A clean, soft heart is.

The Verse: Bhagavad-gita 16.3 — Krishna tells Arjuna: shining courage, forgiveness that lets go of hurt, steady holding on, a clean heart, no wish to hurt back, and no puffed-up pride — these beautiful qualities shine in the child who is born for goodness.

Core Teaching

  1. Kshama means forgiveness — letting go of the hurt that lives inside my own heart. When someone hurts me, I do not have to keep the hot stone. I can put it down. My heart is happier without it.
  2. Forgiving is NOT saying 'it was okay.' It is not letting someone hurt me again and again. Forgiving just means my own heart is free. The hurt stays outside; Krishna stays inside.
  3. Kshama is strong, not weak. Little Prahlada forgave a father who had hurt him again and again. That tiny boy had a bigger heart than a giant king. When we forgive, we become just a tiny bit like Prahlada — and Krishna smiles.

Five-Minute Introduction

Bring a small smooth pebble to class. Hold it tight in your fist and ask the children, 'If I squeeze this stone really hard for a whole day — whose hand gets tired? Whose palm gets red?' Let a few children answer. Then say: 'When someone hurts us — calls a name, breaks our toy, says something mean — our little heart grabs the hurt like a hot stone. We think squeezing it makes us strong. But whose heart is burning? OURS.' Now open your palm and show the stone resting quietly. 'In our tradition, there is a beautiful Sanskrit word for setting the hot stone down. Kshama. It does NOT mean the hurt was okay. It means my own heart will not stay on fire.' Chant 'ksha-MAA' three times with fists opening slowly on each chant. Ask: 'Who here has ever held a hot stone in their heart?' Take 2-3 quick honest answers. Bless them all.

40-Minute Lesson Plan

5 min — Opening: pebble demonstration, fist-open chant 'ksha-MAA' three times. 10 min — Read aloud: Little Prahlada Forgives His Father. Pause on the moment Prahlada folds his hands and asks Narasimha to forgive his father. Let the silence land. 8 min — Read aloud: Anya and the Broken Elephant. Pause at 'Whose hand is burning right now?' — invite children to open their own fists with you. 10 min — Worksheet Activity 1 (Hot Stone or Soft Heart) and Activity 3 (chant with fist-opening). 5 min — Closing: each child places a hand on their chest and whispers 'My heart is clean.' 2 min — Take-Home instruction.

Two-Hour Lesson Plan

10 min — Opening circle with pebble demonstration and chant 'kshama' with fist-opening gesture. 15 min — Prahlada story with a small cloth Krishna/Narasimha figure; let one child be Prahlada and fold his hands to request forgiveness for his father. Slow down on that moment. 15 min — Anya's story; paired role-play of the tulsi-plant conversation between Amma and Anya. 20 min — Let-Go Stone Craft (Activity 2); each child walks outside to place their stone in soil or water — this is the ritual heart of the class. 15 min — Turn-Around Role Play (Activity 4) paired; rotate partners once. 15 min — Bhajan time: sing 'Kshama Kariye' or any simple bhajan about Krishna's forgiveness, with fist-opening gestures. 15 min — Talking circle: 'What is one hot stone you want to put down this week?' (Children may pass — honor silence). 10 min — Quiet drawing: the 'kshama garden' — each child imagines the tiny flower they will plant tonight. 5 min — Closing chant and take-home instruction. Buffer: 0 min.

Discussion Guide

Primary children understand kshama best through the BODY — squeezing a fist, opening a fist. The whole lesson can be taught with fists and breaths. Never force a child to say 'I forgive' in public; that only hides anger without releasing it. Instead, give them the private 'three breaths + kshama' ritual they can do quietly. Honor all feelings first: 'Yes, that hurt. Of course you feel angry. Anger is a normal feeling — it just is not a good roommate if it stays too long.' Watch for the child who hides hurt ('I don't care') and the child who nurses hurt (brings up old stories over and over). Both need kshama in different ways. Use the Prahlada story carefully — never suggest that children should silently tolerate abuse. Make clear: Prahlada kept chanting AND Krishna came to protect him. Forgiveness of the heart is separate from safety of the body. For primary children, the most common hurts are accidents (broken toys, spills) and small meannesses (name-calling). Start there. Build outward.

Common Misconceptions

  • Misconception: 'If I forgive, I am saying what they did was okay.' Response: Kshama never says 'it was okay.' It only says 'my heart will not stay hurt.' You can forgive AND tell a grown-up. You can forgive AND set a limit ('please don't push me again'). You can forgive AND stay away from someone unsafe. Kshama is about YOUR heart being free — it is not a permission slip for the other person.
  • Misconception: 'Forgiving is for weak people. Strong people hit back.' Response: Think of tiny five-year-old Prahlada. His father hurt him again and again. Yet when Krishna Himself asked what he wanted, Prahlada asked for his father's forgiveness. That tiny boy had the BIGGEST heart in the whole kingdom. Hitting back is easy — any small heart can do it. Letting go is strong — only a Krishna-heart can do it.
  • Misconception: 'If I stop feeling angry right away, that means I really forgave.' Response: Anger can take a little while to cool — like hot chai. Kshama is not about never feeling the hurt; it is about not holding the hurt forever. Let children know: first the hurt comes, then three breaths, then talking to Amma or Appa, then 'kshama.' The stone does not have to drop the very first second — it just has to drop before bedtime.
