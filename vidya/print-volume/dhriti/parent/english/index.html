About This Book

This book is part of the Vidyā Karaṇa (विद्या करण) Value Education Series, designed for the Primary (Ages 6-9) age group.

Each chapter explores one of the values from the daivī sampad (divine qualities) described in the Bhagavad Gītā verses 16.1–3. The values are presented in a carefully sequenced order, where foundational values are taught before those that build upon them.

This Parent Book helps you reinforce at home what your child is learning at school. You do not need to read the full stories — your child will tell you! Instead, this book gives you conversation starters, signs to look for, and simple practices to do together as a family.

For each value, you will find:

  • Value at a Glance — What the value means, in plain language
  • The Verse — The scriptural foundation, in your language
  • Stories Your Child Heard — Brief summaries so you can ask about them
  • The Sanskrit Word — Ask your child to teach it to you!
  • Home Practice — The same action your child was given at school
  • Dinner Table Questions — 3–4 conversation starters for family discussion
  • Signs of Growth — Observable behaviours showing your child is internalizing the value

The most powerful teaching happens not in the classroom but at the dinner table.

Dhriti

Dhriti is the quiet hand that keeps holding. When I started something bright and happy, and then the middle got long and tired — dhriti is the hand inside me that says, 'Keep going, little one. Just one more line. Just one more day.' Dhriti does not shout. Dhriti does not run. Dhriti only STAYS. Krishna's own hand is inside my dhriti, helping me hold on.

The Verse

Bhagavad-gita 18.33: Krishna says: The steady hand that keeps holding — that holds the mind quiet, the breath calm, and the senses kind, without wandering — THAT steady hand is the good kind. Its name is dhṛti.

What Dhriti is NOT

Dhriti is not stubbornness — holding on to a bad habit just because I started. It is not pretending I am not tired. It is not pushing myself until I cry. True dhriti is gentle and steady, like a quiet lamp that keeps shining — not like a firework that bursts bright and dies. It stays because it is right, not because 'I must win.'

  • Starting the colouring with joy on page 1 and throwing it away on page 2 because it got boring
  • Practising my song only on the days I feel like it, and skipping the hard days
  • Giving up on the long maths problem after one wrong answer
  • Saying 'I already tried!' after trying only once

Stories Your Child Heard

Bharata Holds the Sandals for Fourteen Long Years — Long ago in Ayodhya, four brothers loved each other dearly — Rama, Lakshmana, Bharata, and Shatrughna. One sad day, a big mistake sent Lord Rama to the forest for fourteen years. Bharata was far away. When he heard, his heart broke like a clay pot.

Kavya and the Thirty-Day Sitar — Kavya was eight. One Sunday she heard a sitar concert and fell in LOVE. She ran home. 'Amma, Appa — I want to learn!' Appa thought. 'Kavya-beta, dhriti first. Will you promise thirty mornings — even on hard days?'

The Sanskrit Word

dhṛti (धृति) — DHRI-ti (aspirated 'dh', short 'i')

Meaning: The quiet hand that keeps holding. Staying steady when the middle gets long and tired. Not sparkle, not shouting — just gentle, patient, Krishna-filled holding-on.

Ask your child to teach you this word!

Home Practice

For thirty mornings, pick ONE small kind practice and keep it. Good choices: water the tulsi, make your own bed, draw one page, read for ten minutes, sing one bhajan line, do one Surya Namaskar, tidy one shelf. Use the thirty-tick card from class. On tired days do only five minutes — still tick. Before bed, tell Amma or Appa: 'Today my dhriti held / wobbled / fell — and tomorrow it will hold again.' Place one small object on your home altar (the card, a flower, a flashcard) and bow each morning, whispering: 'Krishna, my quiet hand will hold today.'

Duration: Every day for thirty days
Look for: By the end of thirty days, the child has ticked at least 25 squares on the card (with honesty about wobbles), can describe at least 3 tired days when they held on with five small minutes, and can recite the Dhriti chant with open palms. Parents notice the child returning to unfinished work without being pushed.

Signs of Growth

By the end of thirty days, the child has ticked at least 25 squares on the card (with honesty about wobbles), can describe at least 3 tired days when they held on with five small minutes, and can recite the Dhriti chant with open palms. Parents notice the child returning to unfinished work without being pushed.

When our quiet hand has held on through all the long days, something deep and shining begins to grow inside — a slow fire that burns off laziness and grows bright strength. That slow, bright fire has its own beautiful name: tapas.
