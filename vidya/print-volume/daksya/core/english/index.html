About This Book

This book is part of the Vidyā Karaṇa (विद्या करण) Value Education Series, designed for the Primary (Ages 6-9) age group.

Each chapter explores one of the values from the daivī sampad (divine qualities) described in the Bhagavad Gītā verses 16.1–3. The values are presented in a carefully sequenced order, where foundational values are taught before those that build upon them.

This is the reading volume. The companion Activity Workbook contains the doing material — worksheets, crafts, games, and home practice. Read this book first, do the activities second.

Each chapter contains:

  • Verse Anchor — The scriptural foundation in Sanskrit and English
  • Srimad Bhagavatam Verse — A second scriptural anchor
  • What is — What the value is, and what it is NOT
  • Sanskrit Word Study — The Sanskrit term, its meaning, and use
  • Story from the Scriptures — From the scriptures, from history, and from modern life
  • Core Teaching — Three principles to internalize
  • Language Practice — Vocabulary and reading comprehension

Daksya

Daksya means skillfulness — doing our work with CARE.

Verse Anchor

Bhagavad-gita 2.50

बुद्धियुक्तो जहातीह उभे सुकृतदुष्कृते।
तस्माद्योगाय युज्यस्व योगः कर्मसु कौशलम्॥

buddhi-yukto jahātīha ubhe sukṛta-duṣkṛte tasmād yogāya yujyasva yogaḥ karmasu kauśalam

Word-for-word: buddhi-yuktaḥ — one with steady mind; jahāti — gives up; iha — in this life; ubhe — both; sukṛta-duṣkṛte — good and bad results; tasmāt — therefore; yogāya — for yoga; yujyasva — keep practising; yogaḥ — yoga; karmasu — in actions; kauśalam — skill.

Translation: Krishna says: When we keep our mind peaceful and connected to Him, we do our work beautifully — without worrying about the result. Yoga is skill in action. Doing things with care, for Krishna — that is true skill.

Srimad Bhagavatam Verse

SB 11.11.29

यथाऽर्चिषोऽग्नेः सवितुर्गभस्तयो
निर्यान्ति संयान्त्यसकृत्स्वरोचिषः।
तथा सत्त्वं सिदध्यति शिल्पकौशलम्॥

yathā 'rciṣo 'gneḥ savitur gabhastayo niryānti saṁyānty asakṛt sva-rociṣaḥ tathā sattvaṁ siddhyati śilpa-kauśalam

Translation: Krishna says: Just as sparks fly out from a fire and return, skills and crafts also come from practice and return as gifts. True skill (śilpa-kauśalam) is not born in one day — it grows through loving, careful practice, offered to the Lord.

What is Daksya?

Daksya means skillfulness — doing our work with CARE. When I do something, I take the time to do it right. I fold the clothes neatly. I write my letters clearly. I build the tower carefully. Skill grows from two things — love and practice. Krishna smiles when we do small things with a big heart.

What it is NOT

Daksya is not rushing through our work so we can go play. It is not doing things carelessly and saying 'good enough.' It is not being proud of our skills and showing off. True daksya is quiet, careful work — done because it is our offering to Krishna, not because we want praise.

  • Scribbling the homework in two minutes so I can watch cartoons
  • Stacking the shoes in a messy pile and walking away
  • Finishing the drawing quickly and saying 'I am BEST' instead of making it beautiful

Sanskrit Word Study

dakṣya (दक्ष्य)

Pronunciation: DAHK-shya

Meaning: Skillfulness. Doing our work with care. Taking time to do things rightly — not in a hurry, not in a mess. Skill that grows from love and practice.

Example: When Arav wrote his letters slowly and neatly for Krishna, that was daksya.

Story from the Scriptures

Little Abhimanyu Listens in the Dark — Mahabharata, Adi Parva — the tale of Abhimanyu's learning in the womb

Long ago, in the palace of the Pandavas, Arjuna sat with his wife Subhadra in the soft evening light. Subhadra was going to have a baby soon. Arjuna smiled at her and said, 'Let me tell you about a great battle-trick — the chakra-vyuha.'

The chakra-vyuha was like a huge spinning wheel made of soldiers. It had seven rings, one inside the other. Only a great warrior could break through. Arjuna began to explain.

'Ring one — you enter like this. Ring two — you turn like this. Ring three — you step just so...'

Subhadra listened with her head on a soft pillow. Arjuna's voice was deep and kind. Slowly, slowly, her eyes became heavy. By ring four, she was asleep.

But inside her belly, the tiny soul of her baby Abhimanyu was WIDE AWAKE. His little heart was listening with all its might. He did not want to miss one word.

Ring one — he listened. Ring two — he listened. Ring three, four, five, SIX — he listened with his whole self. He did not wiggle. He did not sleep. He was CAREFUL. He was attentive.

Then Arjuna noticed Subhadra's soft breathing. 'She is asleep,' he whispered, smiling. 'I will tell her the seventh ring another day.' He kissed her forehead and went out.

Baby Abhimanyu had learned only six. But those six — he had learned PERFECTLY. Every word. Every turn. Every step.

When Abhimanyu grew up, he became one of the most careful, most skillful young warriors in all the Pandava camp. He was quiet. He was kind. He practised his archery every morning with soft, steady care.

All his elders said, 'How does this young boy know so much? He moves like a dancer!'

Abhimanyu only smiled. He remembered the warm darkness, and his father's gentle voice, and how he had listened — with all his heart.

This is daksya. Skill that begins with careful, loving attention.

A Story from Today

Arav Writes Slowly

Arav was seven years old. He loved cricket more than almost anything. Every evening his friends gathered under the gulmohar tree with a red tennis ball.

One Monday after school, Arav rushed home. His handwriting notebook was open on the desk. He groaned.

'Ten lines of the letter A,' he muttered. 'And B. And C!'

He could hear the boys outside — 'Arav! Come!'

Arav grabbed his pencil. He scribbled. Scratch-scratch-scratch. The A went crooked. The B looked like a sleeping caterpillar. The C was more like a comma. He finished the whole page in four minutes. He dropped the pencil and ran to the door.

'Arav,' Amma called softly, 'one minute, beta. Come and see your page.'

Arav came back, sighing. Amma did not shout. She did not scold. She just put her hand on the page.

'Beta,' she said, 'look at this A. Is it standing up straight?'

Arav looked. The A was falling over like a tired old man.

'And this B?'

Arav looked. The B had no belly. Just two sad loops.

Amma smiled. 'Arav, Krishna lives in the smallest things too. When you write a nice round B, Krishna sees it. When you write a tired B — He sees that too. Will you try ONE line again, for Krishna?'

Arav's cheeks went pink. He sat down. He took a deep breath. He wrote ONE A — slowly, slowly — standing tall. Then one B — with a nice round belly. Then one C — a clean, open smile.

He looked up at Amma. She beamed.

'Arav, do you see? THAT is daksya. Doing work with CARE. Skill grows from love — not from hurry.'

Arav went back and rewrote the page. He took fifteen whole minutes. When he finished, the letters looked like little happy soldiers standing in a row. He had missed some cricket. But his heart felt warm and round, like his new B.

That evening, before bed, he showed the page to Krishna at the altar. 'For You,' he whispered. Then he went to sleep with a small, proud smile.

Core Teaching

  1. Daksya means doing our work with CARE — taking the time to do it right. Neat letters, careful folding, gentle building — these small things become a beautiful offering to Krishna.
  2. Skill grows from two things — LOVE and PRACTICE. Like Abhimanyu listening in the dark, we begin with loving attention. Like Arav writing ONE letter at a time, we grow with patient practice. Hurry makes skill small. Care makes skill strong.
  3. True daksya is not showing off. It is doing even the smallest work — like stacking shoes, or writing a B — with a quiet, full heart. Krishna sees every careful line. Yogah karmasu kausalam — work done with love IS yoga.
