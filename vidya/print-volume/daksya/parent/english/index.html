About This Book

This book is part of the Vidyā Karaṇa (विद्या करण) Value Education Series, designed for the Primary (Ages 6-9) age group.

Each chapter explores one of the values from the daivī sampad (divine qualities) described in the Bhagavad Gītā verses 16.1–3. The values are presented in a carefully sequenced order, where foundational values are taught before those that build upon them.

This Parent Book helps you reinforce at home what your child is learning at school. You do not need to read the full stories — your child will tell you! Instead, this book gives you conversation starters, signs to look for, and simple practices to do together as a family.

For each value, you will find:

  • Value at a Glance — What the value means, in plain language
  • The Verse — The scriptural foundation, in your language
  • Stories Your Child Heard — Brief summaries so you can ask about them
  • The Sanskrit Word — Ask your child to teach it to you!
  • Home Practice — The same action your child was given at school
  • Dinner Table Questions — 3–4 conversation starters for family discussion
  • Signs of Growth — Observable behaviours showing your child is internalizing the value

The most powerful teaching happens not in the classroom but at the dinner table.

Daksya

Daksya means skillfulness — doing our work with CARE. When I do something, I take the time to do it right. I fold the clothes neatly. I write my letters clearly. I build the tower carefully. Skill grows from two things — love and practice. Krishna smiles when we do small things with a big heart.

The Verse

Bhagavad-gita 2.50: Krishna says: When we keep our mind peaceful and connected to Him, we do our work beautifully — without worrying about the result. Yoga is skill in action. Doing things with care, for Krishna — that is true skill.

What Daksya is NOT

Daksya is not rushing through our work so we can go play. It is not doing things carelessly and saying 'good enough.' It is not being proud of our skills and showing off. True daksya is quiet, careful work — done because it is our offering to Krishna, not because we want praise.

  • Scribbling the homework in two minutes so I can watch cartoons
  • Stacking the shoes in a messy pile and walking away
  • Finishing the drawing quickly and saying 'I am BEST' instead of making it beautiful

Stories Your Child Heard

Little Abhimanyu Listens in the Dark — Long ago, in the palace of the Pandavas, Arjuna sat with his wife Subhadra in the soft evening light. Subhadra was going to have a baby soon. Arjuna smiled at her and said, 'Let me tell you about a great battle-trick — the chakra-vyuha.' The chakra-vyuha was like a huge spinning wheel made of soldiers. It had seven rings, one inside the other. Only a great warrior could break through. Arjuna began to explain.

Arav Writes Slowly — Arav was seven years old. He loved cricket more than almost anything. Every evening his friends gathered under the gulmohar tree with a red tennis ball. One Monday after school, Arav rushed home. His handwriting notebook was open on the desk. He groaned.

The Sanskrit Word

dakṣya (दक्ष्य) — DAHK-shya

Meaning: Skillfulness. Doing our work with care. Taking time to do things rightly — not in a hurry, not in a mess. Skill that grows from love and practice.

Ask your child to teach you this word!

Home Practice

For one week, keep a simple Careful-Work Diary. Pick ONE task each day — tidy your shoes, fold one shirt, water the tulsi plant, make your bed, colour one picture, wash one plate. Take one extra minute and do it with daksya. At bedtime, tell Amma or Appa: 'Today I did ____ with care.' Whisper to Krishna: 'I did this for You.' Notice the warm feeling inside after careful work — that feeling IS Krishna's smile.

Duration: Every day for one week
Look for: By the end of the week, the child can name at least 5 specific careful tasks done this week, shows slower hands during at least one routine activity (shoes, folding, writing), and smiles quietly — not boastfully — when their work is noticed.

Signs of Growth

By the end of the week, the child can name at least 5 specific careful tasks done this week, shows slower hands during at least one routine activity (shoes, folding, writing), and smiles quietly — not boastfully — when their work is noticed.

When our hands become careful and our work becomes an offering, a beautiful inner strength grows — the strength to keep practising even when things are hard. That strength has its own name: tapas.
