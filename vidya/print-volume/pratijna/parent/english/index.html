About This Book

This book is part of the Vidyā Karaṇa (विद्या करण) Value Education Series, designed for the Primary (Ages 6-9) age group.

Each chapter explores one of the values from the daivī sampad (divine qualities) described in the Bhagavad Gītā verses 16.1–3. The values are presented in a carefully sequenced order, where foundational values are taught before those that build upon them.

This Parent Book helps you reinforce at home what your child is learning at school. You do not need to read the full stories — your child will tell you! Instead, this book gives you conversation starters, signs to look for, and simple practices to do together as a family.

For each value, you will find:

  • Value at a Glance — What the value means, in plain language
  • The Verse — The scriptural foundation, in your language
  • Stories Your Child Heard — Brief summaries so you can ask about them
  • The Sanskrit Word — Ask your child to teach it to you!
  • Home Practice — The same action your child was given at school
  • Dinner Table Questions — 3–4 conversation starters for family discussion
  • Signs of Growth — Observable behaviours showing your child is internalizing the value

The most powerful teaching happens not in the classroom but at the dinner table.

Pratijna

Pratijna means a promise I truly keep. When I say I will do something, I do it — even when I don't feel like it. Even when it is hard. My word is my strength. A small promise kept makes my heart brave. A big promise kept makes my heart like a mountain.

The Verse

Bhagavad-gita 9.31: Krishna says: O Arjuna, declare this as a solemn promise — My devotee is never lost. A person who walks in dharma quickly becomes peaceful. You can take this as My vow.

What Pratijna is NOT

Pratijna is not making big promises to look important and then forgetting them. It is not saying 'I promise!' when we already know we will not do it. It is not breaking our word the moment it becomes hard. A promise spoken but not kept is like a flower with no smell — pretty to look at, but empty inside.

  • Saying 'I promise I will clean my room!' and then watching TV instead
  • Telling your friend 'I will share my tiffin tomorrow' and then eating it all alone
  • Promising Amma you will brush your teeth, then skipping it when she is not looking

Stories Your Child Heard

Devavrata Becomes Bhishma — The Boy Who Made a Terrible Promise — Long ago, a young prince named Devavrata lived in the great kingdom of Hastinapur. His father was King Shantanu. His mother was the sacred river Ganga herself. Devavrata was strong, kind, and loved by everyone. The whole kingdom said, 'One day he will be our king.' But one evening, Devavrata saw his father sitting sadly by the river.

Arjun and the Rainy-Day Promise — It was Saturday evening. Arjun was eight years old. His little sister Diya was turning five on Sunday. Diya tugged his sleeve. 'Bhaiya, will you come with me to the temple tomorrow morning? Before school? Just you and me. I want to put my birthday flowers at Krishna's feet.'

The Sanskrit Word

pratijñā (प्रतिज्ञा) — pra-TIG-nyaa

Meaning: A promise I truly keep. A solemn word. A vow. When I say it, I do it — even when it is hard.

Ask your child to teach you this word!

Home Practice

For one week, wear your Promise Ribbon. Every morning, make ONE small promise to yourself or your family — 'Today I will water the tulsi.' 'Today I will greet Dadi kindly.' 'Today I will finish my homework before play.' Touch the ribbon and whisper 'pratijna'. At bedtime, tell Amma or Appa which promise you kept today. If you forgot, say 'Tomorrow I will try again.' On Sunday, count how many promises you kept — and whisper to Krishna, 'My word is Yours.'

Duration: Every day for one week
Look for: By the end of the week, the child can name at least 3 specific small promises they kept. They begin to pause before promising — saying 'Let me think first' — and can demonstrate the hand-on-heart pratijna gesture with quiet strength.

Signs of Growth

By the end of the week, the child can name at least 3 specific small promises they kept. They begin to pause before promising — saying 'Let me think first' — and can demonstrate the hand-on-heart pratijna gesture with quiet strength.

When we keep our promises steadily, day after day, a new inner strength awakens — a firm, quiet holding power called dhriti.
