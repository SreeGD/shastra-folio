About This Book

This book is part of the Vidyā Karaṇa (विद्या करण) Value Education Series, designed for the Primary (Ages 6-9) age group.

Each chapter explores one of the values from the daivī sampad (divine qualities) described in the Bhagavad Gītā verses 16.1–3. The values are presented in a carefully sequenced order, where foundational values are taught before those that build upon them.

This Teacher Guide is designed to be used alongside the Student Book. It does not repeat the stories or activities — instead, it equips you to deliver each chapter with confidence.

For each value, you will find:

  • Chapter Overview — Value summary, verse reference, core principles, and MI targets at a glance
  • Five-Minute Introduction — An opening hook to start the lesson
  • 40-Minute Lesson Plan — Ready-to-use timing for 40-minute and 2-hour sessions
  • Skit Script — A classroom drama bringing the value to life
  • Role-Play Scenarios — Age-appropriate situations for practice
  • Discussion Guide — Questions with sample responses and facilitation tips
  • Quiz Bank + Answer Key — 10 MCQs with explanations for each chapter
  • Assessment Tools — Rubrics, pre/post surveys, portfolio prompts, and observation logs
  • Common Misconceptions — What children often get wrong and how to address it

The LLM is the karaṇa (instrument); you are the teacher. Trust your instincts.

CHAPTER 1
ह्रीः
Hrih

Hrih is the soft quiet inside me.

Chapter Overview

Hrih is the soft quiet inside me. When I do something good, I don't SHOUT about it — I hold it like a little diya inside my chest, gently, so it keeps shining. When I make a mistake, I feel gentle-sorry — not hiding, not lying — just 'oh, I did that, I am sorry.' Hrih is the hush-hush voice, the little bow of the head, the pink cheeks when Amma praises me. Krishna's name for it is hrīḥ, and He loves this quality in a child.

The Verse: Bhagavad-gita 16.2 — Krishna names many pretty qualities of the godly heart — kindness, truth, gentleness — and right in the middle, He places hrīḥ. Hrīḥ is the soft quiet inside. It is the hush-hush smile when I do something good. It is the gentle-sorry feeling when I make a mistake. Krishna loves a child whose heart has hrīḥ.

Core Teaching

  1. Hrih is the soft quiet inside me. When I do something good, I do not SHOUT it from the rooftop. I hold it gently, like a little diya inside my chest. My cheeks go pink. My head gives a small bow. I can still be happy — but happy and gentle, not happy and loud. Krishna calls this hrīḥ, and He places it right in the middle of His list of godly qualities.
  2. Hrih is the gentle-sorry feeling when I make a mistake. I do not hide. I do not lie. I do not blame others. I just say 'I did that, and I am sorry.' My heart feels a little warm and pink — and that pink feeling is good. It is not scared-hiding. It is not shame that makes me small. It is a soft knowing — 'oh, I was not my best, let me be better tomorrow.' The pink is Krishna's gentle hand on my heart.
  3. Hrih is NOT feeling bad about who I am. Krishna made me lovely. My family loves me. My smile is beautiful. I can be strong and brave and clever — AND still have hrih. Hrih only means I hold my goodness gently and I hold my mistakes gently. Proud children break their arrows. Hrih-children fly straight and true — just like little Arjuna in Drona's ashram.

Five-Minute Introduction

Stand in a circle. Hold up a small clay diya (or a battery tea-light). Light it. Place it gently in the middle of the circle. Lower your voice to almost a whisper: 'Children, look. This little diya is so soft. It is not a firework. It does not go BOOM. It just... glows. Quietly. Warmly.' Pause. Touch your own chest. 'There is a diya like this INSIDE each of us. When we do something good, the diya glows. When we make a mistake, the diya goes a little pink — gentle-pink, not scared-pink. This soft-quiet-pink-inside has a name. Krishna says it in the Gita. The name is HRIH.' Have the children put one hand on their chest. Chant together, very softly — 'hrīḥ... hrīḥ... hrīḥ...' Ask: 'Has anyone ever felt their cheeks go pink when someone praised them? Has anyone ever felt gentle-sorry after a mistake?' Take 2-3 short answers. Celebrate each one with a soft smile — NEVER a big cheer (that would contradict the value).

40-Minute Lesson Plan

5 min — Diya intro with real diya, pink-cheek chant 'hrīḥ' 3x soft. 10 min — Read aloud Young Arjuna and Drona; children mime 'shoot, bow, pink cheeks.' 8 min — Read aloud Priya at the Art Show; pause at Neha's shouting moment — ask 'what would YOU have done?' 10 min — Worksheet Activity 1 (Shout or Soft) + Activity 2 (Draw Your Diya). 5 min — Pink-cheek closing chant + introduce Quiet Diya Diary take-home. 2 min — Soft line-up (no pushing).

Two-Hour Lesson Plan

10 min — Opening circle, light a real diya, pink-cheek chant. 15 min — Young Arjuna story with small paper bows; children practise 'shoot - bow - pink cheek - whisper thank you, Guruji.' 15 min — Priya at the Art Show; children mime Priya's walk to the teacher — slow, head bowed, ribbon held to chest. 20 min — Worksheet Activities 1 & 2. 15 min — Paired Gentle-Sorry Game (Activity 4) with rotated partners; debrief how the heart feels lighter after saying gentle-sorry than after scared-hiding. 15 min — Diya Craft: each child makes a small clay diya (or paper-plate diya with orange paper flame); they will take it home and light it once a week and whisper their quiet-good-thing and gentle-sorry into it. 10 min — Biographical story: Srinivasa Ramanujan writing quietly in his old notebook; children mime writing numbers in a tiny book. 10 min — Closing: children sit in the circle, the teacher asks 'Who did ONE good thing today that they will NOT brag about?' Children raise a finger (not a hand) silently. Each child takes home a Quiet Diya Diary. Buffer: 10 min.

Discussion Guide

Primary children learn hrih through the BODY and the VOICE. Model soft voice yourself. Model the bowed head. Model the pink cheek (yes, you can demonstrate it with your hand gesture). Distinguish hrih carefully from three confusions: (1) SHAME as a weapon ('you should be ashamed!') — this is dangerous, children freeze or rebel; hrih is never weaponized against the child. (2) Scared-hiding (the child who lies, hides the broken cup, blames the sister) — this is the OPPOSITE of hrih; gently teach 'gentle-sorry is safer than scared-hiding, because Krishna loves a truth-telling heart.' (3) Bragging ('I am the BEST! I WON!') — gently redirect, never humiliate: 'That's lovely, kanna — now whisper it softly to your diya.' Watch for two kinds of children. The Loud Child who always shouts achievements — do NOT shame them; teach them the SOFT alternative by celebrating their quietest moments. The Hiding Child who is scared of being seen at all — they may look like they have hrih, but they have fear; gently invite them forward, celebrate their courage, and trust the diya. Finally, hrih is the perfect value to teach in VOICE. Have a 'whisper hour' — for one hour, everyone speaks in soft voices, praises in whispers, says gentle-sorry softly. Children love the secret-intimate feel of it. The whole room's diya gets brighter when the noise goes down.

Common Misconceptions

  • Misconception: 'Hrih means I have to feel ashamed of myself all the time.' Response: NO. Hrih is NOT shame. Shame makes us feel bad about WHO we are. Hrih is only the soft pink feeling about WHAT I did — either a good thing held gently, or a mistake held with a gentle-sorry. Krishna made us lovely. We are always loved. Hrih does not erase our beauty; it only asks us to carry our goodness quietly, like a diya under the hand, so it does not blow out.
  • Misconception: 'If I have hrih I can never show anyone my good work.' Response: That is a misunderstanding. Priya still brought her drawing to the art show. She still let the judges see. Hrih does not mean we HIDE our gifts — it means we do not BRAG about them. When Ajji asks to see your drawing, show her. When teacher asks, show her. Just do not run around shouting 'LOOK AT ME!' The diya needs to be seen — it just does not need to be shouted about.
  • Misconception: 'Gentle-sorry means I have to cry and feel terrible.' Response: No, gentle-sorry is SOFT, not sad. It is a warm pink feeling that says 'oh, I did that, I am sorry, let me do better.' We are not drowning in tears. We are just being HONEST. Krishna loves honest hearts. Gentle-sorry actually makes us LIGHTER inside, not heavier. Try it — the next time you spill something, say 'I am gentle-sorry' and watch how the heart suddenly goes clean.
  • Misconception (for teacher/parent): 'Praising my child loudly will make them confident.' Response: Too much loud praise can teach a child to NEED it — to shout for their own validation. Instead, try quiet praise — a soft smile, a gentle touch on the head, 'Well done, kanna,' whispered. Teach by showing: YOU hold your own achievements quietly. YOU say gentle-sorry in front of them when you make a mistake. The diya is caught, not taught — and loud teachers grow loud children.
