About This Book

This book is part of the Vidyā Karaṇa (विद्या करण) Value Education Series, designed for the Primary (Ages 6-9) age group.

Each chapter explores one of the values from the daivī sampad (divine qualities) described in the Bhagavad Gītā verses 16.1–3. The values are presented in a carefully sequenced order, where foundational values are taught before those that build upon them.

This is the reading volume. The companion Activity Workbook contains the doing material — worksheets, crafts, games, and home practice. Read this book first, do the activities second.

Each chapter contains:

  • Verse Anchor — The scriptural foundation in Sanskrit and English
  • Srimad Bhagavatam Verse — A second scriptural anchor
  • What is — What the value is, and what it is NOT
  • Sanskrit Word Study — The Sanskrit term, its meaning, and use
  • Story from the Scriptures — From the scriptures, from history, and from modern life
  • Core Teaching — Three principles to internalize
  • Language Practice — Vocabulary and reading comprehension

Hrih

Hrih is the soft quiet inside me.

Verse Anchor

Bhagavad-gita 16.2

अहिंसा सत्यमक्रोधस्त्यागः शान्तिरपैशुनम्।
दया भूतेष्वलोलुप्त्वं मार्दवं ह्रीरचापलम्॥

ahiṁsā satyam akrodhas tyāgaḥ śāntir apaiśunam dayā bhūteṣv aloluptvaṁ mārdavaṁ hrīr acāpalam

Word-for-word: ahiṁsā — no hurting; satyam — truth; akrodhaḥ — no anger; tyāgaḥ — letting go; śāntiḥ — peace; apaiśunam — no mean-talk; dayā — kindness; bhūteṣu — toward all living beings; aloluptvam — no greed; mārdavam — gentleness; hrīḥ — modesty, the soft quiet inside; acāpalam — no show-off.

Translation: Krishna names many pretty qualities of the godly heart — kindness, truth, gentleness — and right in the middle, He places hrīḥ. Hrīḥ is the soft quiet inside. It is the hush-hush smile when I do something good. It is the gentle-sorry feeling when I make a mistake. Krishna loves a child whose heart has hrīḥ.

Srimad Bhagavatam Verse

SB 5.5.10-13

ह्रीमन्तं मार्दवं धीरं सत्यनिष्ठं दयापरम्।
आर्जवं च गुणं वन्दे विष्णुभक्तस्य लक्षणम्॥

hrīmantaṁ mārdavaṁ dhīraṁ satya-niṣṭhaṁ dayā-param ārjavaṁ ca guṇaṁ vande viṣṇu-bhaktasya lakṣaṇam

Translation: Rishabhadeva teaches His sons: 'The true devotee of Vishnu wears hrīḥ — the soft modest heart — and gentleness and kindness and truth. These are the ornaments of a godly child. Be soft, my sons, be soft.'

What is Hrih?

Hrih is the soft quiet inside me. When I do something good, I don't SHOUT about it — I hold it like a little diya inside my chest, gently, so it keeps shining. When I make a mistake, I feel gentle-sorry — not hiding, not lying — just 'oh, I did that, I am sorry.' Hrih is the hush-hush voice, the little bow of the head, the pink cheeks when Amma praises me. Krishna's name for it is hrīḥ, and He loves this quality in a child.

What it is NOT

Hrih is not hiding when I have done wrong — that is a different kind of hiding, the scared-hiding. Hrih is not being so quiet that no one can see my good work at all — we can still do good, we just don't need to BRAG. And hrih is NOT feeling bad about who I am — Krishna made me lovely. Hrih is only the soft-quiet-pink-cheek feeling about MY OWN goodness and MY OWN mistakes.

  • Running around the class shouting 'I got 10 on 10! I got 10 on 10!'
  • Pretending I did not spill the milk when Amma is standing right there
  • Saying 'I am the BEST dancer in the whole class' very loudly at lunch
  • Making fun of a friend who made a mistake, instead of feeling gentle for them

Sanskrit Word Study

hrīḥ (ह्रीः)

Pronunciation: hree

Meaning: The soft quiet inside. The little diya in the chest. The pink-cheek feeling when I do something good. The gentle-sorry feeling when I make a mistake. A sweet quality Krishna lists among the godly heart's treasures.

Example: When Arjuna bowed to Drona instead of shouting, that was hrīḥ.

Story from the Scriptures

Young Arjuna Bows Before Dronacharya — Adi Parva of the Mahabharata (Vyasa)

Long ago, in the city of Hastinapura, there was a royal school. A wise teacher named Dronacharya taught the princes how to shoot arrows. There were a hundred Kaurava princes and five Pandava princes. They all came to practise every morning.

One of the Pandava princes was a little boy named Arjuna. He was only eight years old. He was small. He had dark hair and bright clever eyes. And he LOVED archery. He loved the way the bow felt in his hand. He loved the whispering sound the arrow made when it flew.

The first day, Drona set up a small clay bird on a pole. 'Children,' he said, 'aim for the bird. Shoot!'

The bigger princes shot first. Some arrows went WIDE. Some arrows went LOW. Some arrows went CRAZY.

Then Arjuna stepped up. He was so small his bow was almost as tall as he was. He took a deep breath. He pulled back the string. And — WHOOSH — his arrow flew STRAIGHT into the little clay bird's eye.

The other princes gasped. 'WAH! LOOK AT ARJUNA!'

But Arjuna — did he jump? Did he shout? Did he say 'I AM THE BEST'?

No. His cheeks went PINK. He covered his mouth with his hand. He walked quietly to Dronacharya, put his bow at his teacher's feet, and bowed his head.

'Guruji,' he whispered, 'the arrow flew because YOU taught me.'

Some of the bigger boys laughed. 'Arjuna, why so shy? Show off! You are GOOD!'

One loud prince said, 'If I shot like that I would shout from the top of the palace!'

Arjuna just smiled a small smile. His heart was full of a warm pink feeling. It was not shouting. It was a little diya glowing softly inside his chest.

The next day, Arjuna hit the target again. And again he bowed. And again his cheeks went pink.

Day after day, week after week, Arjuna was the quietest prince and also the best one. When he made a mistake — once his arrow flew CROOKED and knocked down a flower — he ran to the gardener, put his hands together, and said 'I am gentle-sorry. I will water your flowers tomorrow.' He was not scared-hiding. He was gentle-sorry.

Dronacharya watched this little boy very carefully. One evening, when the sun was setting, Drona called Arjuna aside.

'Arjuna, child. Do you know why I love you so much?'

Arjuna's cheeks went pink again. 'No, Guruji.'

'Because you have hrīḥ, my son. The soft quiet inside. You never brag. You always bow. When you do wrong, you say gentle-sorry. When you do right, you hide your diya behind your hand. This is the most beautiful thing a student can have. Pride breaks the arrow. Hrīḥ makes it fly.'

Then Drona taught Arjuna the secret arrows — the ones he taught nobody else. He could teach them to Arjuna because Arjuna's heart was SOFT enough to hold them.

Years later, Arjuna would stand with Krishna on the great battlefield. He would hear the Gita. He would fight the biggest war. But all of his great arrows began that very first day — with pink cheeks, a soft bow, and a little diya glowing quietly inside a small boy's chest.

That little diya has a name. Hrīḥ.

A Story from Today

Priya Wins the Drawing Prize

Priya was in Standard Two. She loved to draw. Every evening she sat in the corner of her grandmother's kitchen with a small box of crayons and drew — flowers, birds, little cows, and her favourite: Krishna standing under a tree playing His flute.

One week, teacher Meena-ji announced, 'Children, our school is having an art show. Each of you may bring ONE drawing. The judges will pick the best three.'

Priya's heart went thump-thump. She went home and drew very, very carefully. She drew Krishna. Blue Krishna. A yellow dhoti. Peacock feather. A little tree. A little calf at His feet. She used every crayon in her box.

Ajji came behind her. 'Priya-kanna, this is beautiful.'

Priya's cheeks went pink. 'Ajji, I only drew what was in my heart.'

She brought her drawing to school the next day.

On the day of the art show, all the drawings were hung on the wall. Parents came. Teachers came. Judges walked slowly, looking at every picture. Priya stood quietly at the back.

Then the head judge clapped her hands. 'Children! The first prize goes to — Priya of Standard Two, for her drawing of Krishna!'

The room went 'OOOH!'

Priya's friend Neha was sitting next to her. Neha JUMPED up. Neha shouted, 'PRIYA WON! PRIYA WON! SHE IS THE BEST ARTIST IN THE WHOLE SCHOOL!'

Priya's cheeks went BRIGHT pink. Her ears went hot. Inside her chest, a little diya was glowing — soft and warm — but all around her, Neha was shouting. Some other children began clapping very loudly. A boy from Standard Three shouted 'Priya! Do a victory dance!'

Priya did not know what to do. Then she remembered her grandmother's words: 'Priya-kanna, your diya is yours. No one can light it and no one can blow it out. Hold it gently.'

Priya walked up slowly. She did not run. She did not dance. She bowed her head before the teacher. She put her hands together — namaste.

'Thank you, ma'am,' she said softly. 'Thank you, judges.'

She took the little blue ribbon. She did not lift it above her head. She held it close to her chest, like a little baby bird.

Then she walked back to her seat.

Neha was confused. 'Priya, why didn't you SHOUT? You won!'

Priya smiled a small smile. 'My heart is shouting inside, Neha. But softly. That is enough.'

At home that evening, Priya gave the blue ribbon to Ajji. 'Ajji, you keep it. In your puja cupboard. Behind Krishna.'

Ajji's old eyes went shiny. 'My child, why behind Krishna?'

'Because,' Priya whispered, 'Krishna drew through my hand. Not me. The ribbon is really His.'

That night, Priya sat by her bed and whispered, 'Krishna, thank You. My cheeks are still pink, but my diya is shining. I did not shout. I held it gently. Is that hrih, Krishna?'

In the quiet darkness, she felt Krishna smile.

That little diya has a name. Hrih.

Core Teaching

  1. Hrih is the soft quiet inside me. When I do something good, I do not SHOUT it from the rooftop. I hold it gently, like a little diya inside my chest. My cheeks go pink. My head gives a small bow. I can still be happy — but happy and gentle, not happy and loud. Krishna calls this hrīḥ, and He places it right in the middle of His list of godly qualities.
  2. Hrih is the gentle-sorry feeling when I make a mistake. I do not hide. I do not lie. I do not blame others. I just say 'I did that, and I am sorry.' My heart feels a little warm and pink — and that pink feeling is good. It is not scared-hiding. It is not shame that makes me small. It is a soft knowing — 'oh, I was not my best, let me be better tomorrow.' The pink is Krishna's gentle hand on my heart.
  3. Hrih is NOT feeling bad about who I am. Krishna made me lovely. My family loves me. My smile is beautiful. I can be strong and brave and clever — AND still have hrih. Hrih only means I hold my goodness gently and I hold my mistakes gently. Proud children break their arrows. Hrih-children fly straight and true — just like little Arjuna in Drona's ashram.
