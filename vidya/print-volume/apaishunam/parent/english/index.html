About This Book

This book is part of the Vidyā Karaṇa (विद्या करण) Value Education Series, designed for the Primary (Ages 6-9) age group.

Each chapter explores one of the values from the daivī sampad (divine qualities) described in the Bhagavad Gītā verses 16.1–3. The values are presented in a carefully sequenced order, where foundational values are taught before those that build upon them.

This Parent Book helps you reinforce at home what your child is learning at school. You do not need to read the full stories — your child will tell you! Instead, this book gives you conversation starters, signs to look for, and simple practices to do together as a family.

For each value, you will find:

  • Value at a Glance — What the value means, in plain language
  • The Verse — The scriptural foundation, in your language
  • Stories Your Child Heard — Brief summaries so you can ask about them
  • The Sanskrit Word — Ask your child to teach it to you!
  • Home Practice — The same action your child was given at school
  • Dinner Table Questions — 3–4 conversation starters for family discussion
  • Signs of Growth — Observable behaviours showing your child is internalizing the value

The most powerful teaching happens not in the classroom but at the dinner table.

Apaishunam

Apaishunam means NOT finding fault in others and NOT whispering mean things about them behind their backs. When I see someone's mistake, I do not run to tell the whole class. I do not make a circle with my friends and whisper 'did you SEE what she did?' I close my mouth kindly. I see the good FIRST. If something is really important, I go straight to a grown-up I trust — I do not gossip. Krishna loves the child whose mouth is a safe place for other people's little mistakes.

The Verse

Bhagavad-gita 16.2: Krishna tells Arjuna the shining marks of a divine heart. One of them is apaiśunam — NO fault-finding. When I see someone's mistake, I do not run to tell everyone. I do not whisper behind their back. I see the good first. My mouth keeps the little faults of others safe, like a closed hand keeps a small bird safe.

What Apaishunam is NOT

Apaishunam is NOT hiding a really big bad thing that hurts someone. If a friend is being bullied, or something scary is happening, we DO tell a grown-up we trust — straight to the grown-up, not in whispers. Apaishunam is also NOT pretending to like someone's mistake — we can quietly help them fix it, or just leave it alone. Apaishunam only means we do not make other people's mistakes into a FUN STORY to share with our friends. A mistake is not a snack to pass around.

  • Running to my friends at lunch and whispering 'did you SEE how Priya spilled her water bottle — SO silly!'
  • Making a face and rolling my eyes every time a certain boy speaks in class, so other children notice and laugh
  • Telling Ajji that my cousin Meera was 'really naughty' today — when Meera's small mistake was not even my business
  • Saying in the school bus, 'Ravi is the WORST at football — everyone knows!' — when Ravi is not there to hear

Stories Your Child Heard

Vidura Keeps His Mouth a Safe Place — Long ago, in the grand palace at Hastinapura, lived a wise uncle named Vidura — the king's younger brother. He lived simply: a small house, a soft voice. And he had one rule. Vidura's mouth was a SAFE PLACE for everybody's little mistakes. The palace was a WHISPERY place. Prince Duryodhana and his brothers whispered mean things about their cousins the Pandavas all day. 'Yudhishthira is slow!' 'Bhima is just a big belly!' They made it a game.

Meera Does Not Join the Whisper-Circle — Meera was in Standard Three. She had four friends — Priya, Sara, Naina, and Kavya. One Tuesday at lunch, Arjun — a quiet boy from class — carried his tiffin with an untied shoelace. He TRIPPED. His dal went SPLASH. His face went TOMATO RED. Arjun sat alone with one small roti.

The Sanskrit Word

apaiśunam (अपैशुनम्) — a-pai-SHU-nam

Meaning: NO fault-finding. NO whispering mean things about others behind their back. NO gossip. The a- at the start means 'no', and paiśuna means 'tale-bearing, slander, the carrying of mean stories from mouth to mouth'. A safe mouth — a mouth that does not spread other people's little wrongs.

Ask your child to teach you this word!

Home Practice

For one week, keep a Safe-Mouth Book beside your bed. Each night before sleep, with Amma or Appa, think back over the day and share three things: (1) ONE time today your mouth was SAFE — you did NOT whisper a mean thing about someone, even when it was tempting. (2) ONE GOOD thing you noticed about someone today — a classmate, a cousin, a sibling, anyone (no mean buts). (3) If any mean thing slipped out, draw a small heart with a tiny crack and write 'tomorrow I try again.' No shame — just a fresh start. Before sleeping, cup your hand softly at your heart (like you are holding a little sparrow safe inside) and whisper: 'Krishna, my mouth is safe tonight. The little bird inside is safe. Thank You.'

Duration: Every night for one week
Look for: By the end of the week, the child can describe at least 3 specific moments when they closed their mouth against a whisper-chance, and can name at least 5 good things about at least 5 different classmates or family members. They can demonstrate the 'soft-fist-at-heart' gesture while saying 'apaishunam' softly. The Safe-Mouth Book has at least 5 entries and any 'cracks' have been followed by a successful 'safe mouth' the next day.

When the safe mouth of apaishunam grows into seeing and speaking the straight-simple truth without any bending or hiding, that bright open quality has its own beautiful name: arjava (straightforward honesty).
