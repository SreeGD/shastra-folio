About This Book

This book is part of the Vidyā Karaṇa (विद्या करण) Value Education Series, designed for the Primary (Ages 6-9) age group.

Each chapter explores one of the values from the daivī sampad (divine qualities) described in the Bhagavad Gītā verses 16.1–3. The values are presented in a carefully sequenced order, where foundational values are taught before those that build upon them.

This Parent Book helps you reinforce at home what your child is learning at school. You do not need to read the full stories — your child will tell you! Instead, this book gives you conversation starters, signs to look for, and simple practices to do together as a family.

For each value, you will find:

  • Value at a Glance — What the value means, in plain language
  • The Verse — The scriptural foundation, in your language
  • Stories Your Child Heard — Brief summaries so you can ask about them
  • The Sanskrit Word — Ask your child to teach it to you!
  • Home Practice — The same action your child was given at school
  • Dinner Table Questions — 3–4 conversation starters for family discussion
  • Signs of Growth — Observable behaviours showing your child is internalizing the value

The most powerful teaching happens not in the classroom but at the dinner table.

Adambhitva

Adambhitva means NO showing off. When I do something good, I don't need to make a big big show. When I know something, I don't brag to prove I am better than my friends. When Amma praises me, I say 'thank you' and keep playing — I do not run around telling everyone. Adambhitva is the simple child inside me — the one who just DOES good things without needing claps and whistles. Krishna puts this word near the very top of His list in Gita 13.8, because He loves simple unshowy hearts.

The Verse

Bhagavad-gita 13.8: Krishna teaches Arjuna a list of things that make a heart truly wise. Very near the beginning He says: adambhitvam — do NOT show off. When you do a good thing, don't make a big fuss. When you know something, don't brag to prove you are better. Keep your goodness simple and quiet — like a flower that just blooms, without announcing it.

What Adambhitva is NOT

Adambhitva is NOT being sneaky or hiding what I did. I can still tell Amma honestly what I did. I can still show my drawing to teacher when she asks. I can still answer in class when I know the answer. Adambhitva only means I do not DRESS UP my goodness to look bigger than it is. I do not stand on a chair to say 'LOOK AT ME'. I do not keep saying 'I already know that!' to prove I am cleverer than my friends. Simple does NOT mean small — it means just real-size, no puffing up.

  • Standing on the bench at lunch yelling 'I got full marks! Who else got full marks? NO ONE!'
  • When teacher asks a question, waving my hand and shouting 'I know! I know! I KNEW it first!' even when others were going to answer
  • Wearing my best shiny clothes to school every day just so everyone says 'WOW'
  • Doing seva at the temple — but only when Appa is watching, so he will say how good I am

Stories Your Child Heard

Hanuman Keeps His Big Powers Secret — Long ago in Kishkindha forest lived a band of monkeys. Their minister was a small-looking monkey named Hanuman. Hanuman had a BIG secret. As a baby, he had jumped UP to bite the sun (he thought it was a mango!). His father Vayu, the wind-god, had blessed him with super-strength. This little monkey could jump over MOUNTAINS, lift ELEPHANTS, run faster than wind.

Kavya Hides Her Medal Behind the Ganesha — Kavya was in Standard Three. She loved running. Every morning before anyone woke up, she ran around the park three times. She did not tell her friends. She just ran. One Saturday, her school sent her to an inter-school race. Kavya touched her heart at the start line. 'Krishna, I am just going to run. You run with me.'

The Sanskrit Word

adambhitva (अदम्भित्व) — a-DAMBH-it-va

Meaning: NO show-off. No bragging to prove I am better. Doing good things simply — real-size, not puffed up. Keeping my powers folded, like Hanuman. The a- at the front means 'no', and dambha means 'big show' or 'puffing up'.

Ask your child to teach you this word!

Home Practice

For one week, keep an Invisible Good-Things Jar at home. Each day, do ONE small good thing that NO ONE notices — tidy a sibling's shoes without telling, water the tulasi plant quietly, pick up a dropped pencil, say a soft 'Ram Ram' when no one is listening, put your own plate in the sink without being asked. After each invisible good thing, drop ONE small dried chickpea (or a folded bit of paper) into your jar. Do NOT tell friends about the jar. Do NOT post it. Do NOT compete with siblings. Before bed, touch the jar gently and whisper: 'Krishna, You saw. That is enough.'

Duration: One invisible good thing per day for one week
Look for: By the end of the week, the child has at least 5 chickpeas in the jar and can describe at least 3 specific invisible good deeds WITHOUT bragging about them. The child can demonstrate the 'folded flag' gesture (both hands folded together at chest) while saying 'adambhitva' softly. They can explain in their own words why Hanuman did not show his powers until Ram called.

Signs of Growth

By the end of the week, the child has at least 5 chickpeas in the jar and can describe at least 3 specific invisible good deeds WITHOUT bragging about them. The child can demonstrate the 'folded flag' gesture (both hands folded together at chest) while saying 'adambhitva' softly. They can explain in their own words why Hanuman did not show his powers until Ram called.

When the no-show-off heart of adambhitva grows up, it becomes a quiet inner refusal to feel big and important about oneself — and that older, wiser quality has its own beautiful name: amānitva.
